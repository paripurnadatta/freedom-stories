In the autumn of 1930, Lahore jail held an older prisoner called Randhir Singh. He was a deeply religious Sikh, who had fought the British for years.

One thing about the young Bhagat Singh troubled him. Bhagat Singh did not believe in God.

So Randhir Singh tried to change his mind. He talked and talked. It did not work.

## A sharp remark

In the end, Randhir Singh lost patience. Bhagat Singh had become famous, he said. That fame had made him proud. His pride was standing like a black curtain between him and God.

That stung. Bhagat Singh decided to answer properly, in writing.

## Two days of writing

On 5 and 6 October 1930, he sat in his cell and wrote a long essay. He called it "Why I am an Atheist".

<aside class="note"><b>Atheist</b>: a person who does not believe in God.</aside>

He was waiting for the verdict in his trial. It came the very next day, on 7 October. He was sentenced to death.

## What did he say?

First, he answered the charge of pride. "It is not my vanity, my friends," he wrote. He pointed out that he had stopped believing in God years before, when he was an unknown young man. So fame could not be the reason.

<aside class="note"><b>Vanity</b>: being too proud of yourself.</aside>

Then he gave his reasons, one by one. Why, he asked, would an all-powerful God allow so much pain and unfairness in the world? He thought people should solve their problems by thinking hard and working together.

For him, he wrote, reason was the guiding star.

## Brave in a different way

It would have been easy to pray in his position. Many people find comfort in God when they are facing death. Bhagat Singh knew that. He admitted in the essay that believing can make hard times feel easier.

But he would not pretend. He wanted to face the end with only what he truly believed.

## Printed after his death

The essay was printed on 27 September 1931, in a Lahore weekly called The People. That day would have been his 24th birthday. He had been dead for six months.

Many of his friends disagreed with him. Many readers today do too, and that is all right. What makes the essay famous is how calmly and honestly a 23-year-old explained what he believed, even while he waited to die.
