On the morning of 8 April 1929, two young men sat in the visitors' gallery of the Central Legislative Assembly in Delhi. On the floor below, members were waiting for the President to announce the fate of the Public Safety Bill and the Trade Disputes Bill, two laws that would let the government deport political agitators without trial and choke off strikes. As the announcement came, Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt threw two bombs over the railing.

They had picked the spot. The bombs fell among empty benches, filled the chamber with smoke and noise, and slightly hurt a few members. Nobody died. The two men scattered leaflets, shouted "Inquilab Zindabad", and stood where they were. Both carried pistols. Neither tried to leave.

The leaflet opened with a line the French anarchist Auguste Vaillant had used at his own trial in 1894: it takes a loud voice to make the deaf hear. Bhagat Singh was twenty-one. He had worked out that a trial would carry his voice much further than an escape.

## A family already in trouble

He was born in late September 1907 in Banga, a village in the Lyallpur district of Punjab that is now in Pakistan. That spring his uncle Ajit Singh had led the peasant agitation against the Punjab Colonisation Bill, and in May the government deported him to Mandalay along with Lala Lajpat Rai. His father, Kishan Singh, and another uncle, Swaran Singh, were also in jail around the time of his birth.

The family kept two stories about the boy, and they have been retold so often that it is hard to separate them from legend. In the first, a very young Bhagat watched his elders sowing a field and said he would plant guns, so that guns would grow. In the second, he was eleven when he heard of the shooting at Jallianwala Bagh in April 1919, and he went to the garden in Amritsar and came home with a bottle of earth soaked in blood. Both come from family memory, not from records of the time.

What the records do show is a teenager who left his government-aided school during the Non-Cooperation Movement and enrolled at the National College in Lahore, set up by Lajpat Rai's circle for students who had walked out. When Gandhi called off the movement in February 1922 after the violence at Chauri Chaura, many of those students felt abandoned. Bhagat Singh started reading instead: Irish and Russian revolutionaries, Marx, the anarchists, histories of every rising he could find in Lahore's libraries.

## From reading to organizing

In 1926 he helped found the Naujawan Bharat Sabha in Lahore, a youth group that ran lectures and magic-lantern shows about earlier martyrs. In September 1928, at a secret meeting in the ruins of Feroz Shah Kotla in Delhi, he and his comrades renamed the Hindustan Republican Association. It became the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association. The new word mattered to him. He wanted the British gone, and he also wanted to know who would own the land and the factories once they were.

His closest companions in the group were Sukhdev Thapar, Shivaram Rajguru and its commander, Chandrashekhar Azad.

## Lahore, December 1928

On 30 October 1928, Lala Lajpat Rai led a silent march in Lahore against the all-white Simon Commission. Police under Superintendent James Scott broke it up with lathis, and Lajpat Rai was struck repeatedly. He died on 17 November.

The HSRA decided to kill Scott. On 17 December, outside the police office in Lahore, they shot the wrong man: Assistant Superintendent John Saunders, a 21-year-old officer who had not given the order. Rajguru fired first and Bhagat Singh followed. As they ran, a head constable named Chanan Singh chased them, and Azad shot him dead.

Bhagat Singh cut his hair, shaved, put on a hat and a Western suit, and boarded a train out of Lahore. Durga Devi Vohra, the wife of a fellow revolutionary and remembered as Durga Bhabhi, travelled with him as his wife, her small son on her lap, while Rajguru played their servant. They reached Calcutta without being stopped.

## The courtroom as a stage

After the Assembly bombs, he could have used that disguise again. Instead he walked into custody, and in June 1929 a Delhi court sentenced him and Dutt to transportation for life. Their statement to the court was printed across the country. In it they said that revolution did not have to mean bloodshed, and that their aim was a change in the whole order of society.

By then the police had found the group's bomb workshops in Lahore and Saharanpur and arrested Sukhdev, Rajguru and others. The evidence tied Bhagat Singh to the Saunders killing. A second case, the Lahore Conspiracy Case, began with him as its central accused.

## A hundred days without food

In June 1929, held in Mianwali jail, he refused food. He and Dutt demanded what they believed political prisoners were owed: books, newspapers, decent food and an end to forced labour. Other prisoners in the case joined them. Jawaharlal Nehru put out a public statement backing the strike in July, and visited the hunger strikers in Lahore jail the next month.

Jatin Das, a young Bengali in the same case, kept going longest. He died on 13 September 1929, on the sixty-third day of his fast. His body was taken by train from Lahore to Calcutta, and crowds met it at the stations along the way. Bhagat Singh's own strike lasted well over a hundred days before he ended it in October.

## A trial without the accused

The prisoners turned the Lahore hearings into political theatre: slogans, songs and long speeches. On 1 May 1930 the Viceroy, Lord Irwin, issued an ordinance that handed the case to a special tribunal. It could proceed without the accused in the room, and the only appeal left was to the Privy Council in London.

On 7 October 1930 the tribunal sentenced Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru to hang. The Privy Council refused the appeal early in 1931.

## Writing in a cell

In jail he filled a notebook with copied passages and notes from more than a hundred authors. When his father petitioned the tribunal for mercy, he wrote to him in fury, saying it felt like a stab in the back. In October 1930, after a fellow prisoner suggested his atheism was vanity, he wrote "Why I Am an Atheist", a calm and closely argued essay that was published in Lahore after his death. In his last weeks he and his two comrades wrote to the Governor of Punjab asking to be treated as prisoners of war and shot by a firing squad instead of hanged.

## 23 March 1931

On 5 March 1931, Gandhi and Irwin signed their pact. Gandhi raised the death sentences with the Viceroy, but he did not make them a condition of the agreement.

The executions were expected on 24 March. They were carried out on the evening of the 23rd, inside Lahore Central Jail. Afterwards the bodies were taken out through the back of the prison and burned in secret on the bank of the Sutlej near Hussainiwala. Days later, when Gandhi arrived for the Congress session at Karachi, young men met him with black flags.

Bhagat Singh was twenty-three. Batukeshwar Dutt, who had stood beside him in the Assembly gallery, lived until 1965. He asked to be cremated at Hussainiwala, next to his friends, and he was.
