It is the morning of 8 April 1929. We are in Delhi, inside a grand round building called the Council House. In one of its halls sits the Central Legislative Assembly, where the British rulers of India make laws.

Up in the visitors' gallery, two young men are sitting very still. One of them wears a neat felt hat. His name is Bhagat Singh. He is twenty-one years old. Beside him sits his friend, Batukeshwar Dutt.

Down below, the members are waiting for news about two new laws. One law will let the government throw people out of India without a trial. The other will make it harder for workers to go on strike.

Bhagat Singh looks down at the floor. He has already picked his spot. It is a place where the benches are empty.

Then he stands up and throws a bomb.

## Boom, smoke, and a shower of paper

There is a huge bang. The hall fills with smoke. Dutt throws a second bomb. People scream and run for the doors. A few members get small cuts and bruises, but nobody is killed. The bombs were made to be loud, not deadly.

Then something strange happens. The two young men do not run away. They throw leaflets into the air and shout "Inquilab Zindabad!" It means "Long live the revolution!"

Both of them have pistols in their pockets. They could try to escape. Instead, they stand there and wait for the police.

Why would anyone do that? To find the answer, we have to go back to a small village in Punjab.

## A boy born into trouble

Bhagat Singh was born in September 1907 in Banga. It is a village in Punjab that is now in Pakistan.

His family was already in trouble with the British. That same year, his uncle Ajit Singh had led farmers in a protest against an unfair land law. The British sent him far away to Mandalay, in Burma, as a punishment. His father and another uncle were in jail too.

His family loved to tell stories about him. In one story, little Bhagat watched grown-ups planting seeds in a field. He said he would plant guns instead, so that guns would grow. In another story, he was eleven when British soldiers shot at a peaceful crowd in Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar, in April 1919. The family says he went to the garden and brought home a little bottle of soil stained with blood.

Did these things really happen? Nobody can check. They come from the family's memories, not from old records. But they tell us how his family saw him: as a boy who could not stand by and watch.

## The boy who read everything

When Bhagat Singh was a teenager, Gandhi asked Indians to stop working with the British. Bhagat Singh left his school and joined the National College in Lahore. It was started by Indian leaders for students like him.

Then, in 1922, Gandhi suddenly called the movement off, after some protesters killed policemen at a place called Chauri Chaura. Many young students felt let down. What should they do now?

Bhagat Singh did something you might not expect from a revolutionary. He read. He read about freedom fighters in Ireland and Russia. He read about workers and farmers, and who should own the land.

In 1926 he helped start a youth group in Lahore called the Naujawan Bharat Sabha. In 1928 he and his friends formed a secret group with a long name: the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association. His closest friends in it were Sukhdev, Rajguru and their leader, Chandrashekhar Azad.

## The day Lala Lajpat Rai was hurt

In October 1928, a group of British officials called the Simon Commission came to Lahore. Its job was to decide India's future, but it did not have a single Indian member. People were angry.

A respected old leader, Lala Lajpat Rai, led a silent march against it. The police charged at the marchers with long sticks called lathis. Lajpat Rai was hit again and again. A few weeks later, on 17 November, he died.

Bhagat Singh and his friends were furious. They decided to kill the police officer they blamed, James Scott.

## A terrible mistake

On 17 December 1928, they waited outside the police office in Lahore. But they shot the wrong man. He was John Saunders, a young officer who had not given the order. As they ran, an Indian head constable named Chanan Singh chased them, and Azad shot him too.

The police were now hunting everywhere for Bhagat Singh. So he changed how he looked. He cut his hair, shaved his beard, and put on a hat and a smart Western suit. Then he walked straight onto a train.

Sitting next to him was Durga Devi, the wife of a fellow revolutionary. People called her Durga Bhabhi. She pretended to be his wife, holding her little son on her lap. Rajguru pretended to be their servant. Nobody guessed. They reached Calcutta safely.

So here is the puzzle. A man who could escape like that went to the Assembly a few months later, threw his bombs, and then just stood still.

## The loudest voice in India

The answer was printed on those leaflets. They began with a line borrowed from a French rebel: it takes a loud voice to make the deaf hear.

Bhagat Singh believed the British were not listening to Indians. Running away would make him a hunted man. But a trial would make him famous. Every word he said in court would be printed in newspapers across the country. The courtroom would become his loudspeaker.

He was right. In court, he and Dutt explained that revolution did not have to mean killing. They said it meant changing the whole of society, so that farmers and workers were treated fairly. In June 1929 they were sentenced to prison for life. By then, the police had also linked Bhagat Singh to the Saunders shooting. A second, bigger trial began, and this one could end in death.

## A hundred days without food

In jail, Bhagat Singh noticed that political prisoners were treated like thieves. They got bad food, no books and no newspapers. So in June 1929 he stopped eating. Dutt joined him, and so did others.

Weeks went by. They grew thin and weak. Jawaharlal Nehru spoke up for them and came to visit them in jail. One of the prisoners, a young man from Bengal named Jatin Das, kept fasting until he died on 13 September 1929, after sixty-three days. When his body was taken by train to Calcutta, huge crowds waited at every station to say goodbye.

Bhagat Singh kept going for more than a hundred days before he stopped.

## A trial without him in the room

At the second trial, the prisoners sang songs and shouted slogans. The judges could not keep order. So in May 1930, the Viceroy made a special rule. A special court could now carry on even when the prisoners were not in the room.

On 7 October 1930, that court gave its decision. Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru were to be hanged.

## The prisoner with a pen

You might think a man waiting to die would give up. Bhagat Singh kept writing. He filled a notebook with notes from more than a hundred writers. When his father begged the court to spare his son's life, Bhagat Singh wrote him an angry letter. He did not want to ask the British for mercy.

He also wrote an essay called "Why I Am an Atheist". It explained why he did not believe in God, and he wrote it calmly and carefully, like a teacher. Near the end, he and his two friends wrote to the Governor of Punjab with one last request. They asked to be shot like soldiers, not hanged like criminals.

## The last evening

In March 1931, Gandhi and the Viceroy, Lord Irwin, made an agreement called the Gandhi-Irwin Pact. Gandhi asked the Viceroy to save the three young men, but the pact did not include them.

Everyone thought the hanging would happen on 24 March. The British did it one day early, on the evening of 23 March 1931, inside Lahore Central Jail. Then, in the dark, they took the bodies out through the back of the jail and burned them in secret beside the Sutlej river, near a place called Hussainiwala.

Bhagat Singh was twenty-three years old.

His friend Batukeshwar Dutt, the other young man in the gallery that morning, lived until 1965. Before he died, he asked for one thing. He wanted to be cremated at Hussainiwala, beside his friends. And he was.
