It is the morning of 23 March 1931, inside Lahore Central Jail. Three young men sit in three small cells. Their names are Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru.

They know they are going to be hanged. The British rulers have said it will happen tomorrow, on 24 March. So today their families are coming to say goodbye.

But nothing about this day will go as planned.

## The meeting that never happened

The families arrive at the jail gate. They have waited a long time for this last meeting.

The jail has a rule. Only close blood relatives may go inside. Sukhdev's uncle, Lala Achint Ram, is turned away, because he is only an uncle.

<aside class="note"><b>Blood relative</b>: a parent, brother or sister. Someone born into the same family.</aside>

The families talk it over. If they cannot all go in, then none of them will. In protest, they go away without seeing the three young men at all.

People who wrote about this day later do not agree on every detail of that morning. One says only the parents were allowed in. Another says it was the uncle who was refused. But they all agree on one thing. There was no last meeting.

## Lunch from Bogha

There is a man called Bogha who works in the jail. He is a Dalit. In those days, many people would not eat food that a Dalit had touched.

<aside class="note"><b>Dalit</b>: people who were treated as "untouchable" under the unfair caste system, and kept apart from others.</aside>

Bhagat Singh asks Bogha to cook lunch for the three of them. Bogha is shocked. He says no. But Bhagat Singh will not give up. He tells Bogha that he has looked after them like a mother. So the three friends eat Bogha's food.

Years before, Bhagat Singh had written an essay against untouchability. For him, a free India had to be fair to everyone.

## A secret from the barber

At about two o'clock in the afternoon, the jail barber, Barkat, comes past the cells. He carries a secret. The hanging will not be tomorrow. It will be today, in a few hours.

The British have changed the plan. They are afraid that huge crowds will gather outside the jail on the 24th. So they have decided to do it a day early, quietly, in the evening.

Bhagat Singh asks Barkat to carry two small gifts to friends in another part of the jail. One is his black fountain pen. The other is a comb with his name carved on it.

One of those friends was a journalist called Virendra, who was also a prisoner. He kept the comb. Later, he wrote down everything he saw and heard that day. Much of this story comes from him.

## A few more pages

At four o'clock, the head warder, Chattar Singh, comes to the cells. He tells them to bathe and get ready.

<aside class="note"><b>Warder</b>: a guard in a jail.</aside>

Bhagat Singh's lawyer had brought him a book about Lenin, the leader of the Russian revolution. Bhagat Singh goes back to it. When the guards come to take him, he asks them to wait. He has a few pages left.

The story goes that he said one revolutionary was meeting another. When he reached the end, he closed the book and said, "Chalo." Let's go.

Did it really happen that way? Some historians are not sure. The story was told by people who loved him, and it may have grown in the telling. But we know for certain that he read hungrily, all through his years in jail.

## Arm in arm

The three friends come out of their cells. They hug each other. Then they link arms, with Bhagat Singh in the middle, and walk towards the gallows.

<aside class="note"><b>Gallows</b>: the wooden frame used for hanging people.</aside>

They are singing as they walk. Then they shout "Inquilab Zindabad!" Long live the revolution!

Other prisoners hear them. They shout it back. Soon the slogan is ringing from cell to cell through the whole jail. Even prisoners who were there for ordinary crimes join in.

## A little after seven

They reach the gallows a little after seven in the evening. Bhagat Singh refuses to have his face covered.

He speaks to the British officials standing nearby. He tells them that Indian revolutionaries are not afraid to die for what they believe.

Then it is over. Bhagat Singh was twenty-three years old.

In his last weeks, he had written to the Governor of Punjab. Since the court said he had fought a war against the King, he asked to be shot like a soldier, not hanged like a criminal. They were hanged anyway. In that letter he wrote one line that is still quoted today: "The war neither began with us nor is it going to end with our lives."

## Out through the back

The jail officials still fear the crowds. So the bodies are not carried out through the front gate. They are taken out through the back of the jail, in the dark.

They are driven to the bank of the Sutlej river, near a village called Hussainiwala. There they are burned in secret, in a hurry, with kerosene.

<aside class="note"><b>Cremate</b>: to burn a body after death. Hindus and Sikhs say goodbye to their dead this way.</aside>

But people find out. They hurry to the river and gather what is left. Among them, Virendra remembered, were Bhagat Singh's sister, and the daughter of Lala Lajpat Rai. His death, more than two years earlier, was where this whole story had begun.

The next evening, a huge procession walked through Lahore behind the remains. Some say a hundred thousand people came.

## What the British got wrong

The British had hanged three young men a day early, in secret, to avoid trouble. It did not work. When the news spread, there were protests and mourning all over India.

They had wanted to keep things quiet. Instead, the story of their last day was told again and again.

Today, every year on 23 March, people gather for a fair called the Shaheedi Mela at Hussainiwala, where the three friends were cremated. They come to remember three young men who walked to the gallows arm in arm, singing.
