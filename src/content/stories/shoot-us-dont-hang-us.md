By March 1931, Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru knew they had very little time left. The court had sentenced all three to hang.

People all over India were asking the British to spare them. There were protests, meetings and appeals for mercy.

The three friends wrote a letter too. But it was not what anyone expected.

## A letter to the Governor

The letter went to the Governor of Punjab, the top British official in the province. Bhagat Singh wrote it for all three of them.

<aside class="note"><b>Governor</b>: the British official in charge of a whole province, like Punjab.</aside>

They did not say they were sorry. They did not ask to be set free. They did not even ask to live.

Instead, they made an argument.

## "Waging war"

When the court had sentenced them, it said they were guilty of "waging war against the King". In other words, the court itself said they had been fighting a war.

Well then, the three friends wrote. If we were fighting a war, we are not criminals. We are prisoners of war.

<aside class="note"><b>Prisoner of war</b>: a soldier who is captured by the enemy during a war.</aside>

And prisoners of war are not hanged like criminals. So they made one request. "We claim to be shot dead instead of to be hanged."

They even asked the Governor to send soldiers from the army to do it.

## Why did it matter?

To us, this might sound strange. Either way, they would die. Why did the method matter so much?

For them, it was about what their deaths would mean. A criminal is hanged for a crime. A soldier is shot in a war. They wanted India, and Britain, to remember that they had been fighting for their country's freedom.

## The line people still quote

The letter also has a sentence that people still repeat today:

"The war neither began with us nor is it going to end with our lives."

They meant that they were only one small part of a much longer struggle. Others had fought before them, and others would carry on after they were gone.

## What happened?

The British did not agree. On 23 March 1931, the three friends were hanged inside Lahore jail.

On that same day, in the High Court in Lahore, a British government lawyer mentioned their letter. He admitted they had asked to be shot rather than hanged.

They had not won their request. But they had made sure everyone knew how they saw themselves: not as criminals, but as soldiers for India.
