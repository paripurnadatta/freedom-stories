In June 1929, Bhagat Singh was moved to Mianwali jail, far away in western Punjab. He had already been sentenced to prison for life for the bombs in the Assembly.

He looked around the jail and noticed something unfair.

British and other European prisoners got good food. They had books, newspapers and clean clothes. Indian prisoners, even those jailed for fighting for their country, got poor food and were made to do hard labour, like thieves.

## The empty plate

On the morning of 15 June 1929, Bhagat Singh refused his food. His friend Batukeshwar Dutt joined him.

<aside class="note"><b>Hunger strike</b>: refusing to eat, so that the people in charge are forced to listen.</aside>

Soon both were in Lahore. On 24 June they wrote down exactly what they wanted:

- food as good as the European prisoners got
- no forced hard labour
- books and paper to write on
- at least one daily newspaper
- better clothes, and soap and other things to keep clean

They were not asking to be let out. They were asking to be treated as political prisoners, with dignity.

## Food in the cell

The jail officials tried to break them. They left tasty food in the cells, hoping the hungry men would give in. They tried to force-feed them. The prisoners fought back.

Other prisoners joined the strike. People across India began to follow the news. In July, Jawaharlal Nehru spoke up for the strikers. In August he came to see them in Lahore jail.

## Jatin Das

One of the strikers was Jatin Das, a young man from Bengal. He grew weaker and weaker, but he would not eat.

On 13 September 1929, after 63 days without food, Jatin Das died. His body was taken by train to Calcutta. At station after station, crowds came to say goodbye.

## How long?

Bhagat Singh grew so weak that he had to be carried into court on a stretcher. He lost many kilos.

He finally stopped in early October 1929, after more than a hundred days. His father had asked him to stop, and so had the Congress party.

Books do not agree on the exact number. Some say 116 days. The historian Chaman Lal, using Bhagat Singh's own letters, counts 112 days. Either way, it was one of the longest hunger strikes of its time.

The government promised better treatment. But more than that, the whole country now knew what was happening behind prison walls, and that was exactly what the strikers wanted.
