In October 1926, there was a loud bang at the Dussehra fair in Lahore. Someone had set off a bomb.

<aside class="note"><b>Dussehra</b>: a big autumn festival. Many towns hold a fair, with crowds, stalls and the burning of a giant Ravana.</aside>

Seven months later, in May 1927, the police arrested Bhagat Singh. He was nineteen years old.

## Why him?

The police said he had something to do with the bomb. Some historians say that was only an excuse. What really worried the police were his words.

He had been writing angry articles under a pen name, Vidrohi, meaning "rebel". One of them praised the young men of the Kakori case. They had robbed a train carrying government money, and the British were about to hang some of them.

<aside class="note"><b>Kakori</b>: a place near Lucknow where revolutionaries stopped a train in 1925 and took the government's money from it.</aside>

He was also running a youth group in Lahore, the Naujawan Bharat Sabha. It held meetings and talked to young people about freedom. To the police, he was becoming dangerous.

## Five weeks in custody

He was held for about five weeks. A photograph from around this time shows him in custody. His long hair is uncovered, with no turban. He looks calm.

It is one of only four real photographs of Bhagat Singh that are known today. Many of the pictures you see of him were painted later.

Then the court agreed to let him go "for good behaviour". That meant he had to stay out of trouble. And there was one more condition.

## The price of freedom

Somebody had to promise to pay sixty thousand rupees if he broke the rules. In 1927, that was an enormous amount of money.

<aside class="note"><b>Surety</b>: a promise to pay a sum of money if the person who is let go does not behave as the court orders.</aside>

The money was not paid straight away. It was a promise. But if Bhagat Singh got into trouble again, it would be lost. The British were hoping the threat would keep him quiet.

## Did it work?

For a while, he had to be careful. But he did not stop. He kept writing for Kirti, a magazine for farmers and workers. He kept building his youth group.

And just over a year later, he joined with friends from across North India to form a new secret group. The British would soon have far more to worry about than an article.

The police had tried to put a price on his freedom. It turned out to be far too low.
