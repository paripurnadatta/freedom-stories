You have probably seen this photograph. A young man looks straight at the camera. He has a thin moustache and a felt hat tipped to one side.

It is on posters, calendars, rickshaws and the backs of trucks. It may be the most famous picture of any Indian freedom fighter.

But it was taken for a very particular reason.

## A trip to the studio

On 3 April 1929, Bhagat Singh and his friend Batukeshwar Dutt walked into a photo studio near Kashmiri Gate in Delhi. It was run by a photographer called Ramnath.

<aside class="note"><b>Kashmiri Gate</b>: an old gateway in the walls of Delhi, with busy shops and streets around it.</aside>

One account says their comrade Jaidev Kapoor arranged the visit. Ramnath photographed each of them.

Five days later, the two young men threw bombs into the Central Legislative Assembly and let themselves be arrested.

## Why have a photograph taken?

They knew what was coming. After the bombs, every newspaper in India would want to print their faces. They wanted to choose the picture people saw.

So their friends collected the prints and the negatives from the studio. Then they handed them to the newspapers.

<aside class="note"><b>Negative</b>: in old cameras, the film that a photograph is printed from.</aside>

On 12 April, the photograph appeared in an Urdu newspaper in Lahore, Bande Matram. On 18 April it was in the Hindustan Times in Delhi. Soon it was everywhere.

## Whose hat?

Here is a small mystery. In Dutt's photographs from the same visit, he wears no hat. You can see this in a poster that seems to come from their hunger strike a few months later.

One writer says the felt hat belonged to the studio, which kept hats for customers to wear.

Bhagat Singh had worn a hat like it before. He wore one when he escaped from Lahore in disguise a few months earlier. So we cannot be sure whose hat this was.

## Only a handful of photographs

Only four genuine photographs of Bhagat Singh are known. The early ones show him in a white turban. One shows him in custody, with long open hair.

After he died, painters made many pictures of him. Many show him in a yellow or saffron turban. As far as anyone knows, he never wore a turban in those colours.

So when you see his face on a wall, ask yourself: is this the real photograph, or someone's idea of him? The real one was planned by a 21-year-old who knew exactly how he wanted to be remembered.
