The North-West Frontier Province had an elected government in 1947. Its chief minister, Dr Khan Sahib, belonged to the Khudai Khidmatgar, the Red Shirts. His brother Abdul Ghaffar Khan led the movement. They had won the elections. They were against the partition of India.

But the Viceroy, Lord Mountbatten, decided that the province must choose where it belonged.

## A question for one province

Mountbatten visited the Frontier on 28 and 29 April 1947. He announced that there would be a referendum. A referendum is a vote in which people answer a single question, instead of choosing between candidates. This was part of his plan of 3 June 1947 for the partition of India.

The Muslim League and the Congress both accepted the plan. The Khudai Khidmatgar did not, and neither did another group, the All India Azad Muslim Conference.

The question would have only two answers. The people of the Frontier could join India, or they could join Pakistan.

## The third choice

The Red Shirts did not want either. On 21 June, a number of leaders met at Bannu and passed a resolution. Among them were Abdul Ghaffar Khan and Mirzali Khan, who was known as the Faqir of Ipi. The Bannu Resolution asked for an independent Pashtunistan, a state for the Pashtun lands.

<aside class="note"><b>Pashtunistan</b>: the name used for a homeland of the Pashtun people, who live on both sides of what is now the border between Pakistan and Afghanistan.</aside>

The British refused. They offered only India or Pakistan. The resolution had named the Pashtun-majority lands as the place for the new state.

Dr Khan Sahib and Abdul Ghaffar Khan boycotted the referendum. To boycott is to stay away on purpose, as a protest. They said that the vote did not give the voters a choice of independence, nor the option of joining Afghanistan.

<aside class="note"><b>Who boycotted?</b> One writer says the Congress boycotted the vote. The page on the referendum says the Congress accepted the plan. The boycott was led by the Khudai Khidmatgar and by Dr Khan Sahib.</aside>

## Old friends, old quarrels

The Khudai Khidmatgar had a long history with the Congress. In 1929 the Muslim League turned the movement away. In August 1931 the Red Shirts joined the Congress. They stayed with it for sixteen years. They won the Frontier elections with the Congress in 1937 and again in 1946.

They had paid a high price for their cause. One writer says that between April and June 1930 alone, about 100 people were killed and more than 1,000 were arrested in the settled districts.

In the summer of 1947 the Hindu–Muslim killings spread through India. One writer says this halted the movement's social programme. The same writer says the Khidmatgars protected thousands of Hindus in their villages.

## How the vote was arranged

On 18 June, the army's headquarters wrote a letter naming eight officers to help the man in charge of the vote, the Referendum Commissioner. Seven were lieutenant-colonels and one was a major. Civilians worked at lower levels, with army officers watching them closely.

Mountbatten told the acting Governor that "each side should have equal facilities in the matter of the supply of petrol",.

There was a code of conduct. It banned election speeches by agreement. Each side was to put up its posters side by side. Political prisoners were freed, except those held for serious crimes.

## The numbers

Polling began on 6 July 1947. The results were published on 20 July. That was only fifteen days after the Bannu Resolution.

About four million people lived in the province. Only 572,798 were registered to vote, about one person in seven. Of those, 51 per cent voted. That leaves about 280,000 registered voters who did not cast a valid vote.

- For Pakistan: 289,244 votes, or 99.02 per cent.
- For India: 2,874 votes, or 0.98 per cent.

The total of valid votes was 292,118. Pakistan's count was more than a hundred times India's.

<aside class="note"><b>Per cent</b>: out of every hundred. 99 per cent means 99 out of every 100.</aside>

The turnout was lowest in the rural Pashtun districts that were Khudai Khidmatgar strongholds. In Mardan, 41.56 per cent voted. In Peshawar, 41.68 per cent did. Among people who were not Muslim, only 1.16 per cent voted.

One estimate on the page says that turnout was about 15 points lower than in the 1946 elections.

## What the boycott meant

A boycott makes a result hard to read. Pakistan won almost every vote that was cast. But the Red Shirts had asked their supporters to stay away. Nobody can say how the vote would have gone if they had taken part.

One writer says the referendum "eventually worked in favour of Pakistan". The Khudai Khidmatgar said the question was unfair. This story does not decide between the two sides.

## After the vote

The Frontier joined Pakistan on 15 August 1947. On 22 August, Jinnah dismissed Dr Khan Sahib's government. The next day, Abdul Qayyum Khan Kashmiri became chief minister.

In September 1947 the Khudai Khidmatgar passed the Sardaryab resolution. It accepted Pakistan and cut its ties with the Congress.

The Muslim League argued that the Red Shirts had not joined in the flag-hoisting on 15 August, so their loyalty was in doubt. Others dispute this. A letter from the Governor, Lockhart, argued against the dismissal.

Abdul Ghaffar Khan and Dr Khan Sahib went to prison. In May 1948 Abdul Ghaffar Khan was arrested under the Frontier Crimes Regulation. What happened next is told in the story of Babarra. You can read what Abdul Ghaffar Khan felt about the Congress's decision in "Thrown to the wolves". In mid-September 1948 the Khudai Khidmatgar were declared unlawful, and the movement's centre at Sardaryab, in Charsadda, was destroyed. The movement lived on in memory. On 20 January 2011 a man named Faisal Khan started a new Khudai Khidmatgar in Delhi.

## Why it matters

The Red Shirts had spent seventeen years working without violence. In the summer of 1947 their protest was a boycott. How history judges that is for each reader to decide.
