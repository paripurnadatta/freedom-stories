This is a sad story. It is told carefully, because the people who tell it do not agree on the numbers. What they do agree on is the date: 12 August 1948.

It happened in a place called Babarra, in Charsadda, near Abdul Ghaffar Khan's home village of Utmanzai.

## A year after the vote

The Frontier joined Pakistan on 15 August 1947. On 22 August its elected government, led by Dr Khan Sahib of the Khudai Khidmatgar, was dismissed. Abdul Qayyum Khan of the Muslim League became chief minister.

The Muslim League said that the Red Shirts had not joined in raising the flag on 15 August, and that their loyalty was in doubt. Others dispute this. The Governor, Lockhart, wrote a letter against the dismissal. In September 1947 the Red Shirts had accepted Pakistan, and one page says Abdul Ghaffar Khan pledged his loyalty to it in 1948.

In May 1948 Abdul Ghaffar Khan was arrested under the Frontier Crimes Regulation, a law that let the government jail people without a proper trial. The authorities said that he was plotting with the Faqir of Ipi, a tribal leader. The Friday Times calls that claim contested.

<aside class="note"><b>Ordinance</b>: a law made by the governor or ruler, without a vote in the assembly.</aside>

## The ordinance

Then the Governor, Sir Ambrose Dundas, issued an ordinance, a law made without the assembly. It was called the Public Safety Ordinance. It let the government hold people without telling them why, and seize their property. Detained people could not challenge the arrest in court.

One writer says the Governor told people that the ordinance was issued on the special orders of the Governor-General, Muhammad Ali Jinnah. The writer gives no document for this, and no other page repeats it.

<aside class="note"><b>Two dates</b>: one page says the ordinance was made in July 1948. Another says June. A newspaper, the Pakistan Times, criticised it on 11 July.</aside>

## The march

Many Khudai Khidmatgar leaders were in jail. The party's provincial council, the jirga, called for a protest on 12 August at Babarra. The procession was led by Salar Ameen Jan, while much of the leadership was in prison. Dr Sohail Khan says it was Salar Ameen Jan who decided that the march should go ahead.

The government cordoned off the area the day before. It also announced a curfew without warning. Thousands of Red Shirts were arrested before the day began. People gathered at Utmanzai and marched towards Babarra.

## The firing

Police fired on the marchers. One page says they fired from buildings overlooking the crowd. A memoir by a man who was there describes many women among the injured and the dead.

The accounts do not agree on why the police fired. The Chief Minister, Abdul Qayyum Khan, told the provincial assembly in September that he had imposed Section 144 at Babarra. Section 144 is a law that bans people from gathering in a place. He said that the firing followed when the crowd did not go home.

The Red Shirts say otherwise. Wali Khan, Abdul Ghaffar Khan's son, wrote in a book that a government officer told him he had neither been asked to impose Section 144 nor had done so.

One page says the police fired on the order of the Chief Minister. Another says it cannot name who gave the order.

## Voices from that day

Some of the people who were there have left words about it. One historian, Dr Sohail Khan, names Speen Malang as the first man to fall. A politician, Mian Iftikhar, says: "It was a rain of bullets on that day."

A man called Sultan Kaka of Prang was missing on 12 and 13 August. His son says that he later buried six bodies in one grave. These are memories told long afterwards, and they cannot be checked.

A man named Hayat Roghani says that nearby hospitals were told not to treat the injured, and that dozens died of it. The article that reports this gives no document to support the claim.

## How many died?

The numbers are far apart:

- The official count, as given on one page, was about 15 killed and about 40 hurt.
- Khudai Khidmatgar sources say about 150 killed and 400 hurt.
- Pakhtun nationalist groups claim 611 dead and 1,200 hurt. One page on the Khudai Khidmatgar says over 600 supporters died. A newspaper writer says "about 600" were killed.

<aside class="note"><b>No agreed number</b>: the official figure and the others differ by a factor of forty. The ANP, a Pakhtun party that carries on the Khudai Khidmatgar's tradition, has asked for an independent inquiry and a Truth and Reconciliation Commission.</aside>

## After the firing

A police report, FIR No. 184, dated 12 August 1948, was filed against the marchers at Prang police station. One politician says the Khudai Khidmatgar were fined 50,000 rupees. The government blew up the party's headquarters at Sardaryab. In mid-September 1948 the Khudai Khidmatgar were banned and more people were arrested.

Abdul Ghaffar Khan had taught his followers never to hit back. After he was freed, he reportedly told them that their path would stay the same.

In 1954 he called for a tribunal to look into the events at Charsadda. In July 1950 the Bengali leader Huseyn Shaheed Suhrawardy said at a meeting in Dhaka that the killing was worse than Jallianwala Bagh.

## How it is remembered

The ANP leader Afrasiab Khattak and others still ask for an independent inquiry. Some call the day "the Karbala of the Pathans". A newspaper in 2016 called it "Pakistan's forgotten massacre". One writer says it was left out of official memory and kept alive by folk songs and stories.

The ban on the movement was lifted only in 1972, after being imposed again in 1955.

## Why it matters

The Khudai Khidmatgar had promised never to use violence. The story of Babarra is the story of what happened when they marched on that promise. Whatever the number, it was a great loss for the families of the dead. The question of what happened, and of who was responsible, has not been settled.
