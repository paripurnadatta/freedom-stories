In 1902, a new club opened in Calcutta. It had a name, and it had a look.

The club was called the Anushilan Samiti. Anushilan is a word from Sanskrit. It means steady, patient effort, the kind you keep doing for a long time.

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: A <i>Samiti</i> is a club or a society. People who share one idea meet there and work together.</aside>

The Anushilan Samiti was an Indian fitness club. Its members lifted weights. They walked. They learned drills. They practised staying calm.

At first it looked like any other gym.

But the club had a secret. Under the floor, in a locked room, it also met to plan something dangerous. It trained young men to fight the British, who ruled India at that time.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: One record says the club was run by a lawyer named P. Mitter, or Pramathanath Mitra. Another record points to a different man, Satish Chandra Basu, as its founder. The two records do not agree on one single name.</aside>

The two names, if you have seen them before, appear in a small book. It was written in 1950, and its pages are faded and grey.

## Why they went underground

By 1905, the British had decided to split the large province of Bengal into two, West Bengal and East Bengal. Many people in Bengal were angry about this. They marched. They sang. They called it the Swadeshi movement, a push to buy Indian things and not British ones.

The partition changed the Anushilan Samiti, too. Before 1905, it had mostly been a place for exercise and quiet talk. After 1905, the people who met in its rooms started to talk about something else. They talked about how to end the British rule, and how to do it fast.

In 1907, the group's circle put out a small book called <i>Vartaman Rananiti</i>. The name means, in the language they wrote it in, "The present-day art of war." It was not a poetry book. It was a training book. It told young men how to handle weapons, and how to plan a raid on a police station.

The Anushilan Samiti felt this anger. Some of its members wanted to do more than march. They wanted to fight back with their hands, not just their voices.

In March 1906, a group inside the club broke away. It was led by a man named Barin Ghose. He was the brother of a very famous writer and thinker, Aurobindo Ghosh.

<aside class="note"><b>Barindra Kumar Ghosh</b> is the full name of Barin Ghose, the man the Wire article calls "Barin Ghose." He was the younger brother of Sri Aurobindo.</aside>

The Anushilan Samiti did not meet only in Calcutta. In the years after 1905, it opened a branch in a city to the east, Dhaka, far away from the first club. The branch was run by a man named Pulin Behari Das. He was, in the next few years, one of the quiet organisers who kept the work going in that part of the province.

This new group took a different path. It did not just want to fight the British. It wanted to spread the news of that fight to everyone, through a paper they would print by hand.

That paper had a name of its own. It was called <i>Jugantar</i>.

Alongside the paper, the same group put out a small book in 1905 called <i>Bhavani Mandir</i>. It was the kind of book the group passed hand to hand, and it carried the same idea as the paper: that quiet protest had not gone far enough.

## The paper that called for revolt

Jugantar was a newspaper, but it was also a small group. It met in secret. It printed pages with a hand press, the kind you push by hand.

Jugantar's pages argued that quiet protest was not enough. They called for action. They called for people to rise up.

In March 1906, Aurobindo Ghosh and another leader, Bipin Chandra Pal, helped start two new papers at the same time. One was Jugantar. The other was a paper called <i>Bande Mataram</i>, which means "Hail the Motherland."

By June 1908, the British shut the Jugantar office down. The group did not stop. It went underground, meaning it moved to secret rooms and kept printing by hand.

The group's work made many young people in Bengal excited and, in some cases, very angry. Some of them thought there was only one way left: to stop the British with their own hands.

## The bomb that fell on the wrong carriage

On the night of 30 April 1908, two young men did exactly that. Their names were Khudiram Bose and Prafulla Chaki.

They were travelling in a horse-drawn carriage near Muzaffarpur, in Bihar. They threw a bomb at it.

The bomb did not hit the carriage they thought it would. It hit a different one. Inside were a woman and her daughter. The woman was called Mrs Kennedy, and her daughter was called Ms Kennedy. Both of them died.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The story is often told as two teenagers throwing a bomb at a carriage they thought belonged to the Chief Presidency Magistrate. The names of the people actually inside are not settled in every account, but the deaths of Mrs Kennedy and her daughter are part of the record.</aside>

Chaki ran away and did not let himself be caught. A day later, he took his own life. Khudiram Bose was caught. He was only fifteen years old. He was held in prison, and in Muzaffarpur jail, on 11 August 1908, he was hanged.

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: To be <i>hanged</i> means to be put to death by the gallows, a wooden structure with a rope. It was the British way of executing people who were found guilty of very serious crimes.</aside>

## The trial that brought Aurobindo before the bar

The bomb did not just end a life. It set off a long legal case. The British arrested many young men in Calcutta. One of them, in a house in Muraripukur, was a thinker and poet named Aurobindo Ghosh.

The trial of Aurobindo and his followers became known as the Alipore bomb case. It lasted from 1908 to 1910.

It was a quiet court, but it was watched by all of India. The lawyers for the accused men spoke of freedom. The lawyers for the British spoke of the law. In the end, the case against Aurobindo fell apart. He was set free. Many of the other men were sent to prison, some to a famous cell on a small island off Andaman.

<aside class="note"><b>The Cellular Jail</b> was a prison on the island of Port Blair, in the Andaman Islands, far off the coast of India. It was one of the harshest places a person could be sent by the British.</aside>

The Anushilan Samiti had started as a gym in Calcutta. By 1910, its members had been tried, imprisoned, killed and exiled. The group's name would carry on for decades, in secret rooms and in prison cells. But the two names at its centre, P. Mitter and Basu, would never be settled by the records. Two men, one story, two different openings.
