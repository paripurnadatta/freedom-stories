Early in 1908 a young man delivered a parcel to a house in Calcutta. It was wrapped in plain brown paper. It looked like a book.

It was a book. But inside, part of the pages had been cut away, and a bomb lay hidden in the hollow.

## The man in the house

The house belonged to Douglas Kingsford, the Chief Presidency Magistrate of Calcutta. A magistrate is a judge in the city's lower courts.

Kingsford was not loved by the young people of Bengal. His court had tried the editors of the newspaper *Jugantar*. He had sent them to prison. When schoolboys took to the streets in protest, a boy named Sushil Sen was whipped on his orders. Wikipedia's page on Prafulla Chaki says he was known for hard sentences, and for ordering whippings, against young political workers.

The Anushilan Samiti decided he was a target.

## The parcel

A man named Hemchandra Kanungo, who also went by Hem Das, made the device. In 1907 he had gone to Paris. There a Russian exile named Nicholas Safranski taught him how bombs were made. When Hem came back he worked with Barin Ghosh in Calcutta.

The book he used was a law book. It was Herbert Broom's *Commentaries on the Common Law*. A young man named Paresh Mallick carried it to Kingsford's home in brown paper.

Kingsford took the parcel. He did not open it. He put it on a shelf, meaning to look at it later.

## Moved away

By March 1908 the government feared for his safety. It promoted him to District Judge and sent him to Muzaffarpur in northern Bihar, a long way from Calcutta.

Kingsford packed up his house. His furniture went with him. So did his library. The unopened parcel went too.

## What came next

The Samiti did not give up on Kingsford. In April two young men, Prafulla Chaki and Khudiram Bose, travelled to Muzaffarpur with a second bomb. What happened to them is told in "The night a bomb fell on the wrong carriage".

The books tell us nothing more about the parcel itself. They say only that it went with him.

## Why it matters

The book shows how the Samiti worked. It used a disguise, a messenger and a bomb-maker who had learned his skill abroad. It also shows that the plan failed by chance. The man it was meant for never opened the parcel.

People still disagree about Kingsford's court. Many young Bengalis saw his sentences as cruel. But others would say that no court, however harsh, gives anyone the right to kill the judge. Both views are held in India today.
