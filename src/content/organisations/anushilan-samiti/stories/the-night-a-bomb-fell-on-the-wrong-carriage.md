In April 1908 two young men got off a train in Muzaffarpur, a town in Bihar. They were far from home. They carried a bomb, and they had a plan.

They told people at a rest-house for travellers that their names were Haren Sarkar and Dinesh Chandra Roy. Neither name was real.

Their real names were Khudiram Bose and Prafulla Chaki. Khudiram was eighteen. Prafulla was nineteen.

## Two boys, two roads

Khudiram's life began with a custom. His two older brothers had died as babies. So when he was born in December 1889, his family "sold" him to his eldest sister for three handfuls of grain. The old belief was that this would keep him alive. The grain was called *khud*, and that is how he got his name.

He lost both parents while he was still small. His sister Aparupa brought him up. At school in Medinipur he heard talk of revolution, and at fifteen he became a volunteer with the Anushilan Samiti.

Prafulla was born in a village in what is now Bangladesh, in a family with some land. At school in Rangpur he joined a student protest and was thrown out of the school. Later Barin Ghosh, one of the Samiti's leaders, took him to Calcutta.

<aside class="note"><b>Anushilan Samiti</b>: a secret group of young Bengalis who wanted to end British rule by force. It began as a club for exercise and study. You can read how in "The gym that was something else".</aside>

## The man they were sent to find

The target was a British judge named Douglas Kingsford. In Calcutta he had been a magistrate who gave hard sentences to young people in the protest movement. The young men of the Samiti hated him for it.

An earlier plan had already failed. A bomb hidden inside a book had been sent to his house, and he had never opened the parcel. You can read that story in "The book that was a bomb". By March 1908 the government had moved Kingsford to Muzaffarpur, for his own safety.

The Samiti followed him there.

## Three weeks of watching

Khudiram and Prafulla stayed at the rest-house for more than three weeks. They watched where the judge went and when. Kingsford often went to the European Club in the evening to play cards.

The police in Calcutta had heard about the plan. They sent a warning to Muzaffarpur. Kingsford was told, but he ignored it. Four men were put on guard at his house.

One evening the two young men went to the park across from the club. They dressed like schoolboys. A constable saw them. The next evening they came back and a constable noticed them again, so they slipped away and then doubled back in the dark. One of them waited in the shade of a tree with the bomb.

## The evening of 30 April

The card game ended at about half past eight. Kingsford and his wife drove away in one carriage. Close in front of them went an almost identical carriage. In it sat two English ladies, Mrs Kennedy and her daughter. They had been playing cards with the judge's party.

Khudiram ran up to the first carriage as it passed the tree. He threw the bomb inside.

There was a loud explosion. The carriage was wrecked. Kingsford was not in it. Miss Kennedy died within the hour. Her mother died two days later, on 2 May.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The sources do not agree on who the ladies' family was. Wikipedia's Khudiram page calls Mr Kennedy a barrister. Its page on Prafulla Chaki calls him "a local congressman". The Alipore case page also says "barrister", so that is the word used here.</aside>

The two women had nothing to do with the quarrel. Neither had ever heard of the Samiti.

## The long walk

The two young men ran in different directions.

Khudiram walked all night. By morning he had gone about twenty-five miles and reached a small station called Waini. He stopped at a tea stall to ask for a glass of water. Two constables saw his dusty feet and his tired face. They asked him a few questions and did not like the answers. A revolver fell from his clothes as he struggled with them. They held him.

On Khudiram they found thirty-seven bullets, thirty rupees, a railway map and a page of the train timetable.

Prafulla had a longer road. A villager named Trigunacharan Ghosh took him in, gave him food and let him rest. That night Prafulla took a train towards Calcutta. A police sub-inspector named Nandalal Banerjee was in the same compartment. He got talking to the young man and grew suspicious. At the station at Mokama he tried to arrest him.

Prafulla would not be taken alive. He used his last bullet on himself and died there on the platform. It was 2 May 1908.

## The trial

Khudiram was brought back to Muzaffarpur. People crowded the station to see the boy. The Statesman newspaper wrote that he looked "determined" and that he cried out "Vande Mataram" as he sat in the carriage.

At first Khudiram said he alone was to blame. He did not yet know that Prafulla was dead. Later he took back his statement.

His trial began on 21 May. Lawyers defended him, and some of them took no fee. On 13 June the judge sentenced him to death. One report says Khudiram smiled. Another says he joked that he could teach the judge how to make bombs. Both are stories told afterwards.

His lawyers asked the High Court to look again. They said the first statement had not been taken in the way the law demanded. The High Court heard the case on 8 July and refused. The sentence stood.

## 11 August 1908

Khudiram was hanged at six in the morning in Muzaffarpur jail. The Amrita Bazar Patrika wrote that he "walked to the gallows firmly and cheerfully". Crowds followed his body through the streets and threw flowers on it.

Many people in Bengal felt proud of him. Weavers made dhotis with his name woven along the border, and schoolboys wore them. Songs and plays were made about him.

<aside class="note"><b>Two views</b>: The historian Shukla Sanyal writes that to many Bengalis the two young men answered years of insults from British officers. Others, then and later, said the bomb should never have been thrown. Two women were dead, and they had done no harm.</aside>

## Why it matters

The bomb at Muzaffarpur made people across India ask a new question. Could young people fight British rule with bombs? Bengal's police acted at once, and within days more than thirty men were arrested in Calcutta. That led to the biggest trial of the time, which you can read about in "The trial that watched all of India".

It also left a hard question that people still argue over. Khudiram and Prafulla were brave, and they were very young. But their action killed two women who were not the people they wanted.
