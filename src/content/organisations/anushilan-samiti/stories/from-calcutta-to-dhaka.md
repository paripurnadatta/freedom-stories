In 1902, the Anushilan Samiti opened in Calcutta. By the mid-1900s, it was not only in Calcutta.

The group's meetings had a second home. That home was in Dhaka, far to the east of Calcutta. Dhaka was the capital of another part of the old Bengal province, and it had its own young men who were angry about the British rule.

The Dhaka branch was led by a man named Pulin Behari Das. He was the person who kept the secret meetings going there.

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: A <i>branch</i> of a group is a smaller version of it in another town, run by people there. The main group, Anushilan Samiti, had a Calcutta branch and a Dhaka branch.</aside>

The two branches did not always work in the same way. In Calcutta, the group's name changed and split. Some of its members broke away in 1906 to start a newspaper called Jugantar. In Dhaka, the branch kept its own name, and it kept its own way of meeting.

The Dhaka branch grew, in the years that followed, into one of the most important secret groups in east Bengal. It stayed small, and it stayed secret, but it did the same quiet work as the Calcutta branch: it trained young men, it kept the meetings hidden, and it passed the word along to other towns.

The name Anushilan Samiti was, by then, a bridge between two cities, one secret club, and two sets of young men who did not yet know how far their work would carry them.

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: <i>Anushilan</i> means steady, patient effort. A group that met in secret rooms in two cities, for many years, was living up to the name.</aside>
