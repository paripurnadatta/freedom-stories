In 1902, the Anushilan Samiti opened in Calcutta. By the mid-1900s, it was no longer only there.

The group's meetings had a second home. That home was in Dhaka, far to the east. Dhaka was the capital of another part of the old Bengal province, and it had its own young men, and its own anger about the British rule.

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: A <i>branch</i> of a group is a smaller version of it in another town, run by people there. The Anushilan Samiti had a branch in Calcutta and a branch in Dhaka.</aside>

The Dhaka branch was led by a man named Pulin Behari Das. He kept the secret meetings going there. He did the same quiet work the Calcutta branch did: he trained young men, he kept the meetings hidden, and he passed the word along to other towns in the east.

The two branches did not always work the same way. In Calcutta, the group's name changed and split. Some of its members broke away in March 1906 to start a newspaper called Jugantar. In Dhaka, the branch kept its own name. It kept its own way of meeting, and it did not follow the breakaway group's path.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: One account says the Dhaka branch grew to have its own separate meetings and printed its own pages, apart from Calcutta. Another says the two branches kept close links, and that the Dhaka men sent words back to Calcutta on a regular schedule. The two accounts do not fully agree on how far apart the two branches really were.</aside>

The Dhaka branch stayed small, and it stayed secret. But it did the same quiet work as the Calcutta branch. It trained a second generation of young men, in a city where the British had not yet felt the full weight of the Anushilan name.

In the years before the big trial, the Dhaka branch kept its own meetings, and its own small list of names. The two cities did not move in lockstep. The Dhaka branch's quiet work stayed mostly in the east. It did not reach Calcutta's press, and it did not reach Calcutta's court. And yet the Calcutta branch's name was being spoken, in those same years, in one of the country's biggest trials.

By the time of the Alipore trial in 1908, the name Anushilan Samiti had become a bridge between two cities. It was one secret club, with two sets of young men on either side. And it was a movement that had gone, in just a few years, from a quiet fitness club in Calcutta to a courtroom where the whole country was watching.

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: To go <i>underground</i> means to keep meeting, but in secret, where the police could not find the rooms. The Anushilan Samiti went underground in Calcutta in 1908, and it kept meeting underground for a few years after that.</aside>

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: <i>Anushilan</i> means steady, patient effort, the kind you keep doing for a long time. A group that met in secret rooms in two cities, for many years, was living up to its name.</aside>

In 1909, the Calcutta branch's work reached the courtroom one more time, when the appeal court kept the death sentence for the two men named in the Alipore verdict. The Dhaka branch, far to the east, heard the news through the same secret channels it had always used. The two cities, one name, two quiet rooms, and a country that was watching both of them.
