Pulin Behari Das was born in 1877 in a village in eastern Bengal. His father was a lawyer. As a boy Pulin loved physical exercise. He became a skilled *lathial*, a fighter with a long bamboo stick.

He studied at Dhaka College. He even worked there as a laboratory assistant while still a student. But his heart was in the gym.

<aside class="note"><b>Akhara</b>: a place where people wrestle and train, usually with a mud floor.</aside>

## The akhara at Tikatuli

In Calcutta a woman named Sarala Devi ran a gym of her own. Pulin heard of its success. In 1903 he opened an akhara at Tikatuli, in Dhaka. Two years later he trained in sword-fighting and stick-fighting under a famous teacher named Murtaza.

## A question in the crowd

In September 1906 two visitors toured the new province of Eastern Bengal and Assam. They were Bipin Chandra Pal, a great speaker, and Pramathanath Mitra, the head of the Calcutta Samiti. In his speech Mitra asked who was ready to give his life for the country.

Pulin stepped forward.

Mitra named him to set up a branch in Dhaka. Pulin started it with eighty young men.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia says Pulin founded the branch in October 1906. Banglapedia says September. Both say eighty young men.</aside>

## The branches grow

Pulin had a gift for organising. The Dhaka group soon grew larger than the Calcutta one. It spread to other districts of East Bengal and reached about five hundred branches.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia says the Dhaka Samiti "soon" had over 500 branches. Banglapedia gives that number for 1932. Both say it grew very large.</aside>

Members were mostly school and college students from Hindu families. Pulin also started the National School in Dhaka. It was meant as a training place. Students began with sticks and wooden swords. Later they moved on to daggers, and then to pistols.

The Samiti needed money for weapons. Banglapedia says it got money by raiding the houses of people who were loyal to the British. In early 1908 a group of members robbed the home of a landowner at Barrah, near Dhaka. The money went to buy arms. The people robbed were Indians, not British officers.

In December 1907 the Samiti tried to kill Basil Allen, who had been the magistrate of Dhaka. He was shot at a railway station as he travelled to England. He survived.

## Jail and the long road back

The police arrested Pulin in 1908. After he came out in 1910 he began again, and was arrested a second time. This was the Dhaka Conspiracy Case, in which about ninety men were accused. Pulin was sentenced to prison for life and sent to the Cellular Jail in the Andaman Islands. There he lived alongside other revolutionaries, among them Barindra Ghosh and Vinayak Savarkar.

His term was cut short. He was freed in 1918 and kept under house arrest for one more year.

By then India had changed. Many former revolutionaries had decided to follow Mahatma Gandhi and join the Non-Cooperation Movement. Pulin did not. He did not accept Gandhi as a leader, and he criticised the Congress policy of non-violence. In 1920 he started a new group, the Bharat Sevak Sangh, and published two journals. In 1922 he left politics.

## Back to the gym

In 1928 he opened a new akhara in Calcutta, the Bangiya Byayam Samiti. It taught stick-fighting, sword-play, wrestling and even judo. He died in 1949. The University of Calcutta gives a medal in his name.

## Why it matters

Pulin shows how a fitness club became a movement. He never forgot the gym. He started in one and he ended in one.
