In 1909, a young lawyer stood in a courtroom in Calcutta and argued for a man the government called a criminal. That man's name was Aurobindo Ghosh, a thinker and poet who had been arrested after the bomb was thrown in Muzaffarpur.

The lawyer's name was Chittaranjan Das. He did not win the case for Aurobindo in the way the lawyers for the other men did. But he gave a closing argument that people still quote, years later, about the kind of man Aurobindo was, in his books and in his poetry.

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: To make a <i>closing argument</i> in a trial means to speak to the court after all the evidence has been heard, to say what the lawyer believes the court should decide.</aside>

Das went on to do other things, in the years after. He became the first mayor of Calcutta's municipal corporation. He resigned the Congress presidency after losing a motion at the Gaya session. And in 1923, he co-founded the Swaraj Party, the new party that had decided to go into the councils and work from inside them.

Das died on 16 June 1925, in the hill station of Darjeeling. He was not old. He had been born in 1870, in Calcutta. And he had worked in the city his whole life, first as a lawyer, then as a mayor, and then as the leader of the party he had helped build.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: one account puts Das's death at Darjeeling on 16 June 1925. The exact circumstances of his final days are not settled in every record, so this story keeps the date and place in the main text and does not add detail about how he died.</aside>

After he died, people in Calcutta named things after him. An avenue, a road that runs through the city, carries his name. A cancer institute, the biggest one in the region, is called after him. And a locomotive works, near the town of Asansol, is named for him. He had been a man who used his hands and his voice for the people who did not have a voice in the room.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: one account says the locomotive works near Asansol is named after Das. The exact story of how it was named is not settled in every record, so this story keeps the fact in the main text and the uncertainty in the note.</aside>

Das had been born in 1870, to a family from Telirbagh, in what is now Munshiganj, in Bangladesh. He had gone to London to study law, between 1890 and 1894. And he had come back to Calcutta, where he stayed, and where he built the party that carried his name into the council.

In the years before the party was set up, Das had already done the quiet work that led to it. He had argued for the men who had been arrested, in the courts of Calcutta, in the years after the bomb was thrown in Muzaffarpur. And he had spoken, in the councils, for the people of Bengal, in the years after the first councils were opened in the country.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: one account says Das's work as a lawyer in the courts, and his work in the councils, are the two things that led him to co-found the Swaraj Party in 1923. The exact details of how those two kinds of work fit together, in the years before the party was set up, are not settled in every record, so this story keeps the fact in the main text and the uncertainty in the note.</aside>
