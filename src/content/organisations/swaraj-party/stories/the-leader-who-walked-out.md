In 1899 a lawyer in Allahabad was thrown out of his community. His crime was a sea voyage.

Motilal Nehru had been to Europe, and he went again and again. Some in his community, the Kashmiri Brahmins, believed that a person who crossed the sea lost caste. To get it back, he had to perform a ceremony of penance. Motilal refused. So the community expelled him.

He went on with his work. His law practice was a great success.

## A house with a plan

Motilal was born on 6 May 1861. His father had died before he was born. Like his ancestors, he was more at home in Persian, Urdu and Arabic than in any other Indian language. His elder brother Nandlal was a lawyer too. When Nandlal died in 1887, Motilal was twenty-five. He was now the only earner for a big family.

By 1900 he was rich enough to buy a huge house in the Civil Lines of Allahabad. It had once belonged to the educator Sir Syed Ahmad Khan. Motilal renamed it Anand Bhavan, the "house of joy".

At the housewarming party, a British guest named Sir William Moor said he hoped the great house would become the cement that held the British Empire together in India. It turned out quite differently. Anand Bhavan became a home of the freedom movement.

## From suits to khadi

In 1918, under Gandhi's influence, Motilal gave up English clothes. He was one of the first leaders to do so. In 1919 came the Jallianwala Bagh killings. He is said to have written afterwards, "My blood is boiling."

In February 1919 he had also started a daily newspaper of his own, *The Independent*. He had chaired the board of another Allahabad paper, *The Leader*, but thought it too mild for his views.

That December he was the president of the Congress at Amritsar. When Gandhi put forward the non-cooperation plan at a special Congress in Calcutta in September 1920, Motilal backed it. Among the front-rank leaders there, he was the only one who did. He was arrested during the movement.

Then Gandhi stopped it, after Chauri Chaura. Motilal said openly that this was wrong. In 1922 he joined Chittaranjan Das in the Swaraj Party.

## The Assembly in Delhi

The Central Legislative Assembly sat in Delhi. It had 145 members. Of these, 104 were elected and 41 were chosen by the government. It could not do what it liked. The Viceroy could turn a bill into law even if the Assembly refused it.

In 1923 Motilal was elected to it. He led the Swaraj Party there, as Leader of the Opposition.

<aside class="note"><b>Opposition</b>: the members who disagree with the government and speak against its plans.</aside>

What could such a party do? It could vote. Again and again the Swarajists voted against government bills that asked for money, and spoke fiercely against them. Under Motilal the party managed to defeat finance bills and other laws, or at least to delay them.

On 30 December 1923 the party's general council set out its demands. India should have a government fully answerable to its own people. The government should start by freeing political prisoners and ending its harsh laws. It should then call a round-table conference to agree the main points of a constitution for India.

<aside class="note"><b>Constitution</b>: the basic rules of how a country is governed.</aside>

## Did it work?

The Viceroy still had the last word. The Swarajists knew it. They had a different aim from passing laws. They wanted to make the 1919 law unworkable, by voting down the money the government asked for.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia says that, apart from some heated debates and standoffs over rules, the party's real aim of obstructing British rule failed. GKToday says that the Swarajists' votes against the budget forced the Viceroy to pass it with his own special powers.</aside>

There was another danger. Motilal agreed to join a committee that looked at recruiting Indians as officers in the army. This made some of his colleagues think they could go further and take jobs in the government itself. Soon the party's members began to disagree about taking such posts.

## The walk-out

In March 1926 Motilal asked the Assembly for something big. He wanted a conference of Indian representatives to write a constitution that made India a self-governing dominion, like Canada or Australia. The British Parliament would then pass it into law.

The Assembly turned the demand down. Motilal and his colleagues stood up and walked out of the house.

<aside class="note"><b>Dominion</b>: a country that governs itself but stays inside the British Empire. Canada and Australia were examples.</aside>

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: One Wikipedia page says Motilal and his colleagues walked out. His own page says that they gave up their seats in the Assembly and went back to the Congress. Both may be true. They walked out first, and left for good afterwards.</aside>

## The last years

Two years later, in 1928, Britain sent the Simon Commission to India to look at how India should be governed. There was not one Indian on it. Indians of every party were angry. A committee under Motilal wrote a plan for India's constitution, known as the Nehru Report. It was the first such plan written only by Indians. It asked for dominion status.

Not everyone was pleased. Younger Congress leaders, among them his own son Jawaharlal and Subhas Bose, wanted complete independence. Muslim leaders, especially Muhammad Ali Jinnah, also rejected the report. Motilal presided over the Congress at Calcutta in December 1928. Gandhi found a way out. If the British did not accept the report within a year, the Congress would demand complete independence.

In 1929 Jawaharlal was elected president of the Congress. It pleased Motilal to see his son take over, though Jawaharlal had opposed his father's liking for dominion status.

Motilal was ill by then, and kept out of the great events of 1929 to 1931. He was arrested with Jawaharlal. His health gave way and he was set free. In the last week of January 1931 Gandhi and the Congress leaders were let out of jail. Motilal died on 6 February 1931, with his son and Gandhi beside him.

## Why it matters

Motilal took his party into the government's own house and spoke against it there. People still argue about how much this achieved. When he saw it could go no further, he did not stay. He walked out.
