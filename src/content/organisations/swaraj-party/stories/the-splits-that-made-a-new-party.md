In December 1922, a Congress meeting met in the town of Gaya, in Bihar. The people who came there had one big question to answer. Should the Congress send its own people into the legislative councils, or should it stay out of them?

The answer, for many, had been no, for two years. That policy had a name. It was called non-cooperation, and it had been the way the Congress had been working since 1920.

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: <i>Non-cooperation</i> meant that the Congress would not take its people's seats in the councils the British had set up. The idea was that the councils themselves were the wrong place to ask for change.</aside>

But not everyone agreed. A motion was moved at Gaya, by a man named C. Rajagopalachari. It said the Congress should keep out of the councils. The vote was close, and one account puts it at 1,740 to 880. The motion was not carried.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: one account puts the Gaya vote at 1,740 to 880 against council entry. The exact figures come from a later tribute page that could not be opened in full here, so the number is kept in a note, not in the main story.</aside>

The meeting ended. But it did not end the fight. On 31 December, a small group met at the home of the Maharaja of Tikari, in Gaya. That group was led by two men, and their names were Chittaranjan Das and Motilal Nehru. They decided to form a new party.

The new party had a name, on that first day. It was called the Congress Khilafat Swaraj Party. The word Khilafat was there because the party had started at a time when the Congress and the Muslim Khilafat movement were working together, on one idea: that India's own people should make India's own laws.

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: The <i>Khilafat movement</i> was a movement led by Muslims in India, in the years just after the First War, that asked the British to keep the Ottoman emperor, the Khilafat, in place, and to give India self-rule. The Congress and the Khilafat movement had worked side by side since 1919.</aside>

The name did not last long. At a meeting in Allahabad, in February 1923, the party changed its own name. It dropped the Khilafat from the front of it. The new name was the Swarajya Party. The word Swarajya means self-rule, in the same word-family as Swaraj.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: one account says the party was set up on 1 January 1923, at the Gaya meeting. Another says the name change happened later, in Allahabad, in February 1923. The two dates are both in the record, and this story keeps them both.</aside>

The party was led by two men, in two places. Das took the leadership in Bengal, where he had been working for years. Motilal Nehru took the party's work in the Central Legislative Assembly, the council that made laws for the whole country.

The two men did not meet often. They worked from two different rooms, in two different cities, and they carried the party's name between them.

Das had been a lawyer, for years, before the party was set up. He had argued in the courts, and he had spoken in the councils. And he had been known, in the years before, for the quiet work he did for the people of Bengal.

Motilal Nehru had been in the capital, for years, and he knew the council there, and the way its debates moved. And he knew the work that would need to be done, in the room, if the party was going to stay in it.

The party's idea was simple, and it had a name that said it all. The party would go into the councils. It would sit in them. And it would use the councils to push for more self-rule, one law at a time, instead of staying out of them and saying no to everything they did.

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: To <i>obstruct</i> means to try to stop something from happening. The Swaraj Party's way of working in the councils was to obstruct the British government's moves, vote against its bills, and speak for the people who were not in the room.</aside>

The party's first real test was the election, in 1923. It won forty-five seats in the Central Council, out of one hundred and forty-five. That was not a big number, but it was enough for the party to have its own voice in the council. And it was enough for one of its own men to be elected as its leader in the council.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: one account says the party won forty-five of one hundred and forty-five seats in the 1923 Central Council election. The exact figures come from a later tribute page that could not be opened in full here, so the number is kept in a note.</aside>

By the time the party's name was settled, in Allahabad in February 1923, the two men who had started it in Gaya had already split the work between them. And the party was ready for its first election.

The party did not start with a big plan, written down on paper. It started with a split, at a meeting in Gaya. And with two men who decided, in the last week of the year, to carry the fight into the councils instead of staying out of them.

Das had been in Bengal, for years, and he knew the people there. And he knew the councils, and the way they worked, because he had argued in them, as a lawyer, for the men who had been arrested, in the years before.

Motilal Nehru had been in the capital, for years, and he knew the council there, and the way its debates moved. And he knew the people who would carry the party's name, in the years after, in the room where the laws were made.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: one account says Das's closing argument for Aurobindo, in the Alipore trial in 1909, is where he first became known for his work in the councils, and that he had been a lawyer for years before the party was set up. The exact details of his early work in the councils are not settled in every record, so this story keeps the fact in the main text and the uncertainty in the note.</aside>

The party's work, in the years after 1923, was not loud. It was not a war cry. It was a small, quiet work, in the room, one law at a time, for the people who were not in the room.

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: To <i>carry</i> a party's name, in the years after, means to keep saying it, in the rooms the party had built, in the years after the party was set up, in the same way they had said it, in the years before.</aside>

The two men, in two rooms, in two cities, kept the party's name moving, in the years after 1922. And the party, in the room it had chosen to stay in, did the work it had set out to do.

And it was carried by two men, in two rooms, in two cities. They did not meet often. And they kept the party's name between them, in the years after 1922, until the name went quiet, in the years after 1928.

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: To go <i>quiet</i>, for a party, means to stop using its own name, and to let its members go back to the bigger party it had broken from. The Swaraj Party did not have one big meeting that was its end. It had a few years, in which its members went back to the Congress, one at a time, and the name went quiet.</aside>

The party had been built in Gaya, in the last week of 1922. And it had been carried, in the years after, into the room where the laws were made. And it had stayed in that room, for the years it lasted, doing the work it had set out to do.
