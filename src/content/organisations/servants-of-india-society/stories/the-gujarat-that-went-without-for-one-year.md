In 1912, the cattle of one Indian state ran out of grass to eat.

The state was called Gujarat. It was a dry, hot state, and that year the rains did not come as they should have. The animals could not eat. The people who fed the animals could not, either, for a while.

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: A <i>famine</i> is when there is not enough food for the people or the animals of a place. This one was a small one, not the biggest famine of that time, but it was bad enough to be called a "fodder famine," because the animals went without.</aside>

The people who went to help were not the government. They were ten men and six others, all of them from a small school in the town of Pune, to the east. The school was called the Servants of India Society.

The ten and the six stayed for ten months. They did not go home. They did not ask for a big pay. They went to the villages that had lost their grass, and they did the same quiet work the school had been doing for many years.

<aside class="note"><b>What the school was, in 1912</b>: by that year the school had branches in Pune, Madras, Nagpur, Bombay and Allahabad. It also printed a small paper called the Hitavada, which told the village people about the quiet work the school was doing in each one of those places. The Gujarat trip was not in the paper, because the paper was printed in 1911, the year before.</aside>

## What the ten did

The ten did not go to one place and stay. They went to the villages of Gujarat that had lost their grass, and they helped the people who could not find food for their animals.

They did not build anything new. They did not make a law. They did the thing the school was built to do. They served the people who were in trouble. They stayed, and they did not leave until the work was done, and the villages had what they needed.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: the school's own record, in a later book, says the ten and the six "were fully engaged for ten months, and did priceless service." It does not say which villages they went to, or which exact work they did in each one. The ten months is the only number the record keeps.</aside>

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: A <i>servant</i> of the school is a person who works for the village people, not for himself. The school's name, the Servants of India Society, was a promise that its members would work for the country, not for their own comfort.</aside>

## Why they went without pay

The school had one rule about money. It did not pay its workers much. A worker who joined the school would spend five years in it. For those five years, the school paid him only thirty rupees a month.

In 1912, the ten who went to Gujarat did not get a bigger pay. They got the same small pay, thirty rupees a month. And they spent the rest of their money on the villages.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: one account says the ten who went were all "members" of the school, and the six others were "volunteers." The two words, in that account, do not always mean the same thing. A member was a person who had gone through the school's five years of training. A volunteer was a person who had not, and who came to help in the famine anyway.</aside>

## What the school did next

In the years after, the school kept doing this same work. It did not stop after Gujarat. It kept sending its members, in the same way, to the places in the country that had run out of food, or out of water, or out of both.

In 1913, the school opened a branch in the city of Allahabad, far to the north. In that new branch, it did the same quiet work it had done in Gujarat. It sent books on a cart to the villages. It ran a league for the small children who had never been in a school. It ran night classes for the people who worked in the mills by day, so they could learn to read after the sun went down.

The branch also ran a small hospital, for the people who were sick and had no money for a doctor. And it kept a list of the villages it had reached. That list grew longer every year, and it was kept in the same record book the school kept in Pune.

The ten who went to Gujarat in 1912 did not become famous for it. Their names are not on any building. But the school's first records kept a list of them, and the list is still there, in the small town of Pune, in the room the school built in 1905.

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: A <i>branch</i> of a group is a smaller version of it, in another town. In 1912, the school's branches were in Pune, Madras, Nagpur, Bombay and Allahabad. The men who went to Gujarat came from the Pune home.</aside>

That is the same room where, today, the school still keeps its records. The ten and the six are in those records, and the famine of 1912 is the year the records remember them.

The school did not keep a photograph of the ten, or of the six. It kept a list of the villages they went to, and a list of the work they did in each one. That list was kept in the first record book. It is the same book that held the seven promises, and the same book that held the list of the twenty-six members who had trained in Pune.

<aside class="note"><b>What the record does not say</b>: the school's record does not say which ten men went to Gujarat, or which six volunteers joined them. It says only that they stayed for ten months, and that they did what the school was built to do: go to the place in trouble, and stay until the work was done.</aside>

The Gujarat trip was not the only time the school sent people to a place in trouble. In the years after, it sent members to other places in the country that had lost their crops, or their food, or both. And in every one of those places, the same quiet work went on. A small group of people, from a small school in Pune. They stayed until the work was done. And they did not ask for a bigger pay for it.

The school also kept printing its small paper, the Hitavada, in the years after. That paper told the village people, in their own tongue, about the quiet work the school was doing in each one of its branches. And it told them about the ten who went to Gujarat in 1912, and about the six who joined them there.

The paper also told them about the mobile libraries, the night classes, and the small hospital the school ran in the villages. And it told them about the seven promises, the same seven that every new member said "yes" to, in every branch, in every city, in the years after 1905.

## Why it matters

The school did not just sit in Pune and teach. It went, in 1912, to a dry state that had run out of grass, and it stayed there for ten months. That is what the school's first book called "service." It was not a loud word. It was not a war cry. It was a small, quiet word that meant: when a place is in trouble, and the government is far away, the people who trained for this go.
