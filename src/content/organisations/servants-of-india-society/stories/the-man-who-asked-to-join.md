In 1912 an Indian leader sailed to South Africa. He came at the invitation of a lawyer who was fighting for the rights of Indians there. The leader was Gopal Krishna Gokhale. The lawyer was Mohandas Gandhi.

Gandhi was a younger man, and he looked up to Gokhale. He later called him his political guru. Years afterwards Gandhi wrote that Gokhale was "pure as crystal, gentle as a lamb, brave as a lion".

## A friend in India

Gokhale had been helping Gandhi's cause from far away. In India's Legislative Council he spoke against the system of indentured labour. Under that system poor Indians signed contracts to work for years on plantations overseas, under harsh rules. In 1910 he put a resolution on the matter before the Council. Wikipedia says this work helped bring an end to indentured migration to Natal.

## A plan for Gandhi

By 1913 Gokhale had run the Servants of India Society for eight years. A visitor who talked to him in London that June wrote down what Gokhale said about the group. At the end he added a line. Gandhi, he wrote, had "signified his intention of becoming a worker under the Society."

Gandhi sailed home in 1915. He landed at the Apollo Bunder in Bombay on 9 January. Crowds welcomed him. The government gave him a gold medal called the Kaiser-i-Hind in the King's birthday honours that year. His friendship with Gokhale, says the mkgandhi.org biography, was proof enough that he was a safe politician.

Gokhale wished him to join the Society. He also asked Gandhi for a promise. For one year Gandhi would not speak out on public questions. It was to be a year of learning and "probation".

Gandhi gave his word. About joining the Society, he later wrote that obeying Gokhale's wish was a position he "loved to be in".

## A worry in Pune

Some members of the Society were not so sure. They feared that the ideals and the methods of Gandhi were too far from their own. The Society worked by petitions, studies and lawful pressure. Gandhi had led people to break laws and fill prisons in South Africa.

While the members were still discussing this, Gandhi left Pune. He visited his old home towns of Porbandar and Rajkot. Then he went to Shantiniketan, the school started by the poet Rabindranath Tagore.

## The telegram

At Shantiniketan, a telegram came from Pune. Gokhale was dead. The date was 19 February 1915.

Gandhi was stunned. He went to Pune. According to the Gandhi biography on mkgandhi.org, he mourned Gokhale by going barefoot for a year.

## "I began the wooing"

In his autobiography Gandhi tells what came next. After the mourning ceremonies the members of the Society met to talk about the future. The question came up of whether Gandhi should join.

He felt it was now his duty. While Gokhale lived, he wrote, he had not needed to ask. He simply obeyed. Now Gokhale was gone, and Gandhi felt he was "thrown on my own resources". He believed that joining would please Gokhale's spirit. "So, without hesitation and with firmness, I began the wooing."

Most of the members were in Pune. Gandhi pleaded with them and tried to calm their fears. But the members were divided. One group wanted him. The other was strongly against.

Gandhi saw that both sides cared for him. But he also saw that they cared for the Society at least as much. Those who opposed him said that they and he stood "as the poles asunder in various vital matters". They feared that having him as a member might hurt the very things the Society was made for.

## Two ways of working

The members were not wrong about the difference. Gokhale's Society believed in working "by all constitutional means". Gandhi's way, in the years ahead, would become mass protest: the non-cooperation movement of 1920 and 1921.

Srinivasa Sastri, who became the head of the Society after Gokhale, was Gandhi's friend. Gandhi called him "elder brother". Even so, Wikipedia says, Sastri opposed Gandhi's joining. When Gandhi later asked him about starting non-cooperation, Sastri advised him not to.

## What Gandhi decided

The members parted without deciding. Another meeting was set for later.

Gandhi went home troubled. He asked himself whether it was right to be admitted by a majority vote, when the Society was so sharply divided. He decided that the best course was to withdraw his request. That way the people who opposed him would not be put in an awkward position.

He wrote to Mr Sastri at once. He asked him not to hold the second meeting at all.

Those who had opposed him understood and were grateful. Gandhi wrote that it "bound us in closer bonds of friendship". Then he added a sentence people still remember: "The withdrawal of my application made me truly a member of the Society."

<aside class="note"><b>Two views</b>: The Gandhi biography on mkgandhi.org says that Gandhi made "another effort" to join and then withdrew when he saw the Society was split. Wikipedia's page on Gokhale says he "chose not to become a member". Gandhi's own account says he withdrew so as to spare the members a hard choice.</aside>

## A member in spirit

Years later Gandhi looked back. He wrote that it was well he had not formally joined, and that those who opposed him were right. Their views on matters of principle were "widely divergent", he said.

But there was no bitterness. "We have remained as brothers," he wrote, "and the Society's Poona home has always been for me a place of pilgrimage."

He had never become an official member, he added. "But I have ever been a member in spirit."

## Why it matters

Gandhi and the Servants of India Society wanted the same thing: a better India with fewer people suffering. They chose different roads. The story shows two things about Gandhi. He did not force himself into any room. And he kept his friendship with people who disagreed with his methods.

Soon he began the work that made him famous. The Society kept on in its own quiet way.
