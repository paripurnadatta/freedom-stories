On 23 December 1929, a bomb exploded under a train outside Delhi. Lord Irwin, the Viceroy, was on board. He was not hurt.

The bomb had been placed by the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association, the HSRA. You can read how in the story of the bomb under the Viceroy's train.

This story is about what came afterwards: a quarrel in print between Mahatma Gandhi and the young revolutionaries. It is one of the most famous arguments in India's freedom struggle. Both sides wanted a free India. They did not agree on how to get it.

## At Lahore

The Congress was meeting at Lahore that week. Gandhi moved a resolution. It congratulated the Viceroy and Lady Irwin on their "fortunate and narrow escape".

Not everyone liked it. The vote was close.

The count of the vote is not certain. One article reports 904 votes for the resolution and 823 against. The revolutionaries' own paper says that it passed "with a majority of only 31 votes in a total strength of 1913 members." The two counts do not match, and neither is confirmed by a record of the vote.

## Gandhi's article

About a week later, on 2 January 1930, Gandhi published an article in his weekly, *Young India*. He called it "The Cult of the Bomb".

He was sorry that the Congress had been divided over the resolution. He wrote that if the Viceroy had been killed, the Lahore session would have been cancelled. He called the young revolutionaries "deluded patriots". He said that they should be left without support, "for want of nourishment to their violent spirit". In his view, violence would only hurt India's fight for freedom.

The date of Gandhi's article is told differently. One page gives the date of Gandhi's article as 2 January 1929. That is probably a slip. The bomb was in December 1929, and the article answers it.

Gandhi believed in non-violence. That is the heart of his side of the argument. He thought violence would hurt the cause of freedom.

## The answer

The HSRA wrote back. The paper was called "The Philosophy of the Bomb". It was written by Bhagwati Charan Vohra, working with Chandrashekhar Azad. It carried the byline "Kartar Singh". An editor's note says copies were handed out on 26 January 1930, the day the Congress had named for the country's independence pledge.

The paper said many things. These were its main points:

**It is not a choice between violence and non-violence.** The revolutionaries said that both sides used force of some kind. The real question, the paper said, was "whether you will have soul-force plus physical force or soul-force alone."

**Gandhi did not know what the people thought.** The paper said Gandhi's tours and visits with important people did not show what ordinary Indians felt. "We have, and therefore we claim to know what the masses think," it said.

**Non-violence had not worked yet.** The paper asked how many British officials Gandhi had turned into friends of India. It pointed to South Africa, the Bardoli campaign and the promise of "Swaraj within a year". It said these had not brought freedom.

**Terror was a stage, not the goal.** The paper's most quoted line says: "Terrorism is not the complete revolution and the revolution is not complete without terrorism." It argued that it frightened oppressors, gave hope to the oppressed, and showed that a nation wanted to be free.

**Reforms were bribes.** The paper called the Morley-Minto and Montagu reforms bribes. It said they were meant to pull moderate leaders away from the demand for independence.

**A revolution is bigger than a bomb.** The paper said revolution meant more than fighting the government. It meant changing society, ending the power of the rich over the poor, and building a new order.

It ended with a call to young people, workers, peasants and thinkers. It had a defiant line: "A revolutionary is the last person on earth to submit to bullying." Its last words were "LOVE LIVE REVOLUTION."

Terrorism is the use of fear and violence to force a change. It is a word the revolutionaries used for their own methods in this paper. Some historians prefer "revolutionary terrorists" and say the group saw terror only as a means to a revolution.

## Two sides, fairly told

Gandhi's side is easy to state. He said violence would harm the cause, and that the young men were mistaken, however brave they were. 
The HSRA's side is also easy to state. It said that peaceful protest had not yet freed India, that young people wanted to act, and that change had to reach the poor and not only the government.

Each side believed that its road would lead India to freedom. And each wrote down its reasons in public.

## What happened to the men who wrote it

Bhagwati Charan Vohra died on 28 May 1930, at twenty-six, in Lahore. A bomb he was testing on the bank of the river Ravi exploded. Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru were hanged on 23 March 1931. Chandrashekhar Azad died in a gunfight with the police on 27 February 1931.

Gandhi went on to lead the Salt March in March 1930. After the hangings, as the Karachi Congress showed, the argument about Bhagat Singh followed him to his own meeting.

## Why it matters

This argument is not over. People still ask whether a country can be freed without force, and whether bravery on one side of the argument makes the other side wrong.

You do not have to decide today. But you can read both papers, and notice that each was written by a person who loved India.
