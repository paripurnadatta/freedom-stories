In February 1922, Mahatma Gandhi called off the Non-Cooperation Movement. Many young nationalists were left disillusioned. Some of them decided that a different road was needed.

This story is about the group they formed. It was called the Hindustan Republican Association, or the HRA.

## Three men with a plan

In 1923, three men worked on a plan in Allahabad. One was Ram Prasad Bismil, a poet from Shahjahanpur. Another was Sachindra Nath Sanyal. The third was Jadugopal Mukherjee. Bismil drafted the rules of a new party. A set of rules like this is a constitution.

Sanyal was the oldest and most experienced of them. He was born in Banaras on 3 June 1893. As a young man he joined a secret revolutionary society, the Anushilan Samiti, and started a branch in Patna in 1913. He was involved in a conspiracy in 1915 to start a revolt in the British Indian Army. It was found out. He was caught and sentenced to transportation for life. That meant a sentence to the Cellular Jail in the Andaman Islands. After he came out, he wrote a book about his years in prison, *Bandi Jeevan*, which means "A Life of Captivity". It came out in 1922.

Sanyal also argued with Gandhi in print. Between 1920 and 1924 he wrote to *Young India*, the paper that Gandhi edited, and said that Gandhi's slow way was not enough.

<aside class="note"><b>Transportation</b>: a sentence of being sent far away to prison, here to the Andaman Islands in the Bay of Bengal.</aside>

## 3 October 1924

On 3 October 1924, delegates met at Kanpur, then called Cawnpore. Sanyal chaired the meeting. They finished the constitution and gave the party its name: the Hindustan Republican Association.

<aside class="note"><b>Different lists of founders</b>: One page names Bismil, Sanyal and Jogesh Chandra Chatterjee as the founders. Another names Sanyal, Narendra Mohan Sen and Pratul Ganguly as the men who led the founding meeting. A third lists Bismil, Sanyal and Mukherjee for the work on the constitution. The pages agree that Sanyal and Bismil were central.</aside>

The party chose its leaders and its jobs:

- Sanyal became the national organiser.
- Bismil became the head of the arms division, and organiser for Shahjahanpur district.
- Jogesh Chandra Chatterjee took charge of links with the Anushilan Samiti, the secret society of Bengal.

An executive committee was set up to guide branches in the United Provinces, Bengal, Punjab and Bihar. Other young men joined in the next months. One page lists Chandrashekhar Azad as the commander of the "action troops", and Ashfaqulla Khan as a planner and the person who dealt with the money.

## The aim: a republic of states

The party's aim was clear, and it was set down in its constitution. It wanted to end British rule by an organised armed revolt. Then it wanted to set up a "Federal Republic of the United States of India". The right to vote would belong to every adult.

<aside class="note"><b>Republic</b>: a country without a king or queen, where the people choose their rulers. <b>Federal</b>: made up of states that share power with a central government. <b>Universal suffrage</b>: the vote for every adult.</aside>

## The paper on the first day of the year

Sanyal wrote the party's manifesto, a public statement of its ideas. It was called *The Revolutionary*. It was handed out in the big cities of North India on 1 January 1925.

It called for overthrowing British rule. And it went further. It said that the party would end "all systems which make any kind of exploitation of man by man possible." One page lists more of its ideas: public ownership of heavy industries, transport and power; an end to the holding of land by large landlords; and a promise that members would give up religious communalism.

<aside class="note"><b>Exploitation</b>: using other people's work unfairly, for your own gain. <b>Communalism</b>: putting one's own religious group against another.</aside>

So the HRA of 1925 already spoke about more than the British. It spoke about how Indian society itself should be run. Three years later, its members would add a new word to the party's name: socialist.

## A hidden network

The HRA worked in secret. It had small committees in different regions. To pay for its work the party needed money. Its leaders decided to take government money, not the property of private people.

## Kakori, and the breaking of the party

That choice led them to a train. On 9 August 1925, ten members of the HRA boarded the 8-Down passenger train between Saharanpur and Lucknow. Rajendra Lahiri pulled the chain near Kakori station. The men took the government's treasury chest from the guard's van. It held about 4,600 rupees. During the raid a gun went off by accident, and a passenger was killed. You can read about this on this site.

The police hit back hard. Sanyal was caught at Bankura. Jogesh Chandra Chatterjee was taken at Howrah station. In all, more than forty people were arrested. Police found the party's bomb workshop in Calcutta in 1925, and another at Deoghar in 1927. The trial began at Lucknow in May 1926. Four men were hanged in December 1927: Bismil, Ashfaqulla Khan, Roshan Singh and Rajendra Lahiri. Sanyal and another man were sent to the Andamans. Manmathnath Gupta got fourteen years, and others got five to ten.

Azad was the main leader who was never caught. He gathered the survivors. That is how the story of the HRA goes on, into 1928.

## What Sanyal did next

Sanyal was arrested in the Kakori case. He was sentenced to transportation for life, for the second time. He was released in August 1937. He was arrested again in 1941 and was held in Gorakhpur. He died there of tuberculosis on 7 February 1942. He was forty-eight. He had been a guide to younger men. He mentored Bhagat Singh and Chandrashekhar Azad, and Bhagat Singh wrote about Sanyal's beliefs in his essay *Why I Am an Atheist*.

## Why it matters

The HRA did not win. It lasted four years and then had to start again under a new name. But it set down, in writing, the ideas that its members would stand for. They wanted a free India, and also an India where one person did not live off another's work.

People still disagree about the way the HRA chose to fight. But it helps to know what it stood for. Its members had a constitution, a manifesto and a plan.
