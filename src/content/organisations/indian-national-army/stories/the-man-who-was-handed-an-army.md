On the morning of 17 February 1942, thousands of Indian soldiers were told to gather on an open field in Singapore. The field was called Farrer Park. Two days earlier, Singapore had fallen to the Japanese army. The men were prisoners now.

The British officers had been ordered to a different place, Changi.

This is the story of the man who spoke to them, and of what came of his speech.

## A boy from Sialkot

Mohan Singh was born on 3 January 1909, near Sialkot in Punjab. His father, Tara Singh, died two months before he was born. His mother's name was Hukam Kaur.

<aside class="note"><b>Born where?</b> One page says he was born at Badiana, near Sialkot. Another says Ugoke, Sialkot. Both agree on the district and the date.</aside>

In 1927 he joined the 14th Punjab Regiment as a soldier. In 1931 he was picked to train as an officer. He studied at Kitchener College in Nowgong and at the Indian Military Academy in Dehradun. He was given his commission on 1 February 1935.

In December 1940 he married Jasvant Kaur, the sister of another officer. On 4 March 1941 his battalion sailed for Malaya, to defend it against a possible Japanese attack.

## Captured

Japan attacked in December 1941. Mohan Singh's men were beaten at Jitra, in the north of Malaya. He spent several days in the jungle. Then he was captured and taken to Alor Star.

There he met two men. One was Major Iwaichi Fujiwara, a Japanese officer whose office dealt with Indians in Southeast Asia. The other was Giani Pritam Singh, an Indian exile who worked with Fujiwara.

They asked Mohan Singh to raise an army from the Indian soldiers who had been captured. He was not sure. After meeting a Japanese general he came to believe it could be done.

<aside class="note"><b>Prisoner of war</b>: a soldier who is captured by the enemy in a war and held until it ends.</aside>

## Farrer Park

On 11 January 1942, Kuala Lumpur fell with about 3,500 Indian prisoners. On 15 February, Singapore fell. About 85,000 soldiers were taken there, and about 45,000 of them were Indians.

Two days later the Indians stood at Farrer Park. The soldiers were from the 1st and 5th battalions of the 14th Punjab Regiment, and others. A British colonel, Hunt, handed them over to the Japanese. Then Fujiwara spoke. His words were turned into English and then into Hindustani. He spoke of Japan's plan for an independent India and of a "liberation army". He said they would be treated as friends and allies, not as prisoners. Then he handed the men to Mohan Singh.

Mohan Singh spoke next, in Hindustani. He said that an Indian National Army would be formed to fight for India's freedom. He invited the men to join.

One soldier remembered the speech years later. He said it moved the men, and they cheered. By one estimate, nearly half of them later joined the first INA.

<aside class="note"><b>What did Hunt say?</b> Many soldiers remembered Colonel Hunt telling them that they now belonged to the Japanese army and must obey it. Hunt remembered only saying that they were prisoners of the Japanese. Shah Nawaz Khan, who later became an INA officer, said the men were "handed over like cattle". Many INA veterans said this feeling helped to push them to join.</aside>

Many Indian officers chose not to join. Their decision made it harder for their men to join as well.

## The rules of the army

In April 1942 Mohan Singh called his officers together and they agreed on a set of rules, the Bidadari Resolutions. These said that Indians stood above differences of caste, community or religion. They said that independence was every Indian's birthright. They said an Indian National Army would be raised to win it. And they said that the army would go into battle only when the Congress and the people of India asked it to.

Copies went round the camps. Mohan Singh toured them with Fujiwara. Recruiting began on 9 May.

## Bangkok

In June 1942 Indians from many countries met in Bangkok on 22 and 23 June. Their meeting proclaimed the Indian Independence League. Rash Behari Bose, an old revolutionary who had lived in Japan for many years, was to head its Council of Action. Mohan Singh sat on it as the army's representative, and he was made commander of the "Army of Liberation for India".

<aside class="note"><b>The dates and the count</b>: one page gives the conference as 22 June. Another gives 23 June. The page on the conference lists 34 resolutions, while the page on Mohan Singh says 35.</aside>

The resolutions asked a lot of Japan. They asked that Japan say publicly that India was to be free. They asked that the INA be treated as an allied army and not as a tool, and that Indian prisoners be released to it. They said the army should be used only to free India.

On 1 September 1942 the army was formally set up. About 40,000 men were said to have volunteered.

## The quarrel

It did not last. Mohan Singh grew unhappy with the Japanese army's headquarters. He believed they wanted to use his men as part of their own forces, and had not recognised the INA as a separate army.

On 29 December 1942 he was removed from command. Japanese military police took him away.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: one page says Mohan Singh himself dissolved the army, sent the men back to the prison camps, and expected to be shot. Another says he was removed and arrested by the Japanese. Both agree that the first INA ended in December 1942.</aside>

Rash Behari Bose tried to keep the army together. On 15 February 1943, Lieutenant-Colonel M. Z. Kiani was given command. In 1943 Subhas Chandra Bose came from Germany and built the army again. Mohan Singh was not given his command back.

## After the war

When Japan lost the war, the British took Mohan Singh into custody and sent him to India. The Red Fort trials had stirred up public feeling by then. Because of it, he was only cashiered, which meant he was dismissed from the army.

In 1947, as India was divided, Mohan Singh set up the Desh Sewak Sena on 21 October in Amritsar. Its aim was to help refugees, both Sikh and Hindu, and to see Muslim caravans safely out of India. Later he joined the Congress. He was a member of the Punjab assembly from 1967 and served in the Rajya Sabha, the upper house of Parliament. He was re-elected in April 1972.

He died of cancer on 26 December 1989, at Jugiana near Ludhiana. He was eighty.

## Why it matters

Mohan Singh's army was small, short-lived and unrecognised. But it was the first time thousands of Indian soldiers were asked to serve an army of their own, with an Indian aim. Whether they were right to accept is argued about to this day. The day it began is plain: a field in Singapore, a crowd of soldiers, and a captain who asked them to follow him.
