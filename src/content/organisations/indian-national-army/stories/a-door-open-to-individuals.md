When the war ended, thousands of INA men were in camps. By November 1945 about 12,000 were held. What was to become of them?

## Let go

Within three months, about 11,000 of them were let go. They were cashiered, which means they were thrown out of the army, and they lost their pay. Only about ten courts-martial were held at the Red Fort, and they ended in public anger.

## The question after independence

India became free in 1947. What happened to the INA men then?

One account says that Lord Mountbatten, the last Viceroy, advised that former INA soldiers should not join the armed forces of the new India. Nehru agreed to this, it says, and few joined later. A man named R. S. Benegal, one of the young pilots whom Netaji had sent to Japan, joined the Indian Air Force in 1952.

## What Nehru said

On 15 December 1952, in the Council of States in Delhi, S. N. Dwivedy moved a resolution about the INA men. In the debate, the charge was made that they were being treated as traitors and forgotten. Nehru, who was Prime Minister, rejected the charge.

He said that taking them back into the army all together was not workable. The army had been made smaller after the war, and many INA men were older now and out of touch. But, he said, "the door was completely open to them", as individuals and not as a group.

He told the house what had been done:

- Many INA men had found jobs in the Home Guards, the police and the Armed Constabulary. Some held senior police posts, and some were in the diplomatic service.
- A liaison officer in the Defence Ministry helped them find work in the government.
- A committee on INA inquiry and relief, which Nehru himself chaired, still gave money and help with jobs.
- Some got service pensions and gratuities. Disabled men and the families of the dead received lump-sum grants. About 30 lakh rupees was set aside, and 31 lakh was spent.

He also gave a reason for turning some men away. He said that INA soldiers who had gone to Pakistan had fought against India in Kashmir.

## Still asked

The question did not end there. In 2017 a man named Prodyot Kumar Mitra asked the government, under the Right to Information law, what the status of INA men was. An Information Commissioner, Sridhar Acharyulu, ordered the Home Ministry to answer. He said that the ministry should explain why INA members had been denied freedom-fighter status.

<aside class="note"><b>Two views</b>: Nehru's speech says the door was open to individuals. The page on the INA says former INA men were barred from the new armed forces, and that few joined. Both can be true. The army did not take them back as a group, and some came in one by one. The sources do not give a count.</aside>

## Why it matters

Armies and governments have to make hard choices after a war. But there is also a plain human question here. A man puts his life at risk for his country, and the country has to decide how to thank him. The INA men still hold a place in India's memory, and the debate about what they were owed has gone on ever since.
