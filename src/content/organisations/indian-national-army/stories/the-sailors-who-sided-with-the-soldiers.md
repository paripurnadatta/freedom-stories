The war had ended in 1945. Thousands of men of the Indian National Army were prisoners of the British. The British had to decide what to do with them.

They chose to put some of them on trial in the Red Fort in Delhi. The trials became one of the loudest quarrels in the whole freedom struggle. They began in a courtroom and ended with ships in the harbour at Bombay.

## Three men in the Red Fort

The first trial opened on 5 November 1945. There were three officers in the dock. Shah Nawaz Khan was a Muslim, Prem Sahgal a Hindu and Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon a Sikh. All three had been officers of the British Indian Army before they joined the INA.

The charge was serious. They were accused of "waging war against the King-Emperor". Dhillon was also charged with murder. Shah Nawaz Khan and Sahgal were charged with helping to cause a murder. The government's lawyer was Naushirwan P. Engineer. At first about 7,600 INA men were to be tried. In the end there were only about ten courts-martial, because proving the crimes was hard.

<aside class="note"><b>Court-martial</b>: a trial of soldiers by a court of army officers.</aside>

## Who stood up for them

Jawaharlal Nehru said in Poona that the Congress would take responsibility for the trials. The Congress Working Committee set up a defence committee. It included Nehru, Bhulabhai Desai, Asaf Ali, Tej Bahadur Sapru and Kailash Nath Katju. Sapru led a team of about two dozen lawyers.

The support did not come from the Congress alone. Another page says that the Muslim League, the Communist Party, the Akali Dal and the Hindu Mahasabha all took the side of the INA men as well. 

The court found all three men guilty. It sentenced them to be deported for life.

## The streets of Calcutta

While the trial went on, crowds filled the streets. In Calcutta, students led the marches. A slogan was shouted in many cities. It said, "From the Red Fort comes a call: Sahgal, Dhillon, Shah Nawaz."

<aside class="note"><b>Two dates</b>: One page says the first unrest in Calcutta was between 11 and 14 November. A page on one of the students who died gives 21 November for the rally where he was shot. Both may be true: there may have been several days of protest.</aside>

On 21 November 1945, police fired on a student rally in Calcutta. A young man called Rameshwar Banerjee, aged twenty, was killed. So was Abdul Salam. More than sixty people were hurt. The next day, at least 25,000 students walked in a funeral procession two miles long, and the city shut down. Police fired again on 22 and 23 November. By one count, at least fifty people died in those three days.

People in Bombay, Karachi and Lahore held marches too.

## The sentence is lifted

On 1 January 1946 the Commander-in-Chief, Field Marshal Auchinleck, set aside the sentence of deportation for the three officers. They went free. Later trials went on, and they were moved from the Red Fort to a building next to it. The treason charge was dropped in them after the unrest. The officers were cashiered and lost their rank.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: One page says Auchinleck "commuted" the sentences. Another says the public pressure "forced" him to free the officers. The result was the same. The three were not sent away.</aside>

In February 1946 there was another trial. An INA officer, Abdul Rashid, was given a sentence of seven years. Between 11 and 13 February, Calcutta rose again.

## The navy

On 18 February 1946, at a signals school in Bombay called HMIS Talwar, the sailors refused their orders. Most of them were under twenty-five. They said they were angry about their food and about being insulted because they were Indian. The trials of INA men were also on their minds.

By that afternoon, sailors on two more ships, HMIS Sutlej and HMIS Jumna, had joined them. Within a day, the strike had spread to 22 ships and 12 shore stations in Bombay.

On 19 February, the sailors chose a Naval Central Strike Committee. Its president was M. S. Khan. They wrote a list of demands. It asked for the release of political prisoners and INA men. It asked for the punishment of officers who abused sailors. It asked for pay equal to that of the Royal Navy, and for Indian officers.

<aside class="note"><b>How many?</b> The numbers differ. One page says about 1,100 sailors went on strike at the start. Another says 56 ships and shore stations and over 10,000 sailors were involved in all.</aside>

Troops and Royal Marines were sent in. Aircraft flew over the harbour. Shots were fired. In Bombay, a general strike followed. In Karachi, sailors took over the ship HMIS Hindustan on 21 February. It gave up the next morning after being fired on.

## Who did not join

The sailors found few friends among the top leaders. The Congress and the Muslim League both opposed the revolt. They were afraid it would upset a peaceful handing-over of power. Gandhi spoke against it. Aruna Asaf Ali was one of the few well-known figures who stood with the sailors. The Communist Party was the only national party that backed them, and it called a general strike on 22 February.

Vallabhbhai Patel went to Bombay. Muhammad Ali Jinnah also urged the sailors to give up. The sailors were promised that no one would be punished for it. They surrendered between 25 and 26 February. Another account puts the end on 23 February.

## What happened to the sailors

Their promise was not kept. After the surrender there were arrests and courts-martial. 476 sailors were dismissed. None of them was taken back after independence.

Many people died in the unrest of those days, in Bombay and in Karachi. The sources do not agree on how many. The figure given for the whole of the unrest goes up to 236, but that count includes riots between communities.

## Why it matters

Some historians say that the trials, and the revolts that followed, showed the British that they could no longer count on Indian soldiers and sailors. They argue that this hurried the British out of India. In the winter of 1945–46 Indians of every faith stood together for men who had fought against the British, for a few weeks at least.
