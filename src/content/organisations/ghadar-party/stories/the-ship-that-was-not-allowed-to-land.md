In the spring of 1914, a ship called the *Komagata Maru* left Hong Kong with passengers who hoped to start new lives in Canada. Nearly all of them were Punjabis. Most were Sikhs. They were not allowed to step ashore.

This is what happened to them.

## A rule with no name on it

In 1908 Canada made a rule called the "continuous journey" rule. Shipping companies were pressed not to sell through-tickets from India to Canada. So the rule shut the door on Indians without ever naming them.

Another rule asked newcomers from Asia to show a large sum of money, $200.

A court case in 1913 gave some hope. A judge cancelled the deportation of 38 Sikhs from another ship, the *Panama Maru*. But the government made its rules again, and they were back in force by January 1914.

## The man who hired a ship

Gurdit Singh was a rich Sikh businessman who lived in Singapore and Malaya. He decided to challenge the law by hiring a ship himself. In March 1914 he chartered the *Komagata Maru*. He renamed it *Guru Nanak Jahaz*, after the first Sikh Guru.

The ship left Hong Kong on 4 April 1914, after delays that one page blames on the governor, Sir Francis May. It had 165 passengers. It called at Shanghai on 8 April. At Moji, in Japan, 86 more people came aboard on 19 April. At Yokohama 14 more joined. Among those at Yokohama were two leaders of the Ghadar Party, Maulvi Barkatullah and Bhagwan Singh Gyanee.

The ship had 533 bunks, and an earlier count gave its capacity as about 566 people, 550 in steerage and 16 in cabins. The ship sailed from Yokohama on 3 May with 376 passengers: 340 Sikhs, 24 Muslims and 12 Hindus. All of them came from Punjab.

<aside class="note"><b>The count</b>: the numbers differ. The figures for Hong Kong, Moji and Yokohama add up to 265, not 376, and the page does not explain the difference. One page says 376 at the start. Another says the ship carried 150 people at first, and that about 321 reached the Indian port at the end. The pages say that some left the ship in Japan.</aside>

## Vancouver

The ship reached Vancouver on 23 May 1914. Officials did not let it dock. It was kept out in the water for two months. Officials cut off contact with the shore and put off the examinations of the passengers. One page says they hoped the charter would fail for lack of money.

Only 20 returning residents, and a few others, were allowed to land. The pages give the number of people admitted as 22 in one place and 24 in another. The Sikh community in Vancouver formed a Shore Committee. It raised $20,000 to take over the charter of the ship and tried to help the passengers.

The passengers took their case to the court. On 6 July the British Columbia Court of Appeal ruled against them. It said the immigration laws stood.

Food and water ran short on board. On 19 July about 150 armed police tried to board the ship from a tug, the *Sea Lion*. The passengers drove them back with coal, bricks and scrap metal. The Prime Minister, Robert Borden, ordered a warship, HMCS *Rainbow*, into position, with the threat that it could fire on the ship. A minister, Martin Burrell, then worked out a deal. The government supplied the ship with food, and the captain took back control.

The *Komagata Maru* left on 23 July, with a naval escort. Fewer than two dozen of its passengers had been allowed to land.

## No port for them

On the way back, Britain would not let the passengers land at Hong Kong or Singapore. The ship went on to India. It entered the mouth of the Hooghly River on 26 September. It anchored at Budge Budge, near Calcutta, on 29 September.

<aside class="note"><b>Another date</b>: one page says that police fired on the passengers on 27 September. The others give 29 September.</aside>

The government wanted the men on a special train to Punjab. The passengers refused it. They marched towards Calcutta instead, and armed men stopped them. There was gunfire. About twenty of the passengers were killed. Others were hurt or jailed.

<aside class="note"><b>How many died?</b> One page says police fired and about twenty passengers died. Another says soldiers fired. A third says the total was twenty-two. One footnote counts twenty-six dead in all, including two Europeans, two police officers and two local people. The sources do not agree.</aside>

Gurdit Singh got away. Later he gave himself up, after Gandhi urged him to. He was jailed for five years. Of those who had been on the ship, 27 men escaped arrest.

## After the ship left

Back in Vancouver, the weeks after the ship left were tense. An informer, Bela Singh, had been passing information to the officials. On 5 September 1914 he shot two Sikhs inside the gurdwara, among them its president, Bhag Singh Bhikhiwind. He said he had acted in self-defence. On 21 October 1914 an immigration inspector named William Hopkinson was shot dead outside a courtroom. The man who shot him was Mewa Singh. That story is told in another place on this site.

<aside class="note"><b>Informer</b>: a person who secretly tells the police or officials about others.</aside>

Meanwhile the Ghadar Party was by then calling Indians abroad to come home and rise. One page says the Komagata Maru incident made that call stronger.

## Saying sorry

Canada has since said sorry. In May 2008 the province of British Columbia said it was sorry. In August 2008 Prime Minister Stephen Harper apologised at a Sikh festival in Surrey, but many people wanted him to say it in Parliament. On 18 May 2016 Prime Minister Justin Trudeau made an apology in the House of Commons.

In 2021 the city of Vancouver apologised and chose 23 May as a day of remembrance. In 2024 the day was renamed Guru Nanak Jahaz (Komagata Maru) Remembrance Day. A memorial was unveiled at Coal Harbour in Vancouver in 2012. India built a memorial at Budge Budge in 1952, and Jawaharlal Nehru opened it. In 2014 India issued coins to mark the voyage.

## Why it matters

The people on the *Komagata Maru* did nothing violent. They wished to live and work in a country that belonged to the same empire as theirs. A rule that never named them was used to turn them away.
