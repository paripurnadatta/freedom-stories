In 1909 a man of thirty-nine stepped off a ship in Seattle. His name was Sohan Singh Bhakna. He had been at sea for two months. He had a village behind him in Punjab and a wife he had married when he was ten.

He found work as a labourer at a timber mill near the city.

## A village boy from Punjab

Bhakna was born on 4 January 1870 into a Sikh family near Amritsar. He grew up in the village of Bhakna. He learned Punjabi, Urdu and Persian at the village gurdwara and from the Arya Samaj. He finished primary school in 1886.

In 1906 and 1907 he took part in protests in Punjab against a land law. In February 1909 he left for the United States. He reached Seattle on 4 April 1909.

<aside class="note"><b>Gurdwara</b>: a Sikh place of worship. <b>Arya Samaj</b>: a Hindu reform group that ran schools in Punjab.</aside>

He was not the only Punjabi on that coast. Others worked in the mills and on the farms nearby.

## The first association

In 1912, Bhakna was president of a group of Indian workers in Portland, Oregon. It was called the Hindustani Association of the West Coast. In the summer of 1913 the workers met again, and the group took a new name: the Pacific Coast Hindustan Association. Bhakna was chosen as its president.

<aside class="note"><b>Dates and places</b>: One page says the association was formed on 15 July 1913 at a hall in Astoria, Oregon. Another says May 1913 in Astoria. A third says the meeting was at Stockton in the summer. All agree on the year.</aside>

The members agreed to set up a headquarters in San Francisco. It was called the Yugantar Ashram, after a Bengali newspaper. The address was 436 Hill Street. The party worked from that building from 1913 to 1917.

## The scholar

One man made the group famous in the cities. He was Har Dayal, born in Delhi on 14 October 1884. He had studied Sanskrit in Delhi and then won scholarships to Oxford. He moved to the United States in 1911.

Har Dayal worked with trade unions in California. He was secretary of the San Francisco branch of the Industrial Workers of the World, a union. He made friends among the Punjabi Sikh farmers around Stockton.

He became the party's secretary and its main thinker. He also bought a printing press.

<aside class="note"><b>Trade union</b>: a group of workers who join together to ask for better pay and treatment.</aside>

## A young man called Baba Gernal

A student named Kartar Singh Sarabha also came to the ashram. He was born on 24 May 1896 in the village of Sarabha, near Ludhiana. In July 1912 he sailed to San Francisco, when he was sixteen.

<aside class="note"><b>Did he study?</b> Several pages say he went to enrol at the University of California at Berkeley. The university has no record of him. Another note says he was found working in a timber mill in Astoria in December 1912.</aside>

Bhakna inspired him to take up the cause. Bhakna called him "Baba Gernal". Kartar Singh learned to shoot, to make detonators and to fly, from Americans. Then he gave up his studies and helped Har Dayal with the newspaper. He looked after the Gurmukhi edition, wrote articles and composed poems.

## Not only Punjabis

The party was not only Punjabi, and not only Sikh. Vishnu Ganesh Pingle came from near Poona, in Maharashtra. He was born on 2 January 1888. He left for the United States in 1911 and enrolled in mechanical engineering at the University of Washington in 1912. He joined the party there.

Maulvi Barkatullah, a Muslim, was another of its leaders. So was Taraknath Das. In a party of mill workers, Pingle was an engineering student and Har Dayal a Sanskrit scholar. They came from different faiths, languages and parts of India. What they had in common was a shared wish to end British rule.

## The newspaper

The first issue of the paper *Ghadar* appeared on 1 November 1913. It was in Urdu. A Punjabi edition in Gurmukhi followed in December. Later the paper came out in Gujarati, Hindi, Pashto, Bengali and English as well.

The word *ghadar* means revolt or mutiny. The paper's masthead called it "Angrezi Raj ka Dushman", the "Enemy of British Rule". Its readers were told to expect no help from petitions. It said that only an armed uprising could end British rule.

It was a paper for people of every religion. It spoke against caste and against quarrels between communities. A column in each issue, "The Black Balance Sheet of British Rule", listed fourteen complaints against the British government.

The paper cost nothing. Workers in the Pacific north-west paid for it with donations.

## Poems for the work camps

The party also put out booklets of poems and songs. The booklets were called *Ghadar di Guanj*, which means "Echoes of Mutiny". They were read aloud in the workers' camps.

The first copies of the newspaper reached India on 7 December 1913. The British government banned it there at once. It still got in. Sailors and travellers carried thousands of copies into Bombay, Calcutta and Karachi. They tried to put them in the hands of Indian soldiers.

## The scholar leaves

On 16 March 1914 the American immigration authorities arrested Har Dayal. They meant to send him out of the country as an undesirable alien. He jumped bail and fled to Switzerland. He later settled in Berlin. A new secretary took over the work, Ram Chandra.

## Call to go home

In August 1914 the First World War began. The paper printed an editorial called "Declaration of War". It asked Indians living abroad to go home and start a revolt while Britain was busy.

Over the next months, thousands of Indians sailed for India. One estimate says about 8,000 returned between 1914 and 1918. That number is not certain.

## The building today

In 1917 the party moved to a new building at 5 Wood Street in San Francisco. In 1949 the building and its records were handed to the Government of India. It became the Ghadar Memorial, with a museum and a library. It was opened in 1975.

## Why it matters

The people who started the Ghadar Party were not generals or lawyers. They were mill hands, farmers and students, who paid for a press with their donations. They were far from home, and they believed that a free India was worth going back for. What happened when they went is told in the next story.
