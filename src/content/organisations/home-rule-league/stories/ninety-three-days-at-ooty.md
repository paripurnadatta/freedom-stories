By the summer of 1917, Annie Besant's All India Home Rule League was not even a year old. Yet it had branches in all the important towns. It was strongest in the Madras Presidency. Her daily paper, New India, attacked the British government of India and called for self-rule.

The government of Madras had been trying to quiet her for a year. It had not worked.

## First, the money

The first attempt came on 26 May 1916. It used a law about the press. Besant was told to pay Rs 2,000 to the government within fourteen days. The money was a security deposit. If the paper printed what the government called dangerous, the money would be lost.

Besant paid. She kept writing. She printed books, like "India: A Nation". She put out 10,000 leaflets on self-rule. So the government kept the Rs 2,000. It ordered her to pay a fresh deposit of Rs 10,000.

Many people thought this was unfair. Meetings were held to criticise it. People collected money to help her pay.

<aside class="note"><b>Security deposit</b>: money handed over as a promise of good behaviour. The government keeps it if the promise is broken.</aside>

## Then, the students

Students had become the backbone of many of the league's protests. The government ordered students to keep out of politics. Those who did not obey were suspended. In the Madras Presidency, they were told they could come back only if their parents promised good behaviour.

This angered many people too. Meetings were held everywhere against the order.

## 16 June 1917

Then the government tried something stronger. On 16 June 1917 the Governor of Madras, Lord Pentland, ordered Besant's arrest. Two of her closest workers were arrested with her. One was B. P. Wadia, the assistant editor of New India. The other was George Arundale, the league's organising secretary. Both were theosophists, like Besant. Wadia came from Bombay and had moved to Adyar in 1908. Arundale had just helped Besant set up a National University near Adyar, with the poet Rabindranath Tagore as its first chancellor.

They were not sent to a jail. They were sent to a house at Ootacamund, which people call Ooty. It is a hill station, a cool town in the hills. There they had to stay.

<aside class="note"><b>Interned</b>: kept in one place by government order, without a trial. An interned person is not in a prison cell, but cannot leave or carry on with public work.</aside>

At her house in the hills, Besant flew a red and green flag, in defiance of the government. Red and green were the colours of the Home Rule flag.

## The country answers

The government had hoped to make the league quieter. Instead, the arrests became the thing everyone talked about. There were protests all over India.

The Congress and the Muslim League acted together. They warned that they would start protests if she was not set free. Tilak, who led the other Home Rule League, said there would be passive resistance across the country.

<aside class="note"><b>Passive resistance</b>: refusing to obey unjust orders, peacefully, and accepting the punishment.</aside>

Leaders who had kept away from the league now joined it to show support. Madan Mohan Malaviya, Surendranath Banerjea and Muhammad Ali Jinnah were among them. More and more people signed up as members.

In Madras, the league's honorary president was an old judge, Sir S. Subramania Iyer. He set up a fund to help pay the legal costs of the three. On 24 June 1917, eight days after the arrests, he did something bolder. He wrote a letter to the President of the United States, Woodrow Wilson. He asked America to support India's demand for self-government. That letter caused a storm of its own.

## A promise from London

On 20 August 1917, the British government made an announcement. It came from Edwin Montagu, the Secretary of State for India. He was the minister in charge of India in the British government. Britain's policy, he said, was "the progressive realization of responsible government in India".

In plain words, India would be given more and more self-government, step by step. Montagu said the first steps would be taken as early as possible.

<aside class="note"><b>Responsible government</b>: a government that has to answer to an assembly chosen by the people, and can be removed by it.</aside>

## 17 September 1917

Less than a month later, the government gave way. Besant, Wadia and Arundale were let out on 17 September 1917. They had been kept at Ooty for ninety-three days.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The university course on the Home Rule movement (INFLIBNET) gives the exact dates, 16 June to 17 September 1917, and the count of 93 days. Wikipedia's page on Besant says only that she was interned in June and freed in September 1917.</aside>

Crowds welcomed her all over India. There were welcomes in Madras, in Calcutta, in Allahabad and in Bombay. Processions and meetings were held in many towns to celebrate.

The campaign went on. On 10 November 1917 a huge petition, called the Monster Petition, was sent to the Secretary of State in England. One of those who signed it was C. R. Parthasarathi Ayyangar of Chittoor. Petitions like it, with hundreds of thousands of names on them, reached the British.

## President of the Congress

Three months later, in December 1917, the Indian National Congress met in Calcutta. It chose Annie Besant as its president for the year. She was the first woman ever to hold that post.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The Congress party's own page on Besant says she was made president of the Calcutta session "in August 1917", while she was still at Ooty. Wikipedia, the INFLIBNET course and GKToday all say she was chosen after her release, and that she presided in December 1917.</aside>

In her speech at Calcutta, she spoke about India's old village councils, the panchayats. She said they showed that ruling yourself was nothing new for India.

## What came after

Besant now spent more of her time on campaigns for workers. In 1917 she also started the Women's Indian Association. B. P. Wadia, who had been shut away with her at Ooty, helped found the Madras Labour Union on 13 April 1918. It was one of India's first trade unions.

The promise of August 1917 led, in time, to new laws for India. Leaders argued hard about whether those changes were enough. That argument would split the Home Rule movement in the years that followed.

## Why it matters

The government of Madras tried three times to silence one league: with a fine, with an order to students, and with internment. Each time, more people joined the side it was trying to silence.

Ooty was a hill town, far from the crowds of Madras. Yet for ninety-three days in 1917, a house there was where the whole country was looking.
