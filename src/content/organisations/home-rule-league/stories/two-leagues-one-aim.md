In August 1914 a great war broke out in Europe. Britain went to war with Germany, and it asked its whole empire for help. That included India. Many Indian soldiers were sent to fight for the British.

Indian leaders did not agree about the war. Some backed Britain. They hoped India would be rewarded with more freedom when it was over. Others opposed the war. The British government would not talk about any changes in India while the fighting went on.

Two people decided they would not wait.

## The man back from Mandalay

The first was Bal Gangadhar Tilak. He was a writer and a fierce speaker from Poona. The British had kept him in prison for years in Mandalay, in Burma. He came out on 16 June 1914.

Prison had made him gentler. He now said that violence slowed down reform. He wanted to work "strictly by constitutional means", in his own words. That meant using speeches, petitions and the law, never force.

He also wanted to make peace with the Congress. Some years before, the Indian National Congress had split in two at a meeting in Surat. Tilak's side, the bolder one, had been left outside it.

<aside class="note"><b>Constitutional means</b>: lawful ways of asking for change, like meetings, newspapers, petitions and votes.</aside>

## The woman from Adyar

The second was Annie Besant. She was born in London in 1847 and had come to India on 16 November 1893. She led the Theosophical Society, which had its headquarters at Adyar, in Madras. She ran schools and colleges, and she owned two newspapers.

Besant knew the Irish story well. In Ireland, people had long asked for "home rule". They wanted their own parliament for their own affairs, while staying inside the British Empire. Besant had supported them for years.

When the war began, she borrowed an Irish saying. She said: "England's need is India's opportunity."

<aside class="note"><b>Theosophical Society</b>: a group that studied the religions and ideas of the world. Its members are called theosophists.</aside>

## A deadline for the Congress

In 1915, at a meeting she called in Bombay, Besant explained her plan for a Home Rule League. At the Congress meeting in December 1915, she asked the Congress to let Tilak's side come back in. It agreed.

But she could not get the Congress or the Muslim League to start home rule leagues themselves. So she asked the Congress for something smaller. It should explain home rule to ordinary people and wake up its local committees. If nothing happened by September 1916, she warned, she would start her own league.

## April 1916: Tilak's league

Tilak moved first. In April 1916 he started the Indian Home Rule League at the Bombay provincial Congress at Belgaum.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia says only "April 1916" for the start of Tilak's league. A university course on Indian history (INFLIBNET) gives the exact day, 23 April 1916.</aside>

Joseph Baptista became its president. N. C. Kelkar was its secretary. It worked through six main branches.

Its demands were simple to say. It asked for swaraj, which means self-rule. It wanted states drawn by language. It wanted children taught in their own language.

Tilak travelled from village to village to win support. He asked farmers and local people to join. In April 1916 the league had about 1,400 members. By 1917 it had about 32,000.

## September 1916: Besant's league

The Congress did nothing by the deadline. So in September 1916 Besant started the All India Home Rule League, with its headquarters at Adyar.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The Congress party's own page on Besant, and Wikipedia's page on S. Subramania Iyer, give the day as 1 September 1916. Other accounts say only "September 1916".</aside>

Besant was its president. George Arundale, a fellow theosophist, was the organising secretary. C. P. Ramaswamy Iyer was one of the general secretaries. B. P. Wadia looked after the money. An old judge from Madras, Sir S. Subramania Iyer, became its honorary president.

## Splitting the map

Besant had first tried to join the two movements into one. She failed. So they did the next best thing. They divided India between them.

Tilak's league took Maharashtra, though not the city of Bombay. It also took Karnataka and the Central Provinces and Berar. Besant's league took everywhere else, Bombay city included. That way the two leagues did not quarrel over the same ground.

<aside class="note"><b>Central Provinces and Berar</b>: a large British province in the middle of India. Most of it is now in Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra.</aside>

## What they asked for

Neither league asked the British to leave. Besant's league wrote down its aims. India should become a free nation inside the British Empire, under the King. It should get there by lawful means. The league would support the Congress and keep explaining home rule to the people.

In other words, India would run its own affairs, like Canada, Australia and New Zealand. Those countries still had the British King, but they made their own laws.

<aside class="note"><b>Dominion</b>: a country inside the British Empire that governed itself. Canada and Australia were dominions in 1916.</aside>

## A league that never closed

Until then, Indian politics happened mostly at big meetings, now and then. The Home Rule Leagues worked all year round. That was new.

They built local branches. Besant's league had about 200 branches, loosely joined. In the Madras Presidency alone there were 132 by 1917. The branches held meetings and marches. They opened reading rooms and small libraries.

The leagues printed cheap pamphlets in English and in Indian languages. Some titles were "India, a Nation", "Self-Government for India" and simply "Home Rule". Towns held a Home Rule Week. Besant also started a band of young volunteers called the Order of the Sons of India, and an Indian Boy Scout movement.

People joined. In March 1917 Besant's league had about 7,000 members. By December 1917 it had about 27,000. In Allahabad, Jawaharlal Nehru joined. So did Shankarlal Banker in Bombay. So did the lawyer Muhammad Ali Jinnah.

## The Lucknow meeting

At the end of 1916 the Congress met at Lucknow. For the first time since the split at Surat, Tilak's side and the moderates sat in the same Congress again. The Congress and the Muslim League also signed an agreement there, the Lucknow Pact. The two bodies now asked for self-government together.

<aside class="note"><b>Moderates</b>: Congress leaders who wanted slow, careful change. The bolder group around Tilak was often called the extremists.</aside>

## Why it matters

The Home Rule Leagues never became a movement of the whole country's villages. Most members were town people: lawyers, students, teachers, traders and retired officials. Few women joined, even though a woman led one league.

But the leagues taught Indians a new habit. Politics did not have to wait for one big meeting. It could happen every week, in every town, through a branch, a reading room and a pamphlet. Many young people who joined in 1916 would lead the freedom struggle in the years that followed.
