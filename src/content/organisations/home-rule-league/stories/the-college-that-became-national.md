Imagine walking into your classroom and reading these words on the blackboard: "We don't ask for Home Rule, we demand it." That is what students at one college in Madanapalle used to write. They had read the words in Annie Besant's paper, New India.

## A college in Madanapalle

Madanapalle is a town in the Chittoor district. Today it is in Andhra Pradesh. In 1917 it was part of the Madras Presidency.

The Theosophical Society, which Besant led, had opened a college there. Its first principal was C. S. Trilokekar. He had taught before at the Central Hindu College in Banaras, which Besant had also started.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The INFLIBNET history course says the Theosophical College was opened on 19 July 1915 by the Governor of Madras, Lord Pentland. Wikipedia gives 1917 as the year the college was founded.</aside>

## Students in the movement

When the Home Rule League began in 1916, the college became a busy centre of political work. Students joined the meetings and protests.

The government did not like this. It ordered students to stay out of politics. Students who disobeyed were suspended. They could return only if their parents promised they would behave.

At Madanapalle, the government went further. Because of the protests by Besant and the students, the University of Madras cut its ties with the college.

## A new name, a new university

Besant did not close the college. She gave it a new name: Wood National College. Wood was the family name Besant was born with.

On 16 July 1917, the college opened again as the first part of a new National University. Besant was shut away at Ooty at the time. The National University would teach Indian students in Indian ways, under Indian control. The great Bengali poet Rabindranath Tagore agreed to be its chancellor.

George Arundale, a close helper of Besant, was one of the group that set up this university in 1917. It had its centre near Adyar, in Madras. Other colleges joined it, including a women's college in Banaras and a college in Hyderabad in Sindh.

<aside class="note"><b>Chancellor</b>: the head of a university. The chancellor leads big ceremonies and gives the university its standing.</aside>

## A song in the evening

The college kept its link with Tagore. In February 1919 he came to stay there for a few days. On 28 February 1919 he sang a song of his own for the students. It was "Jana Gana Mana".

Margaret Cousins worked at the college, and she knew European music well. Tagore gave her the notes of the tune. She and her students wrote the melody down in Western notation. During that visit, Tagore also translated the song into English. He called it "The Morning Song of India".

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The INFLIBNET course says Margaret Cousins composed the music for the song there, and calls her the principal's wife. Wikipedia says Tagore gave her the notes and she wrote them down, and calls her the vice-principal.</aside>

## Why it matters

Today "Jana Gana Mana" is India's national anthem. Its English translation was first written at a college that had been punished for joining the Home Rule campaign.
