In 1916, two different people set up two different groups. And they gave them the same name: the Home Rule League.

The first one was set up in April, at a Congress meeting in the town of Belgaum, in the south-west. And the man who set it up was Bal Gangadhar Tilak.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: one account says Tilak's league was set up on 23 April 1916, at a Congress meeting in Belgaum. Another says the league was set up in April 1916, without an exact date. The two accounts do not fully agree on the day, so this story keeps the month, and does not add the day.</aside>

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: <i>Home Rule</i> was a word that meant self-government, for India, inside the British empire. It did not mean full independence, from the empire. It meant that India's own people should make India's own laws, and that the empire should keep the emperor on the throne.</aside>

The first league worked in the states of Maharashtra, and in the provinces of Karnataka, and in the region of Berar, in the central part of the country. And it did not work in the rest of India, in the places the second league would reach, in the months after.

Tilak had been walking from village to village, in the south-west, in the years before 1916. And he had been speaking, in the rooms the people of the village kept, about the word that the first league would carry, in the years after.

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: To <i>walk from village to village</i> means to go, on foot, from one small town to the next, and to speak, in the rooms the people of the village kept, about the word that the first league would carry, in the years after.</aside>

The second one was set up in September, in the town of Adyar, in Madras, on the south-east coast. The woman who set it up was Annie Besant, an English thinker who had lived in India for many years. And her league worked in the rest of India, in the places Tilak's league did not reach.

Besant had been living in Adyar, in Madras, in the years before 1916. And she had been printing the league's newspaper, from the small house there, in the years before the war.

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: To <i>print</i> a newspaper means to make its pages, on paper, one sheet at a time, in the machine that the league kept, in the small house, in Adyar, in the years before the war.</aside>

The two leagues did not meet. They did not share a room, or a meeting, or a plan. And they did not argue with each other, in the open.

They had a quiet understanding, between them, about which places each one would work in. Tilak's league would work in the south-west, and in the central region. And Besant's league would work in the rest of the country, in the places the first league did not reach.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: one account says the two leagues worked in the same places, and that they had to draw a line, between the states Tilak's league worked in, and the places Besant's league worked in, to keep the two from stepping on each other's ground. The exact way the two leagues kept their work separate is not settled in every record, so this story keeps the fact in the main text and the uncertainty in the note.</aside>

Tilak's league had a small start. In April 1916, it had about fourteen hundred members. And by 1917, the number had grown to about thirty-two thousand. That was a big jump, in one year, for a small group that had just been set up.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: one account puts Tilak's league's membership at about fourteen hundred in April 1916, and at about thirty-two thousand by 1917. The exact numbers come from one record, and they are not settled in every account, so this story keeps the figures in the main text and the uncertainty in the note.</aside>

The two leagues, in the same year, in the same country, with the same name, and with the same word in it, did not meet. And they did not argue with each other, in the open.

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: A <i>league</i> is a group of people who work together on one idea, in many places, at the same time. The two leagues, in 1916, were two different groups, with the same name, and with the same word in it.</aside>

And that was the quiet thing about them: two names, one word, and two sets of people who did not sit in the same room. They worked in the same country, in the same year, for the same idea.

The two leagues did not last long, as big groups went. And they did not end, in 1917, the way they had started, in 1916.

But for a few years, in the middle of the war, they carried the word Home Rule. They carried it in two different places, in the same country, and in the same year.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: one account says the two leagues' work, in the years just after the First War, faded, as the word Home Rule stopped being the word of the two leagues, and became the word of a bigger movement, in the country, in the years after. The exact year the two leagues' work stopped, in the open, is not settled in every record, so this story keeps the fact in the main text and the uncertainty in the note.</aside>

Tilak's league, in the south-west, did not stop in 1917, the way it had started, in 1916. And Besant's league, in the rest of the country, did not stop in the same year, either.

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: To <i>stop</i>, for a league, means to stop meeting, and to stop printing its pages, and to stop speaking the word Home Rule, in the towns the league had reached, in the years after the two leagues were set up.</aside>

The word Home Rule, in the years after, in the two places, in the one country, was not just the word of the two leagues. It was the word of a bigger work, in the rooms the two leagues had built, in the places they had reached.

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: To <i>carry</i> a word, in a country, means to say it, in the rooms the people of the country kept, in the years after the two leagues were set up, in the same way they had said it, in the years before. The two leagues, in 1916, carried the word Home Rule, in two different places, in the same country, in the same year.</aside>

It was the word of the people who had kept meeting, and kept printing, and kept speaking it, in the towns the league had reached, in the years after the two leagues were set up.

<aside class="note"><b>Word</b>: To <i>keep meeting</i>, for the people who had built the league, means to come together, in the rooms they had built, in the towns the league had reached, in the years after the two leagues were set up, and to keep speaking the word Home Rule, in the same way they had said it, in the years before.</aside>

And that was the word that the two leagues, in the years just after the First War, carried, in two different places, in the same country, in the same year, in the middle of the war.

The exact places the two leagues' work faded, in the years after, are not settled in every record, so this story keeps the fact in the main text and the uncertainty in the note.</aside>
