On 25 January 1914, a new weekly paper came out in Madras. It was called The Commonweal. Its owner was Annie Besant.

The Commonweal wrote about religious freedom, equal rights for citizens, social reform and education. Most of all, it wrote about self-government. It compared India with Canada and Australia, which already ran their own affairs inside the British Empire.

## A daily of her own

In the middle of 1914 she bought a Madras daily paper called the Madras Standard. She gave it a new name: New India. It came out under that name from 14 July 1914.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: GKToday says she bought the Madras Standard in July 1914. The Congress party's page on Besant says June 1914. Both agree she renamed it New India.</aside>

Besant was the editor. New India was fast and bold. It was read by lawyers, students and political workers in the towns. Every day it demanded Home Rule for India at once.

## What the papers said

When the war began in 1914, New India argued that India had earned something. Indians were helping Britain in the war. So India deserved to rule itself.

The papers wanted the two quarrelling sides of the Congress to make peace. They asked for Tilak and his followers to be let back in. In 1916 they reported at length on the Congress meeting at Lucknow. There, the Congress and the Muslim League agreed to work together.

New India also argued that Home Rule would be good for Britain as well as for India.

When Besant started her league in September 1916, the two papers became its voice. They printed its notices, its meeting dates and its pamphlets.

<aside class="note"><b>Editor</b>: the person who decides what goes into a newspaper and often writes its main articles.</aside>

## The price of a page

The government of Madras thought her articles were dangerous. In May 1916 it used a law about the press against New India. Besant had to hand over Rs 2,000 as a security deposit. If the paper broke the government's rules, the money would be lost.

She paid, and kept writing. In August 1916 the government took the Rs 2,000. Then it ordered her to pay Rs 10,000 more.

Besant did not give in. She took the case to the Madras High Court. Then she took it further still, to the Privy Council. The fight became a well-known battle over the freedom of the press.

Meanwhile people collected money to help her pay.

## Why it matters

Besant's league had branches and meetings. But for many people, it lived on paper. New India brought the words Home Rule to readers every morning.

New India stayed a strong voice for Home Rule for fifteen years. The government tried to make the price of speaking too high. Besant kept paying it.
