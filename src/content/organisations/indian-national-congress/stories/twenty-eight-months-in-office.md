For fifty years the Congress had asked the British government for a say in running India. In 1937 it was given a small one. It had to decide whether to take it.

## The vote

A law called the Government of India Act of 1935 gave Indians more power in the provinces. Elections were held in the winter of 1936 and 1937 in eleven provinces. About 30 million people got the right to vote. That was roughly one in six adults. The vote was limited by property: people with more land or money could vote. Some women could vote too, if they were educated or were the wives or widows of men who qualified.

Polling began on 18 January 1937 and ended on 20 February. The Congress won 706 seats. The Muslim League won 111, about a quarter of the seats set aside for Muslims.

## Should we take the jobs?

Winning an election is one thing. Running a government is another. Under the law, a Governor, who was a British officer, could overrule the ministers and control the money.

Congress leaders were divided. Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose were against taking office. Sardar Patel, Rajendra Prasad, C. Rajagopalachari and Bhulabhai Desai were for it. S. Satyamurti worked inside the Congress to win Gandhi round, and Gandhi told the leaders to accept.

The Congress asked the Governors to promise not to interfere day to day. The Governors refused. For a time there were small governments of other parties in some provinces. In Madras, for example, a leader of the Justice Party took the oath on 1 April.

On 22 June the Viceroy, Lord Linlithgow, said that the government was willing to work with the Congress. On 1 July 1937 the Congress Working Committee decided to form governments.

## Eight provinces

By the middle of 1937 the Congress governed in eight of the eleven provinces. In five it had its own majority: Madras (C. Rajagopalachari), the United Provinces (Govind Ballabh Pant), Bihar (Sri Krishna Sinha), the Central Provinces and Orissa. In Bombay (B. G. Kher), the North-West Frontier Province (Dr Khan Sahib) and, from September 1938, Assam (Gopinath Bordoloi) it led a coalition.

## What they did

The new ministers did a number of things:

- They ended the emergency orders that had been used against civil disobedience, and lifted bans on groups and newspapers.
- They released political prisoners in several provinces, and gave back property that had been taken from satyagrahis.
- In Madras, Bombay and the Central Provinces they set up boards to bring down farmers' debts.
- The United Provinces gave tenants hereditary rights in 1939. Bihar cut rents.
- Bombay passed a law in 1938 to make employers and workers go to arbitration before a strike.
- Madras passed a Temple Entry law in 1939.
- At Wardha, in October 1937, a committee under Zakir Hussain drew up a plan for a new kind of schooling, the Wardha scheme.

<aside class="note"><b>Arbitration</b>: when a neutral person or group listens to both sides of a quarrel and helps to settle it.</aside>

Not everyone was happy. The Muslim League said the Congress governments were unfair to Muslims. It set up the Pirpur Committee in 1938 and the Shareef Report followed in 1939 to look into it. On the other side, peasant groups complained that land reforms were slow, especially in Bihar and the United Provinces.

## Leaving office

On 3 September 1939 the Viceroy declared that India was at war alongside Britain. It was the Second World War. He did this without asking the elected members of the central assembly or the provincial ministers.

The Congress asked for two things: a national government at the centre at once, and a promise of independence after the war. On 17 October the Viceroy refused. On 22 October the Congress Working Committee told its ministries to resign.

They resigned between 28 October and 8 November 1939. Their time in office had lasted about 28 months. The Governors took over the administration.

Muhammad Ali Jinnah of the Muslim League called 22 December 1939 a "Day of Deliverance" to mark the end of Congress rule. B. R. Ambedkar's Independent Labour Party joined in.

## Why it matters

For twenty-eight months, Indians had run provinces under the law. They had passed laws, freed prisoners and started schools. The ministries ended over one question: who decides when India goes to war?
