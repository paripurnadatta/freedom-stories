On 1 March 1883, a retired British officer wrote an open letter. He addressed it to the graduates of the University of Calcutta. His name was Allan Octavian Hume.

He asked young Indians to form their own political group. "If only fifty men, good and true, can be found to join as founders, the thing can be established," he wrote. In the same letter he said: "Every nation secures precisely as good a Government as it merits."

Hume was fifty-three. He had lived and worked in India for more than thirty years. He had retired on 1 January 1882.

<aside class="note"><b>Open letter</b>: a letter written to many people at once, and made public, instead of being sent to one person.</aside>

## A union with a Viceroy's nod

Hume approached Indian leaders himself. He met them, wrote to them and tried to link the political groups in different cities.

In 1885 he went to the Viceroy, the British ruler of India. The Viceroy at that time was Lord Dufferin. Hume asked for his approval to start an "Indian National Union". It was to be a body where educated Indians could speak about the country's affairs.

<aside class="note"><b>Different dates</b>: One page says Hume asked the Viceroy in May 1885. Another says he formed the Union in late 1884. A third says a notice went out in March 1885 for a meeting at Poona that December. All agree that the Union came before the first meeting, and that the first meeting was meant for Poona.</aside>

The plan was a meeting in Poona in December 1885. Then something went wrong.

## Cholera in Poona

A sudden outbreak of cholera hit Poona. Cholera is a serious disease that spreads through dirty water.

The organisers moved the meeting to Bombay, just days before it opened. The Bombay Presidency Association took charge of the new plan. It had been started that same year by Badruddin Tyabji, Pherozeshah Mehta and Kashinath Telang.

The meeting was held from 28 to 31 December 1885. The place was the Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College, near Gowalia Tank.

## Who came

Seventy-two delegates came. A delegate is a person who is sent to speak for others. Most of them were lawyers. They came from all over India:

- Bombay Presidency: 38
- Madras Presidency: 21
- North-Western Provinces and Oudh: 7
- Bengal Presidency: 3
- Punjab: 3

No women were there.

From Bombay came Dadabhai Naoroji, Pherozeshah Mehta, Dinshaw Wacha, Kashinath Telang and Badruddin Tyabji. From Madras came G. Subramania Iyer and P. Ananda Charlu. From Bengal came Womesh Chunder Bonnerjee, Narendranath Sen and Manomohan Ghose.

Two Scottish officials were also among the founders: William Wedderburn and Justice John Jardine.

Two famous names from Bengal were missing. Surendranath Banerjea and Anandamohan Bose were in Calcutta. They were leading a different meeting, the second Indian National Conference.

## The first president

The delegates chose Womesh Chunder Bonnerjee as president of the meeting.

Bonnerjee was a lawyer from Calcutta. He had been born there on 29 December 1844. So he turned forty-one in the middle of the meeting. As a young man he had worked as a clerk in a law firm. In 1864 he sailed to England on a scholarship, and in June 1867 he was called to the bar. A barrister is a lawyer who speaks in the high courts.

Hume became the General Secretary, the person who runs the office.

Bonnerjee opened with four aims. He wanted to:

1. Make friends among the workers from different parts of India.
2. Do away with prejudices of race, religion and province.
3. Write down the considered opinions of educated Indians on public matters.
4. Decide how to work for the public good in the next twelve months.

## A new name

The group had come together as the Indian National Union. Dadabhai Naoroji suggested a new name: the Indian National Congress. The delegates agreed.

<aside class="note"><b>Resolution</b>: a plan or demand that a meeting votes on and accepts. When it passes, it speaks for the whole meeting.</aside>

G. Subramania Iyer, the founder of the newspaper *The Hindu*, moved the first resolution. The meeting passed nine in all. In plain words, they asked the British government to:

1. Set up a Royal Commission, a group of experts, to look into how India was governed.
2. Abolish the Council of the Secretary of State for India, which sat in London.
3. Add elected members to the Imperial and provincial councils, and let them discuss the budget and ask questions.
4. Hold the same civil service examinations in India as in Britain, and raise the age limit.
5. Reduce the money spent on the army.
6. Not take over Upper Burma, because its costs fell on Indian taxpayers.
7. Look again at taxes on trade that hurt Indian industries.
8. Let standing committees be formed in the big cities to organise political work.
9. Hold the next session at Calcutta in December 1886.

## Polite, and loyal

Read the list again. Nobody asked for the British to leave. The early Congressmen thought of themselves as loyal subjects. They wanted a bigger part in running India as a member of the Empire.

Their methods were polite as well. They wrote petitions, which are formal requests. They wrote memorandums, which are short notes of their views. They held public meetings and made speeches.

The British government paid little attention to what they asked.

<aside class="note"><b>The "safety valve"</b>: Some writers say the Viceroy wanted a safe place where angry Indians could speak, so that there would be no revolt like the one in 1857. Other historians say the Viceroy did not trust Hume and was cool towards the early Congress. They say Indian leaders used Hume as a friendly face to keep the officials from stopping the group at the start. The two views are still argued over.</aside>

Not everything went well that year either. In October 1885 Hume and his Indian friends had published an appeal to voters in Britain. It asked them to back candidates who were friendly to India in the general election. The appeal did not work. A historian later called the result "a rude shock", but also a "true realization that they had to fight their battles alone".

## What came next

The Congress met again in Calcutta in December 1886. That time Dadabhai Naoroji was the president. The Congress would meet once a year after that, in a new city each time.

Hume stayed on as General Secretary. He tried later to bring farmers and townspeople into the Congress, and the British officials did not like it. That is another story.

## Why it matters

In 1885 the Congress was small. It had 72 delegates, mostly lawyers, and it spoke in a polite voice. In its first twenty years, it was more a stage for educated, ambitious men than a party of the masses.

But the idea was new. For the first time, people from Bombay, Madras, Calcutta, Lahore and the northern provinces sat in one room and asked for the same things. They had a name, a president, a secretary and nine demands. And they had agreed to meet again the next year.
