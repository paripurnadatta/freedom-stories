In 1857, a young British magistrate named Allan Octavian Hume was working at Etawah, in what is now Uttar Pradesh. The great uprising began. Hume took shelter in the fort at Agra and stayed there for six months.

<aside class="note"><b>Magistrate</b>: an officer who runs a district and judges small cases.</aside>

<aside class="note"><b>Born where?</b> One page says Hume was born on 4 June 1829 in Westminster, London. Another says 6 June 1829 in Montrose, Scotland. Both give the year 1829.</aside>

## Etawah

Hume had come to India in 1849. He joined the Bengal Civil Service the next year and was posted at Etawah. His father, Joseph Hume, was a Member of Parliament.

In January 1858 he returned to Etawah. He raised a force of 650 Indian soldiers who had stayed loyal. He followed a policy of "mercy and forbearance". Only seven people were executed on his orders. The district was calm again within a year, and he was made a Companion of the Bath in 1860.

He also cared about schools. In 1859 he and Koour Lutchman Singh began a Hindi paper called *Lokmitra*. By one count he opened 181 schools, with 5,186 pupils. Two of them were girls. A market in Etawah, Humeganj, is still named after him.

## A career, and a dismissal

He became Commissioner of Customs in 1867 and Director-General of Agriculture in 1870. In 1871 Lord Mayo made him Secretary of the Department of Revenue, Agriculture and Commerce. In 1879 Lord Lytton's government removed him from the Secretariat and moved him to the Board of Revenue. He retired on 1 January 1882.

## The bird man

Hume was also one of the best-known bird experts in India. In 1872 he started a magazine called *Stray Feathers*. He gathered a huge collection at his house, Rothney Castle, in Simla.

In 1883 a servant sold a pile of his manuscripts as waste paper, and much of his work was lost. Hume gave up the study of birds and offered his collection to the British Museum. In 1885, 82,000 specimens were shipped to London, and 75,577 of them were kept. He is called the "Father of Indian Ornithology".

<aside class="note"><b>Ornithology</b>: the study of birds. <b>Specimen</b>: a sample, here a dead bird or its skin, kept for study.</aside>

## The Congress

The same year, 1883, Hume wrote the open letter to the graduates of Calcutta that led to the Congress. In 1885, at the first meeting in Bombay, he was the General Secretary.

Things did not stay easy. The Viceroy, Lord Dufferin, was at first friendly. In private letters he later called Hume an "idiot", an "arch-impostor" and a "mischievous busy-body". In 1888 he described the Congress as a "microscopic minority" of the Indian people.

In 1886 and 1887 Hume tried to bring farmers, townspeople and Muslims into the Congress. Many British officials did not like this. He was upset when the Congress opposed raising the age of marriage for girls. In 1892 he warned that a violent revolt by farmers might come. That frightened Congress leaders and the British officials alike.

<aside class="note"><b>The "safety valve"</b>: Some writers say Hume started the Congress to give angry Indians a safe outlet. Another page says the idea rests on seven volumes of secret reports that never existed, and that William Wedderburn invented them to show Hume as a loyal Briton. Historians also point out that Dufferin did not trust Hume.</aside>

## The last years

Hume's wife, Mary, died on 30 March 1890. He left India in 1894 and settled at Upper Norwood in south London. Near the end of his life he founded the South London Botanical Institute.

He died on 31 July 1912, aged 83. In Etawah the bazaar was shut when the news came. The Congress session that year, at Bankipur, recorded its "profound sorrow". In 1973 India issued a stamp with his portrait.

## Why it matters

Hume served the government for more than thirty years. He then spent his later years asking that government to listen to Indians. Some British officials called him a busybody. Historians still argue about why he did it.
