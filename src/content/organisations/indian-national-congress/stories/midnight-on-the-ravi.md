On the last night of 1929, a crowd gathered by the river Ravi in Lahore. It was close to midnight. A man was about to raise a flag.

The man was Jawaharlal Nehru. He was forty years old, and he was the president of the Indian National Congress. How had a young man come to stand there, and what was he about to promise?

## The road to Lahore

Two years before, in 1927, the British had set up a group to study how India should be governed. This was the Simon Commission. Every member was European. No Indian had a seat on it. Its arrival caused angry protests across the country. In Lahore, the police beat protesters, and Lala Lajpat Rai died after a lathi charge.

The Congress answered by forming its own committee. Motilal Nehru led it, and it wrote the Nehru Report. The Report asked for dominion status. That meant India would govern itself but stay within the British Empire. Many in the Congress were not happy with that. Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose wanted a complete break.

Through 1928 and 1929 the Congress asked for dominion status and received only vague promises. By the end of 1929, its patience was gone.

<aside class="note"><b>Dominion status</b>: a country with its own government that still kept the British king as its head, as Canada and Australia did then. <b>Lathi</b>: a long wooden stick used by the police.</aside>

## A young president

The 1929 session was held in Lahore. The Congress chose Jawaharlal Nehru as its president, and Mahatma Gandhi supported the choice. Nehru was the son of the president of the year before, Motilal Nehru.

The session opened on 29 December 1929. The meeting hall was Bradlaugh Hall, on Rattigan Road, behind the Central Model School.

One account tells of a white horse. A writer in Dawn says Nehru rode through Anarkali Bazaar in Lahore on a white horse on New Year's afternoon. Other accounts say the session opened on 29 December. Dawn's date does not match them, so the timing of the ride is unclear.

In his speech Nehru asked Indians to give up any "lingering loyalty" to the British Empire. In his autobiography, he wrote about the welcome he got. It was "tremendous in volume and intensity," he said. And he reminded himself that the crowds were cheering an idea, a flag and a national demand, not only him.

## The vote

On 31 December 1929 the Congress voted on its main resolution. It was passed almost without a vote against it. The resolution said that Swaraj, in the Congress's plan, now meant "complete independence". The old idea of dominion status was over.

The resolution also did several other things. It said that the Nehru Report was no longer to be followed. It turned down any Round Table Conference with the British, which was a meeting to talk about India's future. It asked Congress members of the councils to resign their seats. And it allowed the Congress to start civil disobedience, a peaceful refusal to obey unjust laws. Preparations for it included the idea of refusing to pay taxes.

Not everybody in the hall liked this. Two leaders, Srinivasa Iyengar and Subhas Bose, walked out and formed their own group, the Congress Democratic Party. In support of the resolution, 172 Indian members of the central and provincial councils resigned.

## The flag on the Ravi

That night, as the old year ended, Nehru led a procession to the river. Thousands of people were there. At midnight he raised the tricolour flag on the bank of the Ravi.

A pledge of independence was read aloud. It included a promise to be ready to withhold taxes. The crowd was asked to agree, and most people raised their hands. A writer in Dawn says that "leader after leader stepped in the waters" of the river. He also gives words that he says were spoken there: "On the banks of this sacred river we pledge, as did our ancestors thousands of years earlier, to remain free."

People shouted slogans, and many wept. One writer says Nehru told the crowd that the flag "must not be lowered so long as a single Indian lives." In the hall, the declaration itself was passed by a show of hands.

<aside class="note"><b>Two dates</b>: Some pages say 31 December 1929 and others say New Year's night. Both point to the midnight between the two years.</aside>

## The declaration

On 1 January 1930 the Congress approved a Declaration of Independence. It was a short paper, about 750 words. It said that India "must sever the British connection and attain Purna Swaraj or complete independence." It called the rule of the British "a crime against man and God". And it said that the struggle would be non-violent.

Who wrote the Declaration? Some pages credit Gandhi with the Declaration, and some credit Nehru. The sources do not settle it.

The Congress chose a date for the whole country to take the pledge: 26 January 1930. On that day people were asked to gather in their own towns and villages. They were to listen to the declaration being read, and show that they agreed by raising their hands.

## What came next

About six weeks later, on 12 March 1930, Gandhi began the Salt March. It was the first big step in the Civil Disobedience Movement.

When India chose a date for its Constitution to take effect, in 1950, it chose 26 January. The choice drew on the anniversary of the Lahore pledge.

## Why it matters

Until Lahore, the Congress had asked for a place inside the British Empire. After Lahore, it asked for the Empire to leave. The goal was now clear, and it was written down.

The dream was still seventeen years away. But when people raised their hands on 26 January 1930, in thousands of places, they were doing something new. They were saying together that India should be free.
