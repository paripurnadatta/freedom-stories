Think of a boy of sixteen, sent far from home to study. In 1860 that is what Badruddin Tyabji was. He had left Bombay for a school in London.

His father, Mullah Tyab Ali Bhai Mian, had sent all seven of his sons to Europe to study. Badruddin's elder brother Camruddin was the first Indian lawyer admitted to practise as a solicitor in England and Wales. Camruddin's example made Badruddin want to join the Bar at fifteen.

## A lawyer in Bombay

Badruddin was born in Bombay on 10 October 1844. He went to University College London and joined the Middle Temple, one of the groups where English barristers trained. His eyesight began to fail, and he went home in 1864. He went back to London the next year, and in April 1867 he was called to the Bar.

<aside class="note"><b>Called to the Bar</b>: allowed to work as a barrister, a lawyer who speaks for clients in the high courts.</aside>

In December 1867 he returned to Bombay. He became the first Indian barrister of the Bombay High Court. In 1871 he led a campaign for an elected city council, and he topped the list of those elected. He, Pherozeshah Mehta and Kashinath Telang became known as "The Triumvirate" and "The Three Stars" of Bombay.

In 1885 he helped to form the Bombay Presidency Association. It hosted the first Indian National Congress, in Bombay at the end of that year. Badruddin and Camruddin both went as delegates.

## "We are not boycotting"

Some people said that Muslims were staying away from the Congress. Badruddin said this was not so. He said he had given up all communal and sectarian prejudice. By "communal" he meant thinking and acting only for one's own religious community.

He missed the second session, in 1886, because of ill health. In December 1887 the Congress met for the third time, in Madras. This time the delegates chose Badruddin Tyabji as their president. The vote was unanimous.

He was the third president of the Congress and the first Muslim. In his address he said he spoke for the Anjuman-i-Islam of Bombay, a Muslim society that he and his brother had helped to start. He also gave this advice to the Congress: "Be moderate in your demands, be just in your criticism, be accurate in your facts, be logical in your conclusions."

At Madras, Badruddin set a rule. The Congress would raise only questions on which Hindus and Muslims agreed. It would leave out local questions. And it would drop any matter that the Muslim delegates opposed, either all of them or nearly all.

His committees accepted this rule.

## The leader who said no

Not everyone agreed. In 1888 a leading Muslim thinker, Sir Syed Ahmed Khan, spoke against the Congress. Sir Syed was the leading critic among Muslim figures.

<aside class="note"><b>The letters</b>: Both men put their views in letters to the editor of a newspaper, the *Pioneer*, in 1888. They were later printed together in a book called *The Present State of Indian Politics*. The words below are from those letters.</aside>

Badruddin wrote first. He explained his rule. He said the Congress asked only for things that were good for everyone. He invited Muslims to join and to "come and discuss them at the Congress". If they disliked a proposal, he said, they could oppose it from inside.

Sir Syed answered, and he gave his reasons. They were these:

- Few of the Muslims at the Congress had been chosen by Muslims. So it was wrong to call them "delegates".
- A Congress was not truly "national" unless the aims of all its people were "identical". In his view, Hindu and Muslim aims were not.
- "The questions on which we can agree are purely social," he wrote. On politics, they did not agree.
- If councils were elected, he said, Hindus would win far more seats, and Muslims would be left in a weak position.
- Politics was dangerous for Muslims. He pointed to 1857, and said that Muslims had suffered the most in that year.

His conclusion was strong. Muslims "should take part in no political Congress". Taking part, he said, "would be for us a curse".

<aside class="note"><b>A translation problem</b>: In these letters the English paper often wrote "nation" for the Urdu word *qaum*, which can also mean "community". So Sir Syed's "our nation" may mean "our community".</aside>

## Allahabad, 1888

The argument continued. At the Congress of 1888 in Allahabad, Badruddin moved Resolution XIII. It said that the Congress would not discuss any subject that Hindu or Muslim delegates, as a body, objected to. The only exception was a subject on which the Congress had already given its view.

This was a way to keep the Congress to matters that both communities accepted.

The two men did not agree. The Congress's own page credits Badruddin with giving the party a national character by bringing Muslims into it. Sir Syed's letters show that he feared Muslims would be outvoted. Both of them were thinking about the future of Muslims in India.

## The judge

In June 1895 Badruddin became a judge of the Bombay High Court. He was the first Muslim to hold that post, and the third Indian. In 1902 he became the first Indian Chief Justice of the High Court of Bombay.

He was known for being fair. When Bal Gangadhar Tilak asked for bail, three other judges refused. Badruddin granted it. He also would not allow anyone to speak with contempt of the Congress in his court. He said that to be president of the Congress was "the highest honour".

Outside court, he fought against purdah, the custom of keeping women out of public view. His family was the first Muslim family to give it up. His daughters went to school in Bombay. In 1904 two of them went to a boarding school in Haslemere, in England. He and his wife, Rahat-un-Nafs, had eighteen children.

He supported the Age of Consent Bill in 1891, even though some Hindus and some Muslims were against it.

## The end

Badruddin Tyabji died in London in August 1906, of a sudden heart attack, while on leave. He was sixty-one.

<aside class="note"><b>The date</b>: One page gives two dates, 19 August and 26 August 1906.</aside>

Mahatma Gandhi wrote that for years Tyabji was "a decisive factor in the deliberations of the Congress".

## Why it matters

In 1887 a Muslim was chosen to lead a mostly Hindu party. In 1888 a respected Muslim leader told Muslims to stay out of it. Both are part of the same story. Even then, people in India disagreed about how Hindus and Muslims could work together in politics.

The two men wrote down their reasons, politely and in full. That is worth remembering when we read about the quarrels that came later.
