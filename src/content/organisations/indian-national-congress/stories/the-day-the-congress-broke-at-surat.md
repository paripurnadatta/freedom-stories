Surat is a city on the banks of the river Tapti, in Gujarat. In the last week of December 1907, the Indian National Congress held its 23rd yearly meeting there. It ended in shouting, flying shoes and a police order to clear the hall.

To see why, we have to go back two years.

## Two ways to ask

The British had divided Bengal into two parts, and the move had made many people restless. In 1906, at Calcutta, the Congress passed four resolutions: for Swaraj, for Swadeshi, for boycott and for national education. Swaraj means self-rule. Swadeshi means using things made in India. Boycott means refusing to buy or use something as a protest.

Not everyone in the Congress liked these resolutions. Two groups had grown inside the party.

The group called Extremists, or Nationalists, included Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Lala Lajpat Rai, Bipin Chandra Pal and Aurobindo Ghosh. They wanted the boycott to cover more than foreign cloth. They wanted it to cover government schools, courts and jobs as well. They wanted full Swaraj, and they wanted the four Calcutta resolutions kept in full.

The group called Moderates included Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Pherozeshah Mehta and Surendranath Banerjea. They wanted the boycott limited to Bengal and to foreign goods. They believed in constitutional methods, such as petitions and legal reform. They were ready to change or drop the Calcutta resolutions.

The split had been showing for two years. At the Banaras meeting in 1905, Tilak and his followers held a separate conference of their own. But they agreed to go on working inside the Congress.

<aside class="note"><b>Moderate and Extremist</b>: These were the names used at the time. The two groups differed over how bold the demands should be, and over what to boycott.</aside>

## A new city, a new president

The meeting was to be held at Nagpur, which was friendly ground for the Extremists. It was moved to Surat, in the Bombay Presidency.

There are two stories about why. One page says Gokhale moved it because he feared that Tilak would win at Nagpur. Another says that, under the Congress rules, a leader from the host province could not be president. Surat was in Tilak's home province, so he could not stand. Both statements appear in print, and they do not fit together neatly.

The Extremists wanted Lala Lajpat Rai as president. He declined, to avoid an open fight. The Moderates put forward Dr Rash Behari Ghosh. The Extremists asked for a firm promise that the Calcutta resolutions would be kept. The Moderates would not give it before the session.

Before the main meeting, the Extremists held a conference of their own on 24 December. Aurobindo Ghosh was its president. Over a thousand people came. Each paid one rupee to enter and signed a pledge.

## 26 December

The main session opened in Surat on 26 December. Ambalal Desai stood and proposed Dr Ghosh as president. Surendranath Banerjea seconded him.

An English journalist, Henry Nevinson, was in the hall and wrote what he saw. Shouting broke out at once. The chairman of the Reception Committee, Malvi, rang his bell, but nobody heard it. Banerjea climbed on a table to speak. The chairman then suspended the sitting for the day.

## 27 December

The next day Malvi called Banerjea to speak, and then declared that Ghosh was elected. There was a lot of shouting.

Tilak went up to the front of the platform. He wanted to move an amendment about the election. Nevinson heard the exchange:

- Malvi: "You cannot move an adjournment of the Congress."
- Ghosh: "I declare you out of order!"
- Tilak: "You have not been elected."

Tilak then appealed to the delegates. Nevinson saw some Moderates trying to pull Tilak from the platform. Dr Ghosh stood on a table. Gokhale stood between Tilak and the others, to shield him.

Then a shoe flew. Nevinson called it a "Mahratta shoe". It struck Surendranath Banerjea on the cheek and then hit Sir Pherozeshah Mehta. Young men from Maharashtra rushed the platform. The meeting broke up in a fight. Nevinson wrote that the Congress was "dissolving in chaos".

Chairs were thrown or raised, and sticks were waved. The police cleared the hall. The session was suspended, and no business had been done.

<aside class="note"><b>Who did what?</b> The accounts are not alike. One page says that some Moderates sent men to attack Tilak and his friends, and that Extremists answered by throwing eggs and shoes. Another says Gujarati volunteers lifted chairs over Tilak's head. The only eyewitness report here, Nevinson's, tells about the shoe and the rush on the platform. It does not say who threw the shoe.</aside>

## The days after

On 28 December the Extremists met again, and Aurobindo took the chair. Tilak spoke there about the "new spirit" that the Congress needed.

The Moderates stopped the Congress and planned a conference of their own. Lala Lajpat Rai warned Tilak that the government would crack down hard if the Congress split. Tilak thought of joining the Moderates' conference. Aurobindo was against it. He expected that the Moderates would shut the Nationalists out.

In 1908 the Moderates held a convention at Allahabad. They wrote a new set of rules for the Congress. It said that the party must use only constitutional methods. That kept the Extremists out.

The government came down hard on the Extremists. Newspapers that supported them were closed. Tilak was sent to prison at Mandalay, in what is now Myanmar, for six years.

The Congress lost strength. Fewer people came to its yearly meetings. The movement slowed down.

## Together again

The two groups came together again at Lucknow in 1916, under the presidency of Ambica Charan Mazumdar. The party passed a plan for reforms, agreed with the Muslim League, and sent it to the Viceroy.

## Why it matters

At Surat the Congress learned something hard. People who want the same thing, freedom for their country, can still fight bitterly over the way to get it.

The Moderates and the Extremists both loved their country. One side believed in patience and the law. The other believed in bolder steps. The quarrel cost the Congress nine years. When the two groups sat in one hall again, in 1916, they passed a plan for reforms together.
