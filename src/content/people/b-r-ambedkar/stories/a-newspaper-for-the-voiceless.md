If nobody listens to you, what can you do? Ambedkar's answer, in 1920, was to start a newspaper.

He was a professor then, in his late twenties. The year 1920 was busy for him.

## Mooknayak

In 1920 he started a weekly paper in Marathi. It was called *Mooknayak*. Pritchett's timeline translates this as "Leader of the Voiceless". Wikipedia says "Leader of the Silent".

He had help from the Maharaja of Kolhapur. Both sources say so.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Pritchett calls the Maharaja "Shahu I". Wikipedia calls him "Shahu IV". We use only "the Maharaja of Kolhapur".</aside>

The timeline quotes the biographer Keer on the first issue. In it, Ambedkar called India a "home of inequality". He described Hindu society as "a tower which had several storeys without a ladder or an entrance". Then he added: "One was to die in the storey in which one was born."

He wrote that people called "Depressed Classes" had to be saved from "perpetual slavery, poverty, and ignorance".

<aside class="note"><b>Depressed Classes</b>: the name used in those days for groups treated as "untouchable".</aside>

## A king at the table

In March 1920 there was a big meeting at Mangaon, in Kolhapur State. It was a conference of the Depressed Classes. The Maharaja came.

He praised Ambedkar in public. He said Ambedkar would one day be a national leader.

At the end, the Maharaja and the people with him did something surprising. They sat down and ate with Ambedkar and the people of his caste. Keer says this shocked the people who followed old customs.

## Dinner at Nagpur

In May the Maharaja called another such conference, in Nagpur.

In the Central Provinces, the Mahar community had eighteen sub-castes. When the meeting ended, Ambedkar invited their leaders to dinner. They all came.

This sounds small. Keer notes that it took "great persuasion". And he adds that Ambedkar could get only the Mahar sub-castes to eat together. Not all the untouchable communities would sit at one table yet.

## Back to London

In July, Ambedkar resigned from his teaching job and left for London again. He wanted to finish his research.

He paid with his own savings. He also had loans from his friend Naval Bhathena and from the Maharaja of Kolhapur.

He studied again at the London School of Economics and at Gray's Inn. You can read the story of his thesis in *Lost at sea, written twice*.

## Why it matters

Ambedkar began his public work by giving people a way to speak. A newspaper, a conference and a dinner were not battles. But each one was a first step.

Notice, too, how many people helped. A king gave help and a place at his table. A friend lent money. The work began with a few people and a small paper.
