Imagine preparing a speech for weeks. You arrive at the hall. And the organisers say: please do not give it.

That happened to Dr Ambedkar in 1936. He answered by putting the speech in a book.

## The invitation

In December 1935, a group in Lahore called the Jat-Pat-Todak Mandal invited Ambedkar. Its name means roughly "the group that breaks caste". It asked him to lead its yearly conference in the spring of 1936.

The group's secretary, Sant Ram, wrote to him. In the book's opening pages, Ambedkar prints a line from this letter: "Our Executive Committee persists in having you as our President for our Annual Conference."

<aside class="note"><b>Presidential address</b>: the main speech given by the person who leads a meeting.</aside>

## The cancellation

In late April 1936, Frances Pritchett writes, the Mandal "realized the radical nature" of the speech it was about to hear.

The Mandal's letter, which Ambedkar prints, said the address had "unnecessarily attacked the morality and reasonableness of the Vedas." It also said that the last part "deals with the complete annihilation of Hindu religion."

And it said that a "hint about your intention to leave the Hindu fold does not seem to me to be relevant."

Ambedkar wrote that the Mandal's Reception Committee "has now cancelled the Conference." He added, "I am sorry that it has ended in a tragedy."

<aside class="note"><b>Vedas</b>: very old sacred books of Hinduism.</aside>

## The book

On 15 May 1936, Ambedkar published the speech he would have given. It was called *The Annihilation of Caste*. Pritchett calls it a slim book that became famous at once.

We do not know how many copies were printed or who paid. The two pages we read do not say.

## Some lines from it

A few short lines from the speech are easy to follow.

- "Caste is the monster that crosses your path."
- "Hindu Society as such does not exist."
- "One Hindu does not regard another Hindu as his Bhai." (Bhai means brother.)

He also wrote that each caste "not only dines among itself and marries among itself". He was describing a society divided into many closed groups.

We have read only part of the long text, so these lines show just a little of it.

## Months before

On 13 October 1935, Ambedkar had led a conference at Yeola. Pritchett's page gives one line from it: "I solemnly assure you that I will not die as a Hindu."

The Mandal's letter mentions a "hint about your intention to leave the Hindu fold". So the two are linked in its own words.

## Two sides

We have only the Mandal's objections as Ambedkar printed them. We have not read the Mandal's full reply, or its own account of why it cancelled.

## Why it matters

A group that had "caste" in its very name cancelled a speech about ending it. That shows how hard the subject was.

Ambedkar did not stay silent. If a hall is closed to a speech, a book can still be opened.
