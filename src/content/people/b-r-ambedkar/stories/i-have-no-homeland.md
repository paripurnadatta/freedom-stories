Two men sat in a house on a hill in Bombay. One had millions of followers. The other had been asked to come, and was not sure he should trust the first.

The house was called Manibhavan. It stood on Malabar Hill. The date was 14 August 1931.

## Why they met

Gandhi was about to go to London. He was to attend the Second Round Table Conference. This was a meeting where Indian leaders and British officials talked about how India should be governed.

Gandhi was the only person sent by the Indian National Congress. Dr Ambedkar was also invited, to speak for the people then called "Untouchables". Today we say Dalits. The old word is cruel, and it is used here only because the books of that time used it.

Ambedkar had been invited to the conference series in 1930. The writer Frances Pritchett says he left for London in October of that year. He took part in all three conferences.

<aside class="note"><b>Round Table Conference</b>: a series of three meetings held in London from 1930 to 1932. The British government invited Indian leaders to talk about India's future laws.</aside>

## The meeting

We do not have a word-for-word record of the talk. Frances Pritchett's page prints an account from a biography of Ambedkar by Dhananjay Keer. She says the text is slightly edited. So the words below are close, but not certain.

Gandhi began by asking Ambedkar what his complaints were. He spoke of his own long work against untouchability. He also spoke of the money the Congress had spent on the uplift of the Untouchables.

Ambedkar answered plainly. In this account, he said the Congress had only given the matter formal recognition. He said the money had been wasted. He said the Congress should have made the end of untouchability a condition of membership. He said it cared more for strength than for principles.

He also said that Hindus had not changed their attitude. So the Untouchables, he said, should rely on self-help and self-respect, and not on leaders and Mahatmas.

## "I have no homeland"

Gandhi then said, "I know you are a patriot of sterling worth."

Ambedkar's reply is the line people remember: "Gandhiji, I have no homeland." In the account, he says he cannot call a society his own when it treats his people so badly.

<aside class="note"><b>Separate electorates</b>: a plan under which a group votes only for its own candidates. Some leaders wanted this for the Untouchables. Gandhi feared it would split Hindu society for good.</aside>

## The question of separate seats

Ambedkar pointed out that the Round Table Conference was discussing safeguards for Muslims, Sikhs and the "Depressed Classes". He asked what Gandhi thought.

Gandhi said, "I am against the political separation of the Untouchables from the Hindus. That would be absolutely suicidal."

Ambedkar thanked him for his frankness and left. He now knew where Gandhi stood.

## In London

The Second Round Table Conference met from 7 September to 1 December 1931. The Wikipedia page says Gandhi spoke for the Congress alone.

On 8 October, Gandhi told the conference that talks outside the room, between the groups, had failed. They had tried to agree on how seats should be shared.

On 13 November, a committee broke apart. Muslim, Dalit and some other members asked for separate electorates. Hindu and Sikh members said no.

On 18 November, the Prime Minister, Ramsay MacDonald, sent in a report. It said, with regret, that no agreement had been reached. The conference closed on 1 December.

## What they each believed

Gandhi believed that separate voting would cut the Untouchables off from other Hindus. He wanted the whole society to change its heart and to give up untouchability.

Ambedkar believed that without their own political power, the Untouchables would stay at the mercy of others. He did not think kindness alone would be enough.

Each of them wanted to lift up the poor and the shut-out. They disagreed about the road.

## What came next

The British government did not wait for agreement. It issued its own decision in August 1932. The story of what followed is told in other stories on this site, including Gandhi's fast in Yerwada jail.

## Why it matters

"I have no homeland" is a hard sentence. It shows that for Ambedkar, freedom from the British was not the whole question. He also asked: free for whom?

A country belongs to people only when it treats them as its own.
