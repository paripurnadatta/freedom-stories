In July 1913, a young man of 22 arrived in New York.

His name was Bhimrao Ambedkar. The Maharaja of Baroda had given him a scholarship to study at Columbia University. It was £11.50 a month, for three years.

## Looking for a room

According to the biographer Keer, quoted on Frances Pritchett's timeline, Bhimrao arrived in the third week of July. He was first given a room in Hartley Hall. He did not like the food and stayed only a week.

In August he moved to a house run by Indian students. In the end, he took a room in Livingston Hall. He shared it with a friend, Naval Bhathena. Naval was a Parsi, and the two stayed friends for life.

Four years later, Bhimrao would be thrown out of a Parsi inn in Baroda. We tell that story in *Eleven days in Baroda*.

## "In dead earnest"

Pritchett's timeline says that, in later years, Bhimrao told his biographer about his early months. At first he enjoyed the social side of college life. Then, one night, he made a firm decision. He would study in dead earnest.

This is a story he told later, so it is a memory. But the records of the college show how hard he worked.

Columbia's registrar listed his courses. In three years, including summers, he took 29 courses in economics, 11 in history, 6 in sociology and 5 in philosophy. He took 4 in anthropology, 3 in politics, and one each in French and German.

He sat for hours in the reading room of the Low Library.

## Degrees and a paper

In June 1915 he passed his M.A. His main subject was economics. His thesis was called *Ancient Indian Commerce*.

In 1916 he handed in a second thesis, called "National Dividend of India". Keer says this one later became the start of his doctoral work.

On 9 May 1916 he read a paper to a class taught by Professor Goldenweiser. It was called "Castes in India: Their Mechanism, Genesis, and Development". He was proud of it. It came out in a journal in May 1917.

## Teachers and friends

Years later, he told a Columbia alumni magazine who his best friends had been. They were "some of my classmates at Columbia and my great professors". He named John Dewey, James Shotwell, Edwin Seligman and James Harvey Robinson.

In 1936 he wrote about Dewey, "who was my teacher and to whom I owe so much".

## A strange feeling

Ambedkar made a remark about these years in his notes, *Waiting for a Visa*. His five years in Europe and America, he wrote, had "completely wiped out of my mind any consciousness that I was an untouchable".

He wrote this just before telling the story of his arrival in Baroda.

## Why it matters

In New York, he had a room, a library and teachers who became his friends. He used them well.

Then he went home. Four years after he arrived in New York, he could not find a room to rent in Baroda. His own words say it: abroad, he had stopped thinking about caste. At home, it was waiting for him.
