A crowd waited at a railway station with flower garlands. They had come to welcome a guest. An hour later he was lying senseless on a stone bridge.

How did a welcome go so wrong?

## A visit to Khandesh

It was 1929. The government of Bombay had set up a committee to look into the problems of "untouchables". B. R. Ambedkar was one of its members. The members travelled around the province, hearing complaints.

Ambedkar was given two districts in Khandesh. When his work was done, he took a train towards Bombay. But at Chalisgaon he got off. He wanted to visit a village on the Dhulia line. Caste Hindus there had declared a "social boycott" against the untouchables. Nobody would deal with them.

<aside class="note"><b>Social boycott</b>: when a whole community refuses to trade with, work for or speak to a group of people, to punish them.</aside>

The untouchables of Chalisgaon met him at the station. They asked him to stay the night. He had not planned to. But they were eager, so he agreed. Pritchett's timeline gives the date of the accident as 23 October 1929.

## The garlands and the wait

He visited the village, learned what he could, and came back to Chalisgaon by the next train.

The people were waiting. They put garlands on him. Their quarter, the Maharwada, was about two miles from the station. A river lay between. There were many carriages at the station and the walk was short.

Ambedkar expected to go straight there. But nobody moved. He wrote that he could not understand why he was kept waiting.

After an hour, a tonga was brought close to the platform. He got in. The driver was the only other person in it. The rest of the people took a short cut on foot.

<aside class="note"><b>Tonga</b>: a light carriage pulled by one horse.</aside>

## A driver who could not drive

The tonga had gone about two hundred steps when it nearly hit a motor car. A policeman shouted. The car pulled back in time.

Ambedkar was surprised. A driver who worked every day should not be so careless.

Then they came to a culvert over the river. A culvert has no high walls like a bridge. This one had only a row of stones set at intervals along its edge. The road met it at a sharp turn.

At the very first stone, the horse did not go straight. It turned and bolted. A wheel struck the stone with great force. Ambedkar was lifted up and thrown onto the stone paving. The horse and tonga fell off the culvert into the river.

He lay senseless. He wrote that the Maharwada was just across the river. Men from the station got there before him. They lifted him and carried him in, while women, men and children cried out.

His leg was broken. He had several other injuries. Pritchett's timeline says he was kept in bed until the last week of December.

## What had really happened

Later, people told him the true story. The long wait at the station had a reason.

The tonga drivers of Chalisgaon would not carry an untouchable. They thought it was beneath them.

But the Mahars of Chalisgaon would not let their guest walk, either. They felt it did not show him enough respect.

So they made a deal. A tonga owner would hire out his tonga, but not drive it. The Mahars would find their own driver.

They thought this was a good solution. None of them knew how to drive a tonga. It was not their trade. One of them took the reins. He was so nervous that he lost control.

## "To save my dignity"

Ambedkar saw that the Mahars thought they had found a happy solution. He pointed out their mistake. He wrote this: "To save my dignity, the Mahars of Chalisgaon had put my very life in jeopardy."

They had thought about how to honour him. They had forgotten to ask whether he would be safe.

He also saw something sad about the tonga drivers. A Hindu tonga driver, he wrote, was "no better than a menial". Yet the driver still felt he was above an untouchable. This was true even when the untouchable was a barrister-at-law, as Ambedkar was.

<aside class="note"><b>Barrister</b>: a lawyer who argues cases in court. Ambedkar had qualified in London.</aside>

## Why it matters

Untouchability hurt people in ways you would not guess. Here, it stopped a man from getting into a carriage. Then, in the attempt to solve it, it put him in danger.

Ambedkar told this story about himself, and he did not hide the mistake of his own hosts. His lesson was about how deep the custom went.
