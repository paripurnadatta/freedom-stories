Water is the most ordinary thing in the world. You drink it every day without thinking.

But in 1927, in a small town called Mahad, drinking water from a public tank became one of the bravest acts in India's history.

## A tank that was "open"

In Mahad there was a large public water tank, called Chavdar Tale. But the people called "untouchable" were not allowed to use it.

<aside class="note"><b>Tale</b>: a tank or small lake. One writer explains that "chavdar" means pure, drinkable water.</aside>

In August 1923, the Bombay Legislative Council passed a resolution. It said that people of the "Depressed Classes" could use all public places that were built or looked after by the government. In January 1924, the town council of Mahad agreed to open its tank to them.

<aside class="note"><b>Depressed Classes</b>: the name the British government used then for the communities treated as "untouchable". Today we say Dalits.</aside>

But nothing changed. Higher-caste Hindus in the town opposed it. The rule stayed on paper.

## A young man's idea

Not everyone in power was against it. The chairman of the town council, Surbanana Tipnis, supported the decision to open the tank.

In 1924, a 21-year-old student named Ramchandra Babaji More had an idea. He asked Dr Bhimrao Ambedkar, a young lawyer and scholar from Bombay, to come and lead a conference in Mahad.

It took years of planning. At last, on 19 and 20 March 1927, the conference of the Depressed Classes of Kolaba district met in Mahad. Thousands of people came.

## The walk to the tank

Then Dr Ambedkar led them through the town to the Chavdar tank. About 3,000 people walked behind him.

At the water's edge, he bent down and drank. Then the others drank too.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: the Supreme Court's account and Wikipedia say the march to the tank was on 20 March 1927. One magazine article gives 19 March.</aside>

<aside class="note"><b>Satyagraha</b>: a peaceful protest, "holding on to truth". The word was made famous by Gandhi, and Ambedkar used it too.</aside>

Ambedkar felt that something new had happened. "I feel no parallel to this can be found in the history of India," he said.

He explained that the water itself was not the point. "We are going to the Tank to assert that we too are human beings like others," he said.

## The attack

Afterwards, a rumour spread through Mahad that the Dalits were planning to enter a Hindu temple in the town. Angry crowds of higher-caste men attacked the Dalits who had come for the conference. They beat people, broke into their homes and spoiled their grain.

Many Dalits found shelter in the homes of Muslim neighbours. Ambedkar spent the night in a government guest house.

This time, the law was on the Dalits' side. In June 1927, a British magistrate sentenced five of the attackers to four months in prison.

## The "purification"

Then the higher-caste Hindus of Mahad held a ceremony at the tank. They said the touch of the Dalits had made the water impure. Priests chanted, and 108 pots of a mixture made from cow dung, cow urine, milk, curd and ghee were poured into the tank to "purify" it.

For Dalits, it was a public insult.

## Taking it back

Under pressure, on 4 August 1927, the Mahad town council cancelled its 1924 decision. The tank was closed to Dalits again.

Ambedkar planned a second, even bigger satyagraha for December. But the higher-caste Hindus went to court. They claimed the tank was private property. On 14 December 1927, the court ordered that nobody should use the tank until the case was decided.

Ambedkar obeyed the court. He would not go to the tank while the case was going on.

## A fire on Christmas Day

The December conference still went ahead. About 10,000 people came.

On 25 December 1927, a copy of the Manusmriti was burned. The Manusmriti is an ancient Hindu law book. Ambedkar and his followers believed it laid down the rules of caste that kept Dalits at the bottom. Burning it was their way of rejecting those rules.

Ambedkar compared it to the burning of foreign cloth in the swadeshi movement. He knew that most higher-caste Hindus would be against it. "Our struggle is against the mass of caste Hindus," he said, "and naturally we have little support from outside."

<aside class="note"><b>Manusmriti Dahan Din</b>: many Dalits remember 25 December as "Manusmriti Burning Day".</aside>

"It is not as if drinking the water of the Chavdar Lake will make us immortal," Ambedkar said at that conference. The fight was not about water. It was about being treated as equal.

## The women of Mahad

Ambedkar also spoke to the Dalit women at Mahad. He asked them to give up the customs that marked them out as "untouchable", and to wear their saris the way higher-caste women did. Many of them did so straight away.

It was a small change, but it said a big thing: we are no different from anyone else.

## Ten years in court

The court case went on for ten years. In the end, every court said the same thing. The higher-caste Hindus could not prove any old custom that gave them the right to keep Dalits out. In December 1937, the Bombay High Court ruled that Dalits had the right to use the water of Chavdar Tale.

## The tank today

Today, Chavdar Tale is a place of pilgrimage. Dalits come from far away to drink its water and fill bottles to take home. A memorial to Ambedkar overlooks it. In India, 20 March is now marked as Social Empowerment Day.

In March 2026, the government of Maharashtra began a project to clean the tank. It wants the water to be pure again by 2027, a hundred years after Ambedkar drank from it.

Nine years after Mahad, in 1936, Ambedkar wrote a famous essay called Annihilation of Caste. In it he argued that changing how Indians treated each other could not be pushed aside until after political changes. Social reform had to come first.

<aside class="note"><b>Annihilation</b>: destroying something completely.</aside>

## Why it matters

There was water in the tank at Mahad. The problem was a rule in people's minds about who was allowed to touch it.

Dr Ambedkar and three thousand others walked to the water and broke that rule in public, peacefully. They were beaten for it. Then they went to court, and won.
