Imagine you step off a train in a city you have been called to serve. You have a bag, a job waiting, and nowhere to sleep.

That was Bhimrao Ambedkar, standing at Baroda station in 1917. He was already one of the best-educated Indians of his time.

He later wrote: "Where to go? Who will take me?"

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Ambedkar wrote this story about eighteen years later, from memory. He gives the years as 1916 to 1918, and the editor of his notes says "the dates here appear to be a bit confused". Frances Pritchett's timeline says he went home in June 1917. The LSE archivist says the University of London gave him leave in July 1917. We use 1917. His notes say he was a "probationer" in the Accountant General's Office. Pritchett and the LSE article say he was Military Secretary to the Maharaja. We do not know which job title is right, so we only say he worked for the state of Baroda.</aside>

## Why he had come

The Maharaja of Baroda had paid for Bhimrao to study. In 1913 he was sent to Columbia University in New York. Later he went to London to study at the London School of Economics.

The scholarship had a rule. When it ended, he had to work for Baroda State. His money ran out in 1917. So he had to leave London with his studies unfinished.

Bhimrao could have taken other jobs. He wrote that it was wartime and many posts were open. He knew influential people in London. But he felt he must serve the Maharaja first.

There was one more problem. He sent his books home by ship. A German submarine sank it. Pritchett's timeline says the travel firm Thomas Cook and Company paid damages for the lost luggage. Bhimrao used that money to pay his costs of getting to Baroda.

## Free for a while

Something odd had happened to him abroad. He wrote that five years in Europe and America had wiped away any thought that he was an untouchable.

So he stepped off the train in Baroda without a worry about where to sleep.

Then, outside the station, the old question came back.

He knew there were Hindu hotels, called Vishis. They would not take him. The only way in was by pretending to be somebody else. He did not want to risk that.

He had friends in Baroda who had also studied abroad. But he could not be sure they would welcome him. They might feel embarrassed to take an untouchable into their home.

<aside class="note"><b>Untouchable</b>: a word for people who were treated as unclean because of the caste they were born into. This custom is now against the law.</aside>

## The Parsi inn

Everyone else had left the station. A few carriage drivers waited.

Bhimrao asked one if there was a hotel in the camp. The driver said there was a Parsi inn that took paying guests.

Bhimrao was glad. Parsis follow the Zoroastrian religion. He wrote that their religion does not recognise untouchability. So he thought he would be safe there.

He put his bags in a carriage and went. The inn had two floors. An old Parsi man lived downstairs with his family. He was the caretaker, and he sold food to guests.

The caretaker showed him up. Bhimrao began to undress to rest. The caretaker came up with a register in his hand. He could see that Bhimrao did not have the two things that mark a Parsi. Bhimrao wrote their names as Sadra and Kasti. The caretaker asked, sharply, who he was.

<aside class="note"><b>Parsi</b>: a follower of the Zoroastrian religion.</aside>

## A name in the register

Bhimrao did not know that this inn was only for Parsis. He said he was a Hindu.

The caretaker was shocked. He said Bhimrao could not stay.

Bhimrao told him he did not mind staying, if the caretaker did not mind. The caretaker said, "How can you? I have to maintain a register of all those who stay here."

Bhimrao saw the problem. He said he could use a Parsi name in the register.

The caretaker agreed on one condition. Bhimrao must pay a rupee and a half every day for food and room.

Bhimrao later called the whole plan a fraud. That is his word. He wrote it himself.

## A dark hall full of bats

The room was small. Next to it was a bathroom with a tap. The rest of the floor was one huge hall, full of broken planks, benches and chairs.

There were no lights. The caretaker brought a small lantern. Its glow reached only a few inches.

The caretaker came three times a day, with tea, then breakfast, then dinner. He never stayed to talk.

Bhimrao worked at the Baroda government office. He left at ten and came back at eight in the evening. He spent as much time with friends as he could. The thought of the empty hall at night terrified him.

He read books by the small lantern. But bats lived in the hall. Their flapping and chirping gave him a cold shiver.

He wrote that it felt like a dungeon. "Some shelter," he told himself, "was better than no shelter."

His sister's son came from Bombay with some of his luggage. When he saw how his uncle was living, he began to cry loudly. Bhimrao sent him home at once.

He knew he could not carry on pretending for long. So he asked for a government bungalow. His request went from one officer to another. No answer came.

## The eleventh day

On the eleventh morning he was dressed for work. He was picking up some library books when he heard many footsteps on the stairs.

He thought they were new guests. He looked out. He saw a dozen tall, angry Parsi men. Each had a stick.

They lined up in front of his room. "Who are you? Why did you come here? How dare you take a Parsi name?" they shouted. "You have polluted the Parsi inn!"

Bhimrao stood silent. He had no answer. He wrote that the pretending was a fraud. He believed that if he had kept up the game, the crowd might have attacked him. His silence helped. Nobody hit him.

One of the men asked when he would leave. He begged for one more week. They would not listen. They said he must be gone by evening.

He cried bitterly. He wrote that the room was like a prison cell. Still, it was precious to him.

## Nowhere to go

Bhimrao went to his two friends. One was a Hindu. He was sad and angry for Bhimrao. But he said: "If you come to my home, my servants will go." Bhimrao understood. He did not press him.

The other was an Indian Christian. He said his wife was coming the next day and he must ask her first. Later Bhimrao learned why. The wife was from a family of Brahmin background. She would not have taken an untouchable into her house.

It was four o'clock. There was only one place left to go. Bombay.

The train left Baroda at nine at night. Bhimrao had five hours.

## Kamathi Baug

He could not go back to the inn. He feared the Parsis might attack him. He did not want to go to his friend, either. He did not want to be pitied.

So he sat in a public garden called Kamathi Baug, on the edge of the city. He wrote that he sat partly with an empty mind and partly in sorrow. He thought of his father and mother, "as children do when they are in a forlorn condition".

At eight he took a carriage back to the inn. He collected his luggage. The caretaker came out. Neither of them could say a word. Bhimrao paid his bill. The caretaker took it in silence.

Then Bhimrao left Baroda, after eleven days.

## What happened next

Back in Bombay, he had to find work again. Pritchett's timeline, quoting the biographer Keer, says he became a private tutor and did some accounts work for others. He started a business to advise people on investing. It failed when clients learned he was an untouchable.

In 1918 he became a professor at Sydenham College in Bombay. Some other professors objected when he drank from the same water jug that they all used.

## Why it matters

Ambedkar wrote that the scene of a dozen men with sticks never faded. Eighteen years later, he still could not remember it without tears.

He also drew a lesson from it. Until then, he wrote, he had not known that a person who is untouchable to a Hindu can be untouchable to a Parsi too.

Nobody in this story is described hitting him. A caretaker wanted to earn some money. A friend worried about his servants. A crowd wanted to guard an inn meant for Parsis. Each person followed the rules of their own group.

That is one way an unfair custom can last. And a man who had crossed the sea to study could not find a bed.
