Have you ever been so excited about a journey that you could not sleep? Now imagine the journey going wrong, one small step at a time.

That is what happened to a boy named Bhimrao. He was about nine. He wrote about that day many years later, and this story follows his own words.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Ambedkar's notes, called <i>Waiting for a Visa</i>, were written in 1935 or 1936. He says the trip happened "in about 1901". He wrote it from memory. Everything below is how he remembered it.</aside>

## Pulav for dinner

Bhimrao's mother had died. His father worked far away, as a cashier at a place called Koregaon, in the Satara district. The government had started digging a big tank there. It gave work to people who were starving in a famine.

The children stayed in Satara. An aunt looked after them, but she could hardly walk. So the children cooked for themselves.

They could not make bread. They made pulav, because it was the easiest dish. You only had to mix rice and mutton.

One day a letter came from their father. Come to Koregaon for your summer holidays, it said.

None of the children had ever seen a train.

## New clothes for the journey

They got ready with great care. Bhimrao wrote that they ordered new shirts, shiny caps with jewels, new shoes and new silk-bordered dhotis.

His father had said he would send a servant to meet them at the station. So the children wrote back and gave the day.

Bhimrao, his older brother and one of his sister's sons set out. A tonga took them to the railway station, ten miles away. Their aunt cried as they left.

At the station, his brother bought the tickets. He gave each of the younger boys two annas to spend. They bought a bottle of lemonade each. Bhimrao called it "riotous living".

Then the train came in. They climbed on as fast as they could, afraid it would leave without them.

<aside class="note"><b>Anna</b>: an old coin. There were sixteen annas in one rupee.</aside>

## Nobody at Masur

The train reached Masur at about five in the evening. Masur was the station nearest to Koregaon.

The other passengers walked away. The children stood on the platform with their bags.

They waited for the servant. An hour went by. Nobody came.

Then the stationmaster walked up. He asked to see their tickets. He asked why they were waiting.

The children told him about their father. The stationmaster looked at their fine clothes. Bhimrao wrote that the man was sure they were Brahmin children. He felt sorry for them.

Then, as many people did in those days, he asked who they were.

Bhimrao answered at once, without thinking. They were Mahars.

## "His face underwent a sudden change"

Mahars were treated as "untouchable". Many higher-caste people believed that even touching a Mahar made them unclean.

<aside class="note"><b>Untouchable</b>: a word used for people who were treated as unclean because of the caste they were born into. This cruel custom is now against the law in India.</aside>

Bhimrao wrote that the stationmaster was stunned. His face changed. The boys could see that he felt something like disgust. He went into his room.

Fifteen or twenty minutes passed. The sun was almost setting. The father had not come. Now the stationmaster had gone too.

Bhimrao wrote that their joy had turned to sadness.

## A cart that nobody would drive

After half an hour, the stationmaster came back. What did they want to do?

They said they would hire a bullock cart and go to Koregaon.

Many carts stood waiting for customers. But the news that the boys were Mahars had spread among the drivers. Not one of them would carry them. The boys offered to pay twice the fare. Money did not help.

The stationmaster did not know what to do. Then he asked a strange question. "Can you drive the cart?"

"Yes, we can," the boys shouted.

So he made a deal. The boys would pay double. They would drive. The cartman would walk beside the cart. One driver agreed. He would earn his fare, and he would not be "polluted".

It was about half past six when they set out. The driver told them it would take no more than three hours.

## The pool in the dry river

Soon they came to a river. It was almost dry, with a few small pools.

The cartman said they should stop and eat here. They might not find water later. He asked for part of his fare and went to the village for his own meal.

The boys opened the basket their aunt had packed. They were very hungry.

They needed water. One of them went to the pool. Bhimrao wrote that it was thick with mud and with the dung of cattle. The smell was so strong that they could not drink it.

So they ate only a little. Then they waited for a long time for the cartman to return.

## The dark road

At last he came back. For four or five miles the boys drove, and he walked. Then, suddenly, he jumped on and took the reins himself.

The boys thought this was odd. He had refused to carry them for fear of being polluted. Now he sat in the same cart. They did not dare ask him why.

Night fell. There were no street lights. They saw no people and no cattle.

Three hours passed. There was no sign of Koregaon.

Bhimrao wrote that a frightening thought came into their heads. They wore gold ornaments. Maybe the cartman was taking them to a lonely place to hurt them. They asked him again and again. He only said, "It is not very far."

At about ten at night, the boys began to cry. The cartman said nothing.

Then they saw a light in the distance. It was the toll-collector's hut, he said. They would rest there. It took two more hours to reach it.

<aside class="note"><b>Toll-collector</b>: a man who collected a fee from travellers on a road.</aside>

## The last question

The hut stood at the foot of a hill. Many other carts were resting there. The boys were hungry. But again there was the question of water.

The cartman warned them that the toll-collector was a Hindu. He would not help if the boys said they were Mahars. "Say you are Muslims," he told them, "and try your luck."

Bhimrao went to the hut. He spoke in Urdu, which he knew well, so that nobody would doubt him.

It did not work. "Who has kept water for you?" the man said sharply. There was water on the hill, he said, if they wanted to go and get it. He had none.

Bhimrao went back to the cart. He told his brother. His brother only said, "Lie down."

They had plenty of food. They had no water. Bhimrao wrote that hunger was burning inside them, and they must sleep without eating. They could not drink, because they were untouchables.

His brother thought it was not safe for all of them to sleep. So two slept while two kept watch.

## The morning

At five in the morning the cartman said it was time to go. The boys refused. They would not move until eight o'clock.

He did not argue. They left at eight and reached Koregaon at eleven.

Their father was surprised. He said he had not known they were coming. The boys said they had written to him.

Later they found out what had happened. His servant had got the letter. He had never given it to him.

## What he wrote at the end

Bhimrao wrote that this journey had a very important place in his life. He was nine, but it left a mark that never went away.

He had known before that he was an untouchable. He knew he sat in a corner at school, on his own piece of sack. He knew that if the school peon was away, he had to go without water.

But this night was different. It shook him, he wrote, as nothing had before. It made him think about untouchability for the first time. Before that, it had been just the way things were.

<aside class="note"><b>One small puzzle</b>: Ambedkar says the three boys left Satara. A little later he writes "we four children" on the platform. His notes do not explain this. We do not know who the fourth child was.</aside>

## Why it matters

This story is not about a villain. Nobody in it hit the boys. The stationmaster tried to help, in his own way. The cartman did the job in the end.

Yet three tired children spent a night with food and no water, because of the family they were born into.

Ambedkar still remembered it more than thirty years later. When he grew up, he spent his life fighting untouchability.
