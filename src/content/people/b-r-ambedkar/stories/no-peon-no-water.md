Imagine you are thirsty at school. There is water right there. But you are not allowed to touch the tap, or even the cup.

You must wait until a servant comes and pours the water for you, from high above. And if the servant is not there?

Then you go thirsty.

That was a normal school day for a boy named Bhimrao, more than a hundred years ago. He later wrote it down in four short words: "No peon, no water."

<aside class="note"><b>Peon</b>: a servant or helper in an office or school.</aside>

## A boy from a Mahar family

Bhimrao was born on 14 April 1891, in Mhow, an army town in central India. He was the fourteenth and last child of Ramji Sakpal, an officer in the army.

His family belonged to the Mahar caste. In those days, Hindus of higher castes treated Mahars as "untouchable". They believed that even the touch of a Mahar would make them unclean.

<aside class="note"><b>Untouchability</b>: the cruel custom of treating some people as "untouchable" because of the caste they were born into.</aside>

## Sitting on a sack

Around 1896, the family moved to Satara. Soon after, Bhimrao's mother died.

At school, Bhimrao and the other "untouchable" children were kept apart. They were not allowed to sit inside the classroom with the others. Bhimrao had to bring a gunny sack from home to sit on.

And there was the water. He could not touch the tap or the pot. A higher-caste person, usually the school peon, had to pour it for him from a height. No peon meant no water.

## The journey to Koregaon

When Bhimrao was about nine or ten, something happened that he never forgot. Many years later, he wrote about it himself, in some notes he called Waiting for a Visa.

His father was working at a place called Koregaon, where starving people were being given work during a famine. The children had stayed behind in Satara. One day, Bhimrao, his older brother and a nephew set off by train to visit him.

They wore their best clothes, and even some gold jewellery. They wrote to their father to say they were coming, and got off at the station nearest Koregaon, called Masur.

Nobody was there to meet them.

They waited. After about an hour, the stationmaster came up and asked who they were. When they told him they were Mahars, Ambedkar later wrote, the man's manner changed completely. He looked at them with disgust.

## Nobody would take them

The boys needed a bullock cart to reach Koregaon. They offered to pay double. But not one cart driver would agree to carry Mahar children.

At last, the stationmaster asked: "Can you drive the cart?" One cart driver agreed to rent them his cart, as long as the boys drove it themselves while he walked alongside. That way, he would earn his money without being "polluted" by them.

## No water

They drove on. They were hungry and thirsty. Near a dry river, the only water they could find was a muddy pool full of animal waste. They could not drink it.

Late at night, they reached a toll collector's hut. The cart driver told them to pretend to be Muslims, so that they could get water. They tried. The toll collector refused anyway. "Who has kept water for you?" he asked.

So the three boys went without food or water. They slept in the cart, taking turns to keep watch. They reached Koregaon only at about eleven the next morning.

Their letter had never reached their father. His servant had not delivered it.

Ambedkar later wrote that this was the moment when untouchability first hit him as a personal shock, instead of something he simply accepted.

## A new name

Bhimrao's family name was Sakpal. When he started school, his father registered him as Ambadawekar, after the family's home village. Then a teacher, a Brahmin named Krishnaji Keshav Ambedkar, changed it in the school records to Ambedkar. That is the name the world now knows.

## The only one at Elphinstone

In 1897, the family moved to Bombay. Bhimrao became the only "untouchable" student at Elphinstone High School. In 1906, when he was about 15, he was married to Ramabai, who was nine. Their parents arranged it, as was common then.

<aside class="note"><b>Child marriage</b>: in those days, many children in India were married very young.</aside>

He passed his school exams in 1907. In 1908, he joined Elphinstone College. He was said to be the first person from the Mahar caste to do so.

## Across the ocean, and back again

In 1913, the Maharaja of Baroda gave him a scholarship. He went to study at Columbia University in New York. He later earned degrees there.

When he came back, he had to work for the state of Baroda for a while, as part of the scholarship. But no Hindu hotel in the city would let him stay.

At last, he found a place at an inn run by Parsis, by pretending to be a Parsi. He lived there alone, in a dark hall, with bats flying around. On the eleventh day, a group of angry men with sticks came and found out who he was. They ordered him out by evening. He sat for five hours in a public garden, then caught the night train to Bombay.

He had studied in New York. In Baroda, he could not find a bed for the night.

## Why it matters

Bhimrao Ambedkar grew up to be one of the most important people in modern India. He fought all his life for the rights of Dalits, and he led the team that wrote India's Constitution.

He did not learn about injustice from books. He learned it as a thirsty boy at a school tap, and on a dark road to Koregaon. He made sure the rest of India would hear about it too.
