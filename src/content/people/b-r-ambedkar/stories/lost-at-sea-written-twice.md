Imagine working for months on a long piece of research. Then the ship carrying it is sunk in a war.

That is what happened to B. R. Ambedkar in 1917. Years later, it nearly happened again, in a different way.

## The ship that sank

In 1917 Ambedkar's scholarship from Baroda ended. He had to leave London in the middle of his studies. The University of London gave him leave of up to four years to come back and finish.

He packed his books and sent them home on a ship. A German submarine sank it. It was the First World War.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Frances Pritchett's timeline and Wikipedia say it was his book collection that was lost. Ambedkar himself wrote something else in a letter to his Columbia teacher, Edwin Seligman, in 1922. He said he lost "the manuscript of the original thesis" when the steamer was torpedoed. Maybe both were on board. We also do not know whether the "original thesis" meant his Columbia work or his London work. Pritchett lists the Columbia doctorate (awarded in 1927) as a thesis called <i>The Evolution of Provincial Finance in British India</i>.</aside>

Pritchett's timeline says one more thing. The travel firm paid damages for the lost luggage. Ambedkar used that money on his costs of getting to Baroda.

## Back to London, with very little money

In July 1920 he went back to London. He had saved some money and had loans from a friend and from the Maharaja of Kolhapur. Biographers say he lived in poverty and worked for hours at the British Museum.

Not everybody at the London School of Economics knew who he was. In November 1920 a senior teacher, Herbert Foxwell, wrote to the school office. Ambedkar had asked to continue his research. Foxwell said he had found out that Ambedkar already had a doctor's degree. "I am sorry we cannot identify him with the School," he wrote. "But there are no more worlds here for him to conquer."

It was a joke, in a way. Ambedkar was not finished. In June 1921 he got a master's degree from LSE. Then he began a doctoral thesis called *The Problem of the Rupee*.

<aside class="note"><b>Thesis</b>: a long piece of research writing, which a student must hand in to earn a high degree.</aside>

## Sent back

In 1922 Ambedkar told Seligman that he had written out a new thesis, because he had lost the old one. By 1923 he had finished his London thesis, *The Problem of the Rupee*.

Then came bad news. In March 1923 he handed it in, and the examiners did not recommend it. He sent it in again later that year.

Some writers say the examiners thought it was too "revolutionary" and against British rule. The LSE archivist looked at his student file and wrote: "there is no indication of this in Ambedkar's student file."

<aside class="note"><b>Three versions</b>: Pritchett's timeline says the thesis was "challenged on political grounds". The LSE archivist says the file does not show that. A later LSE article says the letters in his file neither prove nor disprove it. Wikipedia says the thesis was accepted in early 1923. The LSE archivist says it was resubmitted in August 1923 and accepted in November. We follow the LSE, because it is based on the school's own records.</aside>

So we know he was asked to revise it. We do not know exactly why.

## Accepted

Ambedkar resubmitted the thesis in August 1923. It was accepted in November. It came out as a book very soon.

His teacher Edwin Cannan wrote the foreword. He found in it "a stimulating freshness", even though he disagreed with some of its ideas. In his own preface, Ambedkar thanked Cannan for a "severe examination" of his arguments, which had "saved me from many an error".

The book was dedicated to the memory of his father and mother. Pritchett's timeline says this was a thank-you for the sacrifices they made, and for the importance they gave to his education.

In the same year, Gray's Inn called him to the Bar. He was now a barrister.

## Why it matters

From 1913 to 1923, Ambedkar's studies took him to New York and to London. He lost his books or his writing in a war. He lived with little money. He had to send his thesis in twice.

After that, he went home to India and began working for the rights of untouchables.
