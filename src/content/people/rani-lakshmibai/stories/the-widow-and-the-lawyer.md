Before the guns of 1858, there was a quiet fight in 1854. It was fought with paper.

## The last letter

Wikipedia says that two days before he died, the Maharaja of Jhansi, Gangadhar Rao, wrote to the East India Company's officers. He asked them to accept his adopted son, Damodar Rao, as the ruler. He asked them to accept Lakshmibai as the boy's guardian. The article quotes him: "Please acknowledge my widow as the mother of this boy during her lifetime."

He died on 21 November 1853.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The Wikipedia article on the Rani dates the letter two days before his death, on 19 November. The Wikipedia article on Damodar Rao says the adoption took place the day before he died. The two dates are close, but they are not the same.</aside>

## The answer

The Governor-General, Lord Dalhousie, did not agree. Jhansi had been handed to the Company in 1817. Under the Doctrine of Lapse, the Company could now take it over.

<aside class="note"><b>Doctrine of Lapse</b>: a Company rule. If an Indian ruler died with no son of his own, the Company took the kingdom. It did not count an adopted son.</aside>

Wikipedia says the Rani wrote a letter of protest on 16 February 1854. Eleven days later Dalhousie wrote a note of his own. In it, he described the late Maharaja's rule as one of decline. He called the Company the "paramount power."

## Hiring a lawyer

The Rani did not give up. Wikipedia says she spoke to a sympathetic British official, Major Ellis. She also hired an Australian lawyer, John Lang.

Her letters referred to treaties of 1803, 1817 and 1842. They also mentioned Hindu law. Wikipedia says Dalhousie turned them down without answering the points. The Rani called the annexation a "gross violation" of treaties. An historian quoted in the article, Rudrangshu Mukherjee, says she was "probably the first" to argue directly with the Company in this way.

Jhansi was taken in May 1854. Wikipedia says Captain Alexander Skene gave her a pension and let her keep the town palace, called the Rani Mahal. She had to leave the fort.

The Company also said she owed thirty-six thousand rupees of her husband's debts. The Rani said that the state's debts belonged to the state, and so to the British, who had now taken it. Wikipedia says the matter was never settled.

## The only English description

John Lang wrote a book called *Wanderings in India*. Live History India says it holds the only English description of the Rani's looks.

Lang says he saw her only for a moment, when a child drew aside a curtain. He says she was about twenty-six or twenty-seven, of middle size, "rather stout, but not too stout." He found her eyes "particularly fine" and thought her face "too round." He says she wore no jewels except gold earrings, and a plain white muslin dress. He did not like her voice.

Live History India warns that Lang probably made some of his story bigger. He liked to boast to his British readers. It says he claimed that she gave him gifts, including an elephant, a camel and a horse, and a portrait of herself. No portrait has ever been found.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: If she was twenty-six or twenty-seven in 1854, she was born around 1827. Wikipedia says British sources used 1827, Indian sources 1835, and some historians 1828 to 1830. Lang's guess agrees with the British date. But it is only a guess from one quick look.</aside>

## What she is said to have said

Live History India says Lang recorded her saying "Mera Jhansi nahin dengee", which means "I will not give my Jhansi." National Geographic says she said, "I will not give up my Jhansi," when offered an annuity. Wikipedia says the line was made up for a novel in 1946. We look at this in "Who said 'I will not give up my Jhansi'?".

## Why it matters

Before she was a soldier, the Rani was a widow who used the law. She cited treaties and hired the best lawyer she could find.

She lost. The Company did not answer her arguments. Four years later, Jhansi went to war.
