"I will not give up my Jhansi!"

Every Indian child seems to know this line. It is printed in textbooks and shouted in films. But did the Rani of Jhansi say it?

## Four answers

**1. A lawyer's book.** Live History India says that John Lang, her Australian lawyer, wrote down the words in Hindi in his book *Wanderings in India*: "Mera Jhansi nahin dengee." The article warns that Lang often made up parts of his stories to please his readers.

**2. A refusal of money.** National Geographic says she said, "I will not give up my Jhansi," when she was offered a yearly payment to leave. It does not name the source for this.

**3. The annexation.** The Wikipedia article on her son, Damodar Rao, says she "reportedly" declared, "I shall not surrender my Jhansi." It does not say where it comes from.

**4. A novel.** The Wikipedia article on the Rani says that the line "I will never give up my Jhansi!" was made up for a novel. The novel was written by Vrindavan Lal Verma, and came out in 1946. The article says it was not a recorded saying.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Live History India credits Lang's book with the Hindi words. Wikipedia credits the line to Verma's novel of 1946. Lang's book was written in the 1850s. If Lang did write the words, they were not invented in 1946. If Verma invented the line, he did not know it from Lang. We have not read either book, and we cannot settle it.</aside>

## What we do know

Wikipedia says the Rani wrote a protest on 16 February 1854, eleven days before Lord Dalhousie replied. It says her final appeals called the takeover a "gross violation" of treaties. So her refusal was real. The words we are not sure of.

## The poem

The other famous piece of writing about her is a poem. Wikipedia says it is "Jhansi ki Rani", by Subhadra Kumari Chauhan, from 1930. It is still learned by heart in schools. In 2007 it was performed in Parliament House by Shubha Mudgal, to mark 150 years since 1857.

National Geographic says Chauhan wrote it after independence in 1947.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia says 1930. National Geographic says after 1947. The two cannot both be right. We lean towards 1930 because Wikipedia names the year, but we have not seen the poem's first printing.</aside>

## Earlier voices

Wikipedia says people were singing and writing about the Rani soon after her death. Folk songs from 1857, 1861 and 1870 survive. The poet Rabindranath Tagore wrote an essay on her in 1877, when he was sixteen. It called her a model of virtue.

British writers saw her differently. Wikipedia says that during the revolt, some linked her with Kali, a fierce goddess. Later writers compared her to Joan of Arc.

## Why it matters

A famous saying can come from three places: the person, the writers who came after them, and the people who needed a hero. It is hard to separate the three.

The Rani did refuse to give up Jhansi. Whether or not she used these exact words, her actions said the same thing.
