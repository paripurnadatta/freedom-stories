In every statue of the Rani of Jhansi, there is a small boy. He is tied to her back as she rides away from the fort.

Who was he? What happened to him after the battles were over? He is not just part of a statue. He was a real child, and he lived for almost another fifty years.

## The boy

His name at birth was Anand Rao. He was born on 15 November 1849. His family was a side branch of the royal family of Jhansi. When the Maharaja of Jhansi, Gangadhar Rao, had no son of his own, the boy was adopted. He was given a new name, Damodar Rao.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia says he was born at Parola Fort, in what is now Maharashtra. A memoir attributed to him says he was born in Jhansi.</aside>

He was adopted at three, says the memoir. Wikipedia and National Geographic both say he was five. The memoir adds that court astrologers had told his family he would be a king. The words in Hindi are "Raj Yog".

## The kingdom that was taken

Gangadhar Rao died on 21 November 1853. The British ruler, Lord Dalhousie, would not accept the adoption. He used a rule called the Doctrine of Lapse. It said that a state without a born son could be taken over by the East India Company.

<aside class="note"><b>Doctrine of Lapse</b>: a rule used by the East India Company. When an Indian ruler died without a son of his own, the Company took over his kingdom. It did not accept an adopted son as heir.</aside>

The Rani asked Dalhousie in Calcutta to accept her son, and the request was turned down. The British gave her an annual pension of five thousand rupees.

<aside class="note"><b>Three versions</b>: Wikipedia says her pension was five thousand rupees a month. National Geographic says an annuity of sixty thousand rupees a year. The memoir says five thousand a year.</aside>

There were seven lakh rupees in the treasury in his father's name. A lakh is a hundred thousand. The British said they would keep it in trust until he was grown up. He never received it. Wikipedia gives the sum as six lakh.

## The siege

When the British attacked Jhansi in the spring of 1858, Damodar Rao was about eight. He says in the memoir that he was too young to remember.

His mother's attendants told him later that she had carried him on her back in battle. The tradition holds that she leaped from the fort on her horse with him on her back. It is more likely that she left in the night with guards, with her son tied to her waist.

She died near Gwalior in June 1858. We tell that story in "The last days at Gwalior".

## Three days at Gwalior

After her death, he stayed in Gwalior for three days. Only sixty of his mother's trusted men were still alive. Four of them took charge of him. Their names were Nanekhan Risaldar, Ganpatrao, Raghunath Singh and Ramchandrarao Deshmukh.

The group had twenty-two horses and sixty camels. They left the camp of Rao Sahib, a leader of the rebellion. They headed for Bundelkhand, a region to the south.

No village would help them. People were afraid the British would punish them.

## Fruit, berries and a cave

They slept in the open, in a forest by a river. They ate wild fruit and berries. The boy never went hungry, he says, but they were afraid of British patrols. In the rains, floods cut off the forest paths.

A village headman agreed to bring food and warn them if soldiers came. He asked for five hundred rupees a month, nine horses and four camels. To avoid suspicion, the group split into small parties of about ten.

They then lived in a cave under a cliff, beside the Vetravati river. There was a temple of Mahadev and a waterfall nearby. They stayed for about two years.

In the month of Bhadrapad, the boy became very ill. The headman's uncle, a local healer, treated him in secret.

## The money runs out

About sixty thousand rupees had come with them from Gwalior. In time it was gone. The headman asked them to leave. They paid him two hundred rupees and asked for their horses. He gave back only three and said the rest had died.

Soon after, local people at a place called Shipri-Kolaras recognised them as rebels. They were held for three days. Then ten horsemen and twenty-five soldiers took them to the British Political Agent at Jhalrapatan.

Their horses had been taken. So the guards took turns to carry the sick boy on their backs.

## A friend at the agency

At Jhalrapatan, an old servant of the Rani, Nanhekhan, was now working for the Political Agency. He spoke up for the boy to a British officer named Flink, who was kind.

The ruler of Jhalrapatan, Raja Prithvi Singh, treated the boy well too. He promised to speak for him to the British Resident.

The boy was kept for about three months near Jhalrapatan. He had no money. He sold two gold bracelets of his mother's, each of thirty-two tolas. He calls them among his last memories of her.

## Indore

On 5 May 1860, he reached Indore. There he met Sir Richard Shakespeare, the British Political Agent.

He was put in the care of a Kashmiri teacher, Munshi Dharmanarayan, who taught him Urdu, English and Marathi. He was allowed to keep only seven followers. He was given a yearly pension of ten thousand rupees, which he accepted because he was a child.

<aside class="note"><b>Several figures</b>: Wikipedia also gives a pension of four hundred rupees, without saying whether it was monthly or yearly. The Free Press Journal says his son later got two hundred rupees a month. No source explains the differences.</aside>

## Later life

He married, and his first wife died soon after. He then married again. A son, Lakshman Rao, was born in 1904. Damodar Rao died on 28 May 1906, at 56.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Live History India gives the same date and says he was 58. The Free Press Journal says 20 May 1906, at 57.</aside>

He loved photography. He painted a portrait of his mother, and no one knows where it is today.

His family took the name "Jhansiwale", meaning "of Jhansi". The family lived in Indore, in rented houses, and later in Nagpur. A great-great-grandson is a software engineer. A newspaper alone tells this last part.

## Can we trust the memoir?

The memoir is the main source for his years in the forest. It is also hard to check.

It is "purported", meaning it is said to be his. It was printed in a Marathi book by the historian Y. N. Kelkar, in 1951 by one account and 1959 by another. The book itself was not seen.

A memoir written long afterwards can get dates and numbers wrong. In this one, the numbers of horses, camels and rupees are the kind of detail that can change in the telling. But the shape of the story matches what other sources say: the Rani died, a few loyal men saved the boy, and the British made him a pensioner.

## Why it matters

Nearly every story of the Rani ends on the battlefield. This one begins there.

A child of eight was left without a mother, a kingdom or money. For two years, a few faithful men carried him, fed him and hid him. Their names are in the memoir. They deserve to be remembered too.
