For several months in 1857 and 1858, Rani Lakshmibai ruled Jhansi. Then a British army came to take it back.

Most of what we know about the siege comes from British reports and from one Indian eyewitness. They do not always agree. This story keeps them side by side.

## An army on the road

The British commander was Major General Hugh Rose. Wikipedia says he led a force called the Central India Field Force. It left Indore on 6 January 1858. On the way it relieved a town called Sagar, defeated an army at Shahgarh, and took the fort of Banpur.

By the time Rose reached Jhansi, Wikipedia says his army was about three thousand men.

Inside Jhansi, the Rani had been getting ready. She had recruited soldiers, and had cannon made. On 15 March the Raja of Banpur, who had fought on her side before, came back with three and a half thousand men.

Wikipedia says that she was still writing to the British in March, hoping to avoid war. It says she may have been pushed into fighting by her own troops. The article is careful here, and so are we. We do not know what was said in the palace.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Did the Rani choose war, or did events choose it for her? One article on the siege says she refused Rose's demand to surrender, which included a threat to destroy the city. The other article says she was still sending peace messages days before the attack. We cannot say which, or whether both are true.</aside>

## The walls

The fort of Jhansi stands on a rock. Wikipedia says the walls were twenty-five feet high. The land around had been cleared, so attackers could be seen from far off. It says there were eleven and a half thousand fighters in the town, and hundreds of tons of supplies.

The article on the siege says the fort had heavy guns that could fire across the town and the countryside.

## The guns begin

Wikipedia gives 22 March 1858 as the day the shelling began. The article on the siege starts its story on 24 March. These two dates are two days apart.

The Rani's best gunners were killed early, says Wikipedia. On 29 March the British made a hole in the wall, called a breach. The defenders quickly blocked it with a barrier of stakes.

## The relief army

On 1 April, an army led by Tatya Tope came to help Jhansi. Wikipedia says it had more than twenty thousand men. Rose sent twelve hundred to meet it at the river Betwa.

The British lost fewer than a hundred men, says Wikipedia. It says the relief army lost over fifteen hundred. It broke and withdrew. Fires set in the forest to slow the British also hurt Tatya Tope's own men.

Jhansi was alone.

## The night of 2 and 3 April

The attack began at three in the morning on 3 April. Wikipedia says two columns went at the south wall. One came through the breach. The other climbed ladders.

The Rani led a counter-attack with fifteen hundred Afghan fighters, but they were pushed back to the fort.

By sunset, the British held the town except for the north-east corner and the fort. Fighting in the streets went on into the next day.

## What a priest saw

One Indian eyewitness wrote about these days. His name was Vishnubhat Godse. He was a priest from a village in Maharashtra. He had walked north in 1857, hoping to be paid at a great religious ceremony, and ended up at Jhansi, where he was looked after by the Rani's father.

Godse wrote his travel book, *Maza Pravas*, about twenty-four years later. It was printed in 1907. His great-great-granddaughter, Sukhmani Roy Vijh, published an English translation in 2012.

Godse says the walls were broken on 2 April. He writes that the British used strong telescopes to find the fort's only water source, and shelled it. He says shells fell on the elephant stables and on the animals of the zoo.

He also says the soldiers set fire to the town, starting at a place called Halwaipura. He says they killed boys and men between five and thirty years old. He says that women were robbed but not killed.

The National Geographic article gives a soldier's own words. A British soldier wrote that he had been told to "spare nobody over 16, except women of course." The article says as many as five thousand people died. Wikipedia's article on the Rani says three thousand.

<aside class="note"><b>Handle with care</b>: These are sad pages. Many people who were not soldiers died in Jhansi that week. The British lost 343 men, killed and wounded, according to one article. Godse had his own views as a Brahmin priest and his own fears. He also wrote his book long afterwards. Treat his numbers, like 60,000 British soldiers, with care. Other sources give about three thousand.</aside>

## Leaving the fort

On the night of 3 April, the Rani left. How, nobody can say for sure.

- **The tradition.** She leapt from the wall on her horse, Sarangi, with her son on her back. The horse died. Wikipedia says this is how the tradition tells it, and that most historians think it unlikely.
- **A British account.** A soldier from India serving under Rose arranged for a horse to wait below the fort. The Rani and her son were lowered down to it.
- **A British officer's idea.** Rose's aide, Anthony Lyster, wrote that Rose left a gap of four hundred yards in the line of sentries, as a trap. Wikipedia says this is doubtful, since Rose knew she was a fine rider.
- **The priest's account.** Godse says she rode a white horse and wore men's clothes and armour. He says she shouted "Jai Shankar" as she came down from the fort. She left by the north gate, and British horsemen chased her for about three miles.
- **The simplest account.** The article on the siege says she left in the night with her son, surrounded by guards, "probably while Rose's cavalry were busy looting."

Wikipedia says she had about three hundred soldiers with her, and her adopted son was tied to her waist. It says she narrowly escaped a British party that caught up with her while she stopped to eat. She reached Kalpi at midnight on 5 April, after riding about 102 miles.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The tradition says she covered the 102 miles in one night. Wikipedia says the more reliable sources put her arrival at midnight on 5 April. That would be a longer ride, not one night.</aside>

## Her father

Her father, Moropant Tambe, left Jhansi too. Wikipedia says he escaped but was caught, and executed on 20 April 1858. Godse tells it differently. He says Moropant was badly wounded and rode to Datia, where a man who sold paan reported him to the ruler. He says the British brought him back to Jhansi and hanged him in front of the Rani's palace.

## The fort falls

The article on the siege says the defenders left the fort on 5 April. The British had taken Jhansi.

## What Rose thought of her

Hugh Rose later wrote about the Rani. Wikipedia quotes his battle report, which calls her "the best and bravest of the rebels." It also quotes him saying she was "remarkable for her beauty, cleverness, perseverance [and] generosity to her subordinates."

National Geographic quotes another line: "The Indian Mutiny had produced but one man, and that man was a woman."

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: We found two different sets of words attributed to Rose. They do not conflict, but they are not the same. We have not seen his original reports.</aside>

## Why it matters

The siege of Jhansi lasted about two weeks. It ended with the fall of a city and the loss of thousands of lives.

For the Rani, it did not end there. She went on to fight at Kalpi and Gwalior. But Jhansi was the place she would not give up, and the siege is the reason her name is remembered.
