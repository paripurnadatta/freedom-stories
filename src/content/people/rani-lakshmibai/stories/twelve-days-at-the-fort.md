For several months in 1857 and 1858, Rani Lakshmibai ruled Jhansi. Then a British army came to take it back.

## An army on the road

The British commander was Major General Hugh Rose. His force, the Central India Field Force, left Indore on 6 January 1858. On the way it relieved a town called Sagar, defeated an army at Shahgarh, and took the fort of Banpur.

By the time Rose reached Jhansi, his army was about three thousand men.

Inside Jhansi, the Rani had been getting ready. She had recruited soldiers and had cannon made. On 15 March the Raja of Banpur, who had fought on her side before, came back with three and a half thousand men.

She was still writing to the British in March, hoping to avoid war. Her own troops may have pushed her into fighting.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: did the Rani choose war, or did events choose it for her? One article on the siege says she refused Rose's demand to surrender, which included a threat to destroy the city. Another says she was still sending peace messages days before the attack. Nobody recorded what was said in the palace.</aside>

## The walls

The fort of Jhansi stands on a rock. Its walls were twenty-five feet high. The land around had been cleared, so attackers could be seen from far off. Heavy guns on the fort could fire across the town and the countryside.

Eleven and a half thousand fighters were in the town, with hundreds of tons of supplies.

## The guns begin

The shelling began on 22 March 1858.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: one article gives 22 March as the first day of shelling. The article on the siege starts its story on 24 March.</aside>

The Rani's best gunners were killed early. On 29 March the British made a hole in the wall, called a breach. The defenders quickly blocked it with a barrier of stakes.

## The relief army

On 1 April, an army led by Tatya Tope came to help Jhansi. It had more than twenty thousand men. Rose sent twelve hundred to meet it at the river Betwa.

The British lost fewer than a hundred men. Tatya Tope's army lost over fifteen hundred. It broke and withdrew. Fires set in the forest to slow the British also hurt Tatya Tope's own men.

Jhansi was alone.

## The night of 2 and 3 April

The attack began at three in the morning on 3 April. Two columns went at the south wall. One came through the breach. The other climbed ladders.

The Rani led a counter-attack with fifteen hundred Afghan fighters, but they were pushed back to the fort.

By sunset, the British held the town except for the north-east corner and the fort. Fighting in the streets went on into the next day.

## What a priest saw

One Indian eyewitness wrote about these days. His name was Vishnubhat Godse. He was a priest from a village in Maharashtra. He had walked north in 1857, hoping to be paid at a great religious ceremony, and ended up at Jhansi, where the Rani's father looked after him.

Godse wrote his travel book, *Maza Pravas*, about twenty-four years later. It was printed in 1907. His great-great-granddaughter, Sukhmani Roy Vijh, published an English translation in 2012.

Godse says the walls were broken on 2 April. He writes that the British used strong telescopes to find the fort's only water source, and shelled it. Shells fell on the elephant stables and on the animals of the zoo.

He says the soldiers set fire to the town, starting at a place called Halwaipura. They killed boys and men between five and thirty years old. Women were robbed but not killed, he says.

A British soldier wrote home that he had been told to "spare nobody over 16, except women of course." As many as five thousand people died, one account says. Another gives three thousand. The British lost 343 men, killed and wounded.

<aside class="note"><b>Handle with care</b>: these are sad pages. Many people who were not soldiers died in Jhansi that week. Godse was a priest with his own fears, and he wrote long afterwards. His figure of 60,000 British soldiers is far too high. Other sources give about three thousand.</aside>

## Leaving the fort

On the night of 3 April, the Rani left. She had about three hundred soldiers with her, and her adopted son was tied to her waist. How she got out, nobody can say for sure.

The best-known tale is that she leapt from the wall on her horse, Sarangi, with her son on her back, and the horse died. Most historians think this unlikely.

A simpler account says she left in the night with her son, surrounded by guards, "probably while Rose's cavalry were busy looting."

Other tellers give other ways out. a soldier from India serving under Rose is said to have arranged for a horse to wait below the fort, and she and her son were lowered down to it. Rose's aide, Anthony Lyster, wrote that Rose left a gap of four hundred yards in the line of sentries, as a trap. This is doubtful, since Rose knew she was a fine rider. Godse says she rode a white horse in men's clothes and armour. She shouted "Jai Shankar" as she came down. She left by the north gate, and British horsemen chased her for about three miles.

A British party caught up with her once while she stopped to eat, but she escaped. She reached Kalpi at midnight on 5 April, after riding about 102 miles.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: the tradition says she covered the 102 miles in one night. The more reliable sources put her arrival at midnight on 5 April, which would be a longer ride.</aside>

## Her father

Her father, Moropant Tambe, left Jhansi too. He was caught and executed on 20 April 1858.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Godse tells it differently. He says Moropant was badly wounded and rode to Datia, where a man who sold paan reported him to the ruler. The British brought him back to Jhansi and hanged him in front of the Rani's palace.</aside>

## The fort falls

The defenders left the fort on 5 April. The British had taken Jhansi.

## What Rose thought of her

Hugh Rose later wrote about the Rani. His battle report calls her "the best and bravest of the rebels." He also called her "remarkable for her beauty, cleverness, perseverance [and] generosity to her subordinates."

Another line is also given: "The Indian Mutiny had produced but one man, and that man was a woman."

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: two different sets of words are credited to Rose. They do not conflict, but they are not the same, and the original reports were not available to check.</aside>

## Why it matters

The siege of Jhansi lasted about two weeks. It ended with the fall of a city and the loss of thousands of lives.

For the Rani, it did not end there. She went on to fight at Kalpi and Gwalior. But Jhansi was the place she would not give up, and the siege is the reason her name is remembered.
