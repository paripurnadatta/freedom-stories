Which angle is the big "flagship" story, and why
Angle 6 (A Nine-Year-Old Boy Tied to His Mother's Back) paired with Angle 9 (The Boy
King Who Lived in a Waterfall Cave) is the strongest flagship story for 10-year-old readers. A
10-year-old reader is almost the exact same age that Damodar Rao was (8 to 10 years old)
during the siege of Jhansi and the flight after Gwalior. Seeing Rani Lakshmibai's siege, escape,
and final battle through the eyes of her adopted child—and discovering what happened to him
and the 60 loyal guards who carried him on their backs and hid him in a river cave for two
years—gives young readers an immediate, concrete human connection to 1857–1860.

Groups and Events
  ● Doctrine of Lapse: East India Company policy used by Lord Dalhousie in 1854 to
     annex Hindu princely states whose rulers died without a biological male heir.
  ●
  ● Jokhan Bagh Massacre (8 June 1857): Killing of about 60 surrendered British men,
     women, and children by mutinous sepoys at Jokhan Bagh in Jhansi.
  ●
  ● Siege of Jhansi (22 March–4 April 1858): Twelve-day British bombardment and
     assault on Jhansi by Major General Hugh Rose's forces.
  ●
  ● Battle of Betwa (1 April 1858), Battle of Kunch, & Battle of Kalpi (22 May 1858):
     Battles in Bundelkhand between Hugh Rose's Central India Field Force and the rebel
     armies of Tatya Tope, Rao Sahib, and Rani Lakshmibai.
  ●
  ● Battle of Gwalior / Kotah-ki-Serai (17–19 June 1858): Final major battle of the Central
     India campaign, in which Rani Lakshmibai was killed in combat against the 8th Hussars
     and 95th Regiment of Foot.
  ●
  ● Rani of Jhansi Regiment (1943–1945): All-female combat regiment of the Indian
     National Army formed in Southeast Asia by Subhas Chandra Bose during World War II
     and named after Rani Lakshmibai.

4. Disputes, legends and doubts
1. Year of Rani Lakshmibai's birth (1827 vs. 1828–1830 vs. 1835)
  ● Version A (19 November 1828, or between 1828 and 1830): Encyclopædia Britannica
     lists c. 19 November 1828 as the commonly accepted birth date, and historians Tapti
     Roy and Rudrangshu Mukherjee place her birth between 1828 and 1830 | Encyclopædia
     Britannica | > "Manikarnika Tambe was born in Kashi (now Varanasi) in about 1827 or
     1828 (the year of her birth is disputed, and some historians believe it was later than
     1828, which is the commonly accepted year)." and Wikipedia: Rani of Jhansi | > "The
     historians Tapti Roy and Rudrangshu Mukherjee have argued that the latter account is
     implausible because of chronological irregularities; they place Manikarnika's birth
     between 1828 and 1830."
  ●
  ● Version B (1827): 19th-century British accounts generally placed her birth around 1827 |
     Wikipedia: Rani of Jhansi | > "Her birth year is disputed: British sources tended towards
     the year 1827, whereas Indian sources generally preferred the year 1835."
  ●
  ● Version C (1835): Traditional Indian accounts often gave her birth year as 1835 (which
     would make her 7 years old at her May 1842 wedding and 22 years old at her death in
     June 1858) | Wikipedia: Rani of Jhansi | > "the couple wed, according to Indian sources,
     in May 1842. If the traditional Indian chronology is correct, Manakarnika would have
     been seven years old"
  ●
2. Year of her marriage to Gangadhar Rao (May 1842 vs. 1852) and birth of
her biological son (1851 vs. 1852)
  ● Version A (Married in May 1842; biological son born in 1851): Wikipedia states they
     married in May 1842 and her biological son was born in 1851 | Wikipedia: Rani of Jhansi
     | > "the couple wed, according to Indian sources, in May 1842... In 1851, Lakshmibai
     gave birth to a son amid much rejoicing"
  ●
  ● Version B (Marriage date given as 1842 or 1852; son born in 1852): Encyclopædia
     Britannica notes her wedding date is variously given as 1842 or 1852, and places the
     birth of her short-lived biological son in 1852 | Encyclopædia Britannica | > "The date of
     the wedding is variously given as 1842 or 1852... In 1852 Lakshmi Bai bore a son, who
     died in infancy"
  ●
3. Amount of pension granted by the British in 1854 and later to Damodar
Rao
  ● Rani Lakshmibai's 1854 pension (Rs 5,000 per month / Rs 60,000 per year vs. Rs
     5,000 per year): Wikipedia states she was granted 5,000 rupees per month (which
     equals 60,000 rupees per year, as given in Wikipedia's Damodar Rao article), whereas
     Damodar Rao's translated memoir in Live History India says an annual pension of Rs
     5,000 | Wikipedia: Rani of Jhansi | > "Granted a lifetime pension of five thousand rupees
     per month (or £6,000 yearly) by the new Company superintendent of Jhansi" vs. Live
     History India | > "my mother, Rani Lakshmibai would get an annual pension of Rs. 5000."
  ●
  ● Money held in trust for Damodar Rao (Rs 600,000 vs. Rs 700,000) and his later
     pension: Wikipedia's Rani of Jhansi article says 600,000 rupees was held in trust and
     Damodar Rao received 150 rupees a month, while Damodar Rao's memoir says 7 lakh
     (700,000) rupees was held in trust and he was initially allotted 10,000 rupees annually
     (or Rs 400 in Indore) | Wikipedia: Rani of Jhansi | > "her adopted son Damodar Rao was
     stripped of the 600,000 rupees held in trust for him, and was instead allocated a monthly
     pension of 150 rupees." vs. Live History India | > "there were 7 lakh rupees in the
     treasury in the name of my late father... I was allotted an annual pension of Rs. 10,000"
  ●
4. Did Rani Lakshmibai take part in or approve the 8 June 1857 Jokhan
Bagh massacre?
  ● Accusations of complicity: Tax collector S. Thornton and two disputed witnesses
     claimed she backed the mutineers or promised false safe passage, and historians note
     that items belonging to killed British residents were later found in her palace and her
     father Moropant Tambe took an active part | Wikipedia: Rani of Jhansi | > "S. Thornton, a
     tax collector in Samthar State, wrote that the Rani had instigated the revolt... items
     belonging to the massacred were later found in her palace."
  ●
  ● Denials and evidence of coercion: In two mid-June 1857 letters to Major Erskine (and
     according to a British captain's servant), Lakshmibai stated that the mutinous sepoys
     threatened to burn her palace and kill her unless she gave them men and guns, and F.
     W. Pinkney's official British report reached no clear conclusion | Wikipedia: Rani of
     Jhansi | > "The sepoys subsequently threatened to set fire to her palace and even to kill
     her if she refused to support them... the official report by F.W. Pinkney came to no clear
     conclusion."

5. How did she escape from Jhansi Fort on 3–4 April 1858, and did
Jhalkaribai act as a decoy?
  ● LEGEND — Jumping on horseback ("Sarangi" / "Badal") from the high fort
     ramparts: Popular tradition holds that she leaped on her horse Sarangi from the high
     wall of Jhansi Fort with Damodar Rao on her back, surviving the fall while the horse died
     | Wikipedia: Damodar Rao of Jhansi | > "Rani Laxmibai evaded capture, according to
     tradition, with Damodar Rao on her back jumping on her horse, Sarangi from the fort.
     They survived but the horse died."
  ●
  ● Historical / British accounts of the escape: One British account states a native soldier
     in Rose's army arranged for a horse below the fort so she and her son could be lowered
     down onto it; another by Rose's aide Anthony Lyster claimed Rose left a 400-yard gap in
     the sentry line as a trap; historians consider it most likely she slipped out at night through
     a gate surrounded by 300 guards | Wikipedia: Rani of Jhansi | > "One British account
     holds that a native soldier serving under Rose secretly arranged for a horse to be
     brought to the fort, so that the Rani could be lowered onto it"
  ●
  ● LEGEND / FOLK NARRATIVE — Jhalkaribai's decoy role and death date: While Kori
     oral traditions and 1951 novels describe Jhalkaribai disguising herself as the Queen and
     going to General Rose's camp, historians note she is absent from 1857 British and
     Indian records and is only mentioned as a maid in a 1907 Marathi book. Sources even
     disagree on when Jhalkaribai died: 4 April 1858, 1881, or in old age | Wikipedia:
     Jhalkaribai | > "quotes Mr. Nareshchandra Koli stating her date of death as 4 April 1858.
     Sarala (1999, pp. 113–114) notes that she died in 1881. Varma & Sahaya (2001, p. 305)
     notes that she died as a very old woman"
  ●
6. Date and exact manner of Rani Lakshmibai's death (17 June vs. 18 June
1858)
   ● Date (17 June vs. 18 June 1858): Some historical accounts date her fatal battle at
      Kotah-ki-Serai to 17 June 1858, while others date her death to 18 June 1858 (which is
      observed in India as her death anniversary) | Encyclopædia Britannica | > "Accounts
      differ on whether Lakshmi Bai was killed on June 17 or 18. However, June 18 is
      popularly observed as her death anniversary in India."
   ●
   ● Manner of death: Eyewitnesses and historians agree she died fighting against the 8th
      Hussars near Kotah-ki-Serai, but accounts differ on whether she was shot by a
      carbine/bullet, struck by a Hussar's sabre, or both | Encyclopædia Britannica | >
      "Dressed as a man, she fought a fierce battle in June 1858 and was killed in combat
      near Kotah-ki-Serai. The exact nature of her death is unknown—she may have been
      shot or stabbed by a saber or both."
