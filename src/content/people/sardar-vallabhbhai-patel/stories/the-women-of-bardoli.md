It was about nine at night in a small village. A big crowd had gathered. The men were singing songs of satyagraha. The women were singing an old saint's song. One line of it said, "All our sorrows have ended, now that the Master has come."

Then a line of women, young and old, came up to Vallabhbhai Patel. They bowed to him. They offered sandal paste and flowers. They put a red mark on his forehead. They laid a few coins before him. This went on for about half an hour.

Mahadev Desai was there. He wrote a book about Bardoli in 1929. He says this was embarrassing for Patel, and moving for everyone else. He calls the village Nani Phalod.

One woman put a five-rupee note down. She also handed Patel a little note in her own writing. Desai prints it. It says: "We are doing nothing out of the ordinary. This is a fight for our own interest, and for our hearths and homes." She promised that she and her husband would bear any loss of property. If her husband went to jail, she wrote, she would "cheerfully bid him godspeed."

<aside class="note"><b>Satyagraha</b>: a way of fighting wrong without violence. People refuse to obey an unfair rule, and they accept the punishment calmly.</aside>

## The tax

Bardoli was a taluka in Surat district. A taluka is a group of villages. Desai writes that it had 137 villages. In 1928 the government planned to raise the land tax there.

Land tax was the money a farmer paid each year on his field. It was fixed by a "settlement" that was meant to hold for thirty years. In Bardoli, officers made a new settlement that raised the tax.

The numbers need care. Desai says the Settlement Officer had proposed an increase of 30 per cent. The Settlement Commissioner said 29. The Bombay government said 22. The farmers said even 22 was unfair, because the price of cotton had fallen.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia says the government raised the tax by 30 per cent in 1926 and that the protest was against a 22 per cent rise. Mahadev Desai, writing a year after the events, says 30 was the Settlement Officer's figure and 22 was the government's final one. We follow Desai, who wrote close to the time.</aside>

## "Refuse to pay"

Farmers asked for help from Vallabhbhai Patel, who had worked with Gandhi in Kheda. Patel was careful. Wikipedia says he warned them that refusing the tax could cost them their land and send them to jail. He said he would lead only if all the villages agreed.

On 6 February 1928, Desai says, Patel wrote to the Governor of Bombay. He asked for an independent inquiry. If there was none, he wrote, he would have to advise the people to refuse the tax and "peacefully and quietly suffer the consequences."

There was no real answer. On 12 February, farmers from 79 villages met at Bardoli. Desai says they had already begun signing a pledge. They passed a resolution to refuse the new tax until the old amount was accepted or an impartial inquiry was held. They did so in "solemn silence", after prayers from the Koran and a Hindu hymn.

## Bugles and locked doors

The government tried to collect. Men called talatis, who kept village records, went to take property from those who would not pay.

Desai says every village had volunteers with bugles or drums. When they saw a talati and a village headman coming, they sounded the alarm. Then, he writes, "women stood in front of locked doors laughing at them."

A month earlier, Desai says, women were few at the meetings. At first they "had as yet no leader of their sex." Then Mithubehn Petit arrived, from a well-known Parsi family of Bombay. She and Bhaktibehn Desai and some other women went among the women of the villages. A month later, Desai says, there was no meeting without its full share of women. Often they outnumbered the men.

Dr Hari Desai, a modern writer, tells another story, from a different source. A collector was sent to Sarbhon. Shops were shut. Doors were locked. No one would hire him a taxi. When he got to Sarbhon, young men beat a drum as he came. Every door was shut and the street was empty. He called the village headmen. They said: "The people will not listen to us."

## The name

Sometime that spring, Patel was given a new name. "Sardar" means leader or chief. People still call him Sardar Patel. But who said it first? We found four answers.

First, Wikipedia says the women of Bardoli gave him the title. It does not name a source or a single woman.

Second, Dr Hari Desai says a woman called Bhikhiben, from a village called Akoti, did. He says it was at a public meeting in April 1928, at a village he calls Nani Palsod. He says that she said, "Vallabhbhai, now onwards you are our Sardar." He takes this from a 1990 doctoral thesis by Maganlal Patel. He also says that the writer Rajmohan Gandhi finds that nobody knows who first used the word.

Third, a government article from the Press Information Bureau says a farmer named Kuverji Durlabh Patel gave him the title at an open meeting.

Fourth, Mahadev Desai himself, in 1929, writes: "I do not know who gave Sjt. Vallabhbhai the appellation of the Peasants' Sardar." It was given "spontaneously," he says, and "taken up by every one who heard it." He was there. He did not know.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: These four accounts do not agree. They may not even be about the same moment. One may say "Sardar" while another says "the Peasants' Sardar". We cannot tell who spoke first. The Sardar Patel Trust says only that he earned the title from "the masses". We use that careful wording ourselves.</aside>

## How it ended

The campaign lasted until the summer. Desai says the end came in early August. The government agreed to an independent inquiry. It agreed to give back the lands taken from the farmers and to free the prisoners. The village officers who had quit were taken back.

Not everything was settled. Desai says some penalties were not cancelled. Wikipedia adds that some deep problems of the poorest farmers stayed. But Desai quotes a leader of the time, Lala Lajpat Rai, who called it "a notable triumph of the popular cause."

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia says an inquiry later found the right rise was 6.03 per cent. Mahadev Desai's book, written in 1929, does not give us this figure. We could not check it in a second source, so we do not use it.</aside>

## Why it matters

The most famous name in the story is a man's. But the farmers of Bardoli did the hard part. They signed a pledge. They sounded the bugles. They locked their doors. They stayed calm, as Patel had asked.

Women, who had once not been at the meetings, were soon filling them. One of them wrote a note to Patel that Mahadev Desai thought worth printing. It said that this was their own fight. We do not know her name. But we know what she wrote.
