Imagine a country about to be free, with a big problem nobody has solved. Much of India was ruled by kings. These kings were called princes or rulers, and their lands were "princely states".

The princes were not independent. The British Crown stood above them. This was called "paramountcy". But in 1947 the British were leaving. What would happen to the princely states then?

Lord Mountbatten, the Viceroy, said the number of states was "something like 565". He said paramountcy would end, and that the states would be free in law to join either new country, India or Pakistan. If nothing replaced the old links, he warned, "only chaos can result."

<aside class="note"><b>Paramountcy</b>: the Crown's power over the princely states. It meant the rulers had to follow British advice on big matters such as defence and foreign dealings.</aside>

## A new department

On 13 June 1947, the Viceroy held a meeting. Nehru, Patel and Kripalani came for the Congress. Jinnah, Liaquat Ali Khan and Abdur Rab Nishtar came for the Muslim League. Baldev Singh came for the Sikhs. They agreed that a new government department should be set up to deal with the states.

The Cabinet of the Interim Government agreed on 25 June. A press note on 27 June said the new States Department "will be in charge of Sardar Patel". He was to work with Sardar Abdur Rab Nishtar, from the Muslim League. V. P. Menon was named Secretary. The government planned to split the department in two, so that India and Pakistan would each have one.

<aside class="note"><b>Interim Government</b>: the government of India in the last months before independence. It ran the country while the British were getting ready to leave.</aside>

The White Paper is an official book published in 1950 by the Government of India. All of this comes from it.

## "My personal friends"

The department began work on 5 July 1947. On that day Patel made a statement to the rulers. It is printed in the White Paper.

He began warmly. He wrote that he was "happy to count many as my personal friends."

He reminded them of history. India had been taken over many times, he wrote, because it was divided and its rulers quarrelled. "We cannot afford to fall into those errors or traps again."

He did not pretend the British rule was fair to the princes. About paramountcy, he said, "In so far as paramountcy embodied the submission of States to foreign will, I have every sympathy with this demand." The demand was that states should be free again.

But he asked them to think about what freedom meant. He asked only for accession on three subjects: defence, foreign affairs and communications. Those were things, he said, in which "the common interests of the country are involved." On everything else, "we would scrupulously respect their autonomous existence."

<aside class="note"><b>Accession</b>: when a state formally joined India. It signed a paper called an Instrument of Accession.</aside>

## No "domination"

Patel then spoke about the princes' worries. He wrote that "there appears a great deal of misunderstanding about the attitude of the Congress towards the States." He promised that it was "not the desire of the Congress to interfere in any manner whatever with the domestic affairs of the States." They were "no enemies of the Princely Order."

He said he would not conduct relations "in any manner which savours of the domination of one over the other." If there had to be domination, he wrote, "it would be that of our mutual interests and welfare."

Then came a line that people still remember: "I suggest that it is therefore better for us to make laws sitting together as friends than to make treaties as aliens."

He also gave a warning. If the states did not cooperate, "the alternative ... is anarchy and chaos which will overwhelm great and small in a common ruin."

## A draft paper

A few weeks later, on 25 July, Lord Mountbatten held a special meeting of the Chamber of Princes. The White Paper prints his speech.

He said he had been relieved when Patel took over the States Department and made "a most statesmanlike statement." He told the rulers about a draft Instrument of Accession that he had sent around. The states would accede on three subjects only. They would take on no money costs. And the Central Government would have no authority "to encroach on the internal autonomy or the sovereignty of the States" in other matters.

Mountbatten was honest about one thing. He said he had "still to persuade the Government of India to accept" this draft. And he gave a date: "if you are prepared to come, you must come before the 15th August." That was the day power would pass to India and Pakistan.

He also said: "You cannot run away from the Dominion Government which is your neighbour." He advised the rulers to accede on the three subjects. At the end he named a Negotiating Committee of rulers and ministers to discuss the terms. It included the Maharaja of Patiala, the Nawab of Bhopal and the Maharaja of Baroda, among others.

## What happened next

The White Paper says the negotiations were finished in about a week. It says that "barring Hyderabad, Kashmir and Junagadh, all the States in the geographical limits of India had acceded to the Indian Dominion by 15th August 1947."

Those three states are other stories. Wikipedia says that Junagadh and Hyderabad were brought into India by force and by a vote, and that Jammu and Kashmir was handled separately. We do not tell those stories here.

## A team, not one man

It is easy to tell this as a story about one person. The official papers say otherwise. They name Nehru, Mountbatten, Menon, the Muslim League's Nishtar, and the princes on the Negotiating Committee. The White Paper itself says that "the task of conducting negotiations with the Princes was entrusted ... to Lord Mountbatten."

Wikipedia says Patel worked with V. P. Menon, Mountbatten and Nehru. A Constitution of India page says Patel is known as the "Iron Man of India" for bringing in "565 princely states".

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The number of states is not agreed. Mountbatten said "something like 565." Wikipedia says that sources give 550, 565, and over 600. The Sardar Patel Trust says "nearly 560." The count depends on what you call a state, since some were tiny estates. We use Mountbatten's words in his own speech, and we do not give a single total.</aside>

## Why it matters

Patel wrote to rulers who were worried about their future. In his letter he asked, he reminded and he warned. He said plainly what he wanted from them, and what he would leave alone.

Not every state agreed at once, and this story does not claim they did. The later parts are told elsewhere. But the official papers show that the first step was a letter that offered friendship, and a short list of three subjects.
