Everyone knows who walked to Dandi in March 1930. Fewer people know who was already in jail when the walk began.

It was Vallabhbhai Patel. He was arrested in a village called Ras, in Gujarat. The Nehru Archive gives the date as 7 March 1930. The Sardar Patel Trust says it was "the first time" he was arrested, and that it was a few days before the Dandi march.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia says Patel was arrested "as Gandhi began" the march. The Nehru Archive and the Trust put it earlier, just before the march. Gandhi's march started on 12 March. We follow the Nehru Archive's date of 7 March.</aside>

## A leader's reaction

The Nehru Archive prints a statement by Jawaharlal Nehru. It is dated 8 March 1930, the day after the arrest, and it was given to the press in Allahabad. The Archive says Patel was sentenced at once to three months in prison, with a fine. It does not state the charge.

Nehru called the arrest and sentence "a significant and happy omen." By that he meant it showed that the country was "in the thick of the fight." He said that Patel's firmness, which had made Bardoli famous, would give strength to the movement from his place in Sabarmati Jail.

Nehru also made a plan. If Gandhi were arrested, he wrote, there should be an immediate hartal across India. Schools and shops that supported the freedom struggle were asked to close.

<aside class="note"><b>Hartal</b>: a day when people stop work and shut shops as a protest.</aside>

We did not find a source we could open that says what Patel did in his last days of freedom. What the Nehru Archive does show is that his arrest made news, and that Nehru treated it as a sign that the movement had begun.

## A year later in Karachi

In March 1931 the Congress held its yearly meeting at Karachi. The Trust says Patel was elected president of the Congress and "presided over the annual session at Karachi." The Constitution of India website says the same.

At that session the Congress passed a Resolution on Fundamental Rights, on 31 March 1931. The Nehru Archive says that Jawaharlal Nehru drafted it and Gandhi made changes. It does not say who moved it.

## What the resolution asked for

It sets out what the Congress wanted any future Indian constitution to promise to every person. The Archive has the words, and some of them are plain enough to read as they are:

- "Freedom of speech and press."
- "Freedom of association and combination."
- "Freedom of conscience and the free profession and practice of religion, subject to public order and morality."
- "No disability to attach to any person of religion, caste or creed in regard to public employment."
- "No civic bar on account of sex."
- "Equal rights to all citizens of access to, and use of, public roads, public wells and all other places of public resort."

It also asked for "religious neutrality on the part of the state." The Archive adds that it included economic aims such as a living wage and free primary education.

<aside class="note"><b>Fundamental rights</b>: basic rights that nobody, not even a government, can take away.</aside>

## Why it matters

The Karachi list is more than ninety years old. The Congress was still fighting for freedom when it wrote the list. Yet it already named equal access to public roads and wells, and it said no one should be kept out of a job because of religion, caste or sex.

Patel was the president of the session. The resolution was mainly the work of others, as the Archive says. But a leader who had gone to jail in the first days of the Salt Satyagraha was in the chair for it. That is the link between the two events in his story.
