Far out in the Bay of Bengal lie the Andaman Islands. In British times, Indians feared them. They called them Kala Pani: the black water.

To be sent across the black water meant exile, far away from your family and your home.

On 23 January 1933, a ship reached the islands with a group of young prisoners. One of them was Batukeshwar Dutt, the friend of Bhagat Singh. He was 22 years old.

## A prison built for loneliness

The prisoners were taken to the Cellular Jail in Port Blair.

The British had built it between 1896 and 1906. Seven long wings, each three floors high, spread out from a tall watchtower in the middle. Along the wings were hundreds of tiny cells.

<aside class="note"><b>Solitary</b>: alone. In the Cellular Jail, each prisoner had a cell to himself, so that he could not talk to anyone.</aside>

Every cell was solitary. The jail was built to keep prisoners apart, so that they could not talk to each other.

Batukeshwar arrived with several comrades, among them Bejoy Kumar Sinha, Kamalnath Tiwari, Kundan Lal, Dr Gaya Prasad and Mahavir Singh.

## Refusing food

The jail had been closed for some years, and opened again in 1932. Batukeshwar's group was among the first prisoners to be sent there after that.

Conditions in the jail were harsh. A historian, Prabal Saran Agarwal, writes that the prisoners slept on wooden floors, in cells with little light or fresh air. There were insects and disease, the food was poor, and there was little medical care. The prisoners asked again and again for things to improve. Nothing changed.

So the prisoners did what Batukeshwar and Bhagat Singh had done before in Lahore. They went on hunger strike. They refused to eat until things changed.

According to Agarwal, three men led the strike: Mahavir Singh, Dr Gaya Prasad and Batukeshwar Dutt. As punishment, the leaders were shut away in dark cells.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: one account says the hunger strike began in February 1933. Wikipedia says it began on 12 May 1933. Sources also disagree on how many prisoners took part: one says 33, another says many more joined in support.</aside>

The jail officers tried to break the strike. On about the sixth day, they began to force-feed the prisoners, forcing milk into them.

<aside class="note"><b>Force-feeding</b>: making a person take food against their will, often through a tube. It can be very dangerous.</aside>

## Three who did not come home

The force-feeding went terribly wrong.

Mahavir Singh was one of Batukeshwar's comrades. He had helped Bhagat Singh in Lahore, and he had arrived in the Andamans on the same ship as Batukeshwar. On 17 May 1933, he died. He was 28.

Two accounts say the milk went into his lungs instead of his stomach. His body was not given back to his family. An attendant later said it had been put into the sea.

Two more prisoners died: Mohan Kishore Namadas on 26 May, and a young revolutionary from Bengal named Mohit Moitra on 28 May 1933.

The news of their deaths reached the mainland. There were protests in big cities across India.

<aside class="note"><b>How did they die?</b> Most accounts say they died because of the force-feeding. One article says two of them died of pneumonia, a lung illness.</aside>

The strike went on. It lasted 46 days.

## What they won

In the end, the British gave way. The prisoners' demands were met.

They got better food and better light. They could write and receive letters. They got medical care. They were allowed to run the kitchen. And they were given newspapers and magazines to read.

## A university in a prison

Reading changed everything.

The prisoners started to study together. They read, argued and debated about freedom, and about what kind of country India should become. Batukeshwar and his friends even started a magazine, written entirely by hand.

On 26 April 1935, a group of prisoners formed a study group called the Communist Consolidation. They called their classes "a veritable university of freedom fighters". They studied the ideas of Karl Marx, and the Russian Revolution of 1917.

<aside class="note"><b>Veritable</b>: real, true. They meant that their prison had become a real university.</aside>

One account says that Batukeshwar was at the centre of these discussions, together with Bejoy Kumar Sinha, Shiv Verma and Jaidev Kapoor. Many of the young men who studied there later became leaders in Indian politics.

## Calls to close the jail

Back on the mainland, people were angry about the Andaman prisoners. Gandhi and the poet Rabindranath Tagore both campaigned for the jail to be closed.

In July and August 1937, the prisoners went on hunger strike again. This one lasted 36 days. It caused an uproar in Bengal and Bihar. Agarwal writes that people outside supported the strike partly because they did not want "another Mahabir Singh". The British gave in. They agreed to move the political prisoners back to jails on the mainland.

By 18 January 1938, all of them had been moved.

## Home, but not well

Batukeshwar was first taken to a jail in Delhi. His health got worse. Dr Rajendra Prasad helped to have him moved to Bankipur jail in Bihar. Gandhi and others asked for him to be freed.

On 8 September 1938, Batukeshwar Dutt was released. He went to live with his brother in Patna.

His years in jail had damaged his health badly. Even so, he did not stop. In 1942 he joined the Quit India Movement, and was jailed again, in Motihari. There, too, he went on hunger strike.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: some accounts say he caught tuberculosis, a lung disease, in prison. Wikipedia says he caught it after he was released.</aside>

## The jail today

During the Second World War, Japan captured the Andaman Islands in 1942 and used the jail for its own prisoners. The British returned in October 1945. The Japanese pulled down two wings of the jail, and two more were knocked down after independence to make room for a hospital.

Today, only three of the seven wings of the Cellular Jail still stand, with the tall watchtower in the middle. In 1979, it was declared a National Memorial. Outside it stands a statue of Mahavir Singh.

## Why it matters

The Cellular Jail was built to make prisoners lonely, silent and broken. Batukeshwar Dutt and his comrades refused. They starved themselves to win books, and then they turned their prison into a place of learning.

Three of them paid with their lives. The ones who survived came home thinking harder than ever about what freedom should mean.
