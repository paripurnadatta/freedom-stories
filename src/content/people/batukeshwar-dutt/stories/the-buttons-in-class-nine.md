Imagine you are in Class 9. You come to school one morning with a small button pinned to your shirt. On it is the face of your hero.

Now imagine that a police officer is writing a report about it.

That really happened, in Delhi, in 1930.

## Heroes on a button

By 1930, Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt were famous all over India. In April 1929, they had thrown harmless bombs in the Central Legislative Assembly in Delhi, and then waited calmly to be arrested.

In prison, they went on a long hunger strike. Newspapers wrote about them every day. Their photographs were printed on posters.

Young people especially admired them. Some of them wanted to show it.

## A letter from the police

On 5 November 1930, a senior officer of the Delhi police wrote a letter. He worked for the Criminal Investigation Department, or CID. That was the part of the police that watched people the government saw as dangerous.

He sent the letter to J. N. G. Johnson, the Deputy Commissioner of Delhi. That was the most senior British official in the city.

<aside class="note"><b>CID</b>: the Criminal Investigation Department, a branch of the police. In British times, it also spied on freedom fighters and their supporters.</aside>

## What worried him

The letter was about a school in New Delhi, called M. B. High School.

The officer wrote that the boys in Class 9 were wearing buttons. On the buttons were pictures of revolutionary leaders, including Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt.

That was not all. Photographs of political and revolutionary leaders had been put up inside the classrooms too.

## Who was to blame?

The officer thought the school was being far too relaxed about all this. He even suggested that the teachers might have encouraged it.

He blamed two people in particular: the headmaster, and the second master, a teacher named Dola Ram.

By the time the letter was written, the headmaster had already ordered the photographs to be taken down. That had happened on 3 November, two days earlier.

## Why it matters

We often think of the freedom struggle as something grown-ups did. But this letter shows that children were part of it too.

A few small buttons, pinned on by boys of fourteen or fifteen, were enough to worry the police of a great empire.

The British could take pictures off a classroom wall. But they could not take a hero out of a child's heart.

Today, almost a hundred years later, that old police letter still exists. It tells us how much those boys loved Bhagat Singh, and his quiet friend from Bengal, Batukeshwar Dutt.
