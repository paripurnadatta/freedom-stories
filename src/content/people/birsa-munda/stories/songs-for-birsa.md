The nation took about a century to honour Birsa Munda, Joseph Bara says. But in the villages around Khunti he was a popular leader long before that, and people made up songs about him.

He was "reminisced in Mundari folk songs and folk tales". One song was written down by two scholars in 1969. It goes:

> For your fellowmen, Birsa, you stood up, Birsa;
> for your motherland, Birsa, you died.

Another song remembers the day he was taken away. Daniel Rycroft prints some lines from it. It says, "Oh Birsa, they arrested you... Oh Birsa, on your hand is the iron chain." It ends, "I grieve that they took you away."

These songs are short and sad. They do not tell us exact dates or numbers. But they tell us how the people felt.

## Birsa's followers and Gandhi

Birsa's followers did not forget him. Bara says some of them wrote down what they remembered, but kept it among themselves. One of them was Bharmi Munda, who had been Birsa's "spokesman and book-keeper". He was sentenced to five years in prison. He wrote his account some time between about 1910 and 1920.

When Gandhi came to Chotanagpur during the non-cooperation movement, many Birsaites joined him. A song from then links the two men:

> By spinning cotton thread, you got swaraj, O, Gandhi, you got it.
> By causing a tumult, you got Chotanagpur, O, Birsa, you got it.

<aside class="note"><b>Swaraj</b>: self-rule, the freedom of a people to govern themselves.</aside>

## The gate at Ramgarh

In March 1939, a leader named Jaipal Singh took charge of the Adivasi Sabha. A special number of its magazine printed a picture of Birsa with handcuffs. In 1940 the Indian National Congress met at Ramgarh. Bara says Jaipal Singh set up a Birsa Munda gate there.

Jaipal Singh also wrote about Birsa in April 1940. He said he had known little of Birsa as a boy, though he came from Khunti. Forty years before, he wrote, people "called him Bhagwan, Dharti Abba and an Avatar".

After independence, books were written about Birsa. Bara says Suresh Singh's book of 1966 used old papers, songs and the notes of Birsa's followers. It led to more books. In 1977 the Sahitya Akademi published a novel about Birsa by Mahasweta Devi, called *Aranyer Adhikar*. It was translated into Hindi as *Jangal Ke Dawedar*. Wikipedia says it won the Sahitya Akademi award in 1979.

## How the nation remembers

In recent years, honours have come quickly. Bara writes that in 2000, the Government of India chose 15 November, Birsa's birthday, to start the new state of Jharkhand. In Parliament there is a 14-foot statue of Birsa and a portrait.

On 10 November 2021, Wikipedia reports, the Union Cabinet decided to mark 15 November each year as *Janjatiya Gaurav Divas*, the Day of Tribal Pride. The main event is held at his samadhi, the place where he is remembered, at Kokar in Ranchi. An airport, a jail, a university and a stadium have also been named for him, Wikipedia says.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The birth date is not agreed. Jharkhand started on his official birthday, 15 November. Bara also reports church records that give 22 July 1872. We have not seen the church book, so we cannot say which is right.</aside>

## Why it matters

Birsa was a local hero for a long time before the nation noticed him. Some argue that people should see him as more than a local leader. He fought for his own people's land and rights. And he did so against a government that ruled the whole country.

You can decide for yourself. But the songs came first. The first people to honour Birsa were villagers who sang.
