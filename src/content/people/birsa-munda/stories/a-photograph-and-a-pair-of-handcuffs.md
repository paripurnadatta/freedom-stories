Look at the picture above. A young man stands in the middle. He wears a turban and a long cloth. Soldiers stand on both sides. Under the picture, a line is printed: "Birsa Munda captured and conducted to Ranchi."

This is one of the only old photographs of Birsa Munda. It was first printed in a book in 1912. The book was *The Mundas and Their Country* by Sarat Chandra Roy. Roy said the photograph was taken after Birsa's last arrest, in 1900.

A scholar named Daniel Rycroft thinks Roy was wrong. His idea changes the whole story of the picture.

## The first arrest

To follow Rycroft's idea, we need to go back to 1895. Birsa was about twenty years old and living at Chalkad. Many Mundas came to him. They called him "Bhagwan", which means God. They also called him "Dharti Aba".

The officials did not like it. Rycroft says one reason was the forest law of 1882. It had taken away the Mundas' rights over forest land. He also says that Birsa told his followers not to plant rice, because his powers would grow the crop. Officials feared that less rice would mean less tax.

The first try to arrest Birsa came on 9 August 1895. It failed. The police were three men: a head constable named Luchman Lal and two others. Birsa's father and family outnumbered them.

The second try worked, on a night in August. There are two stories about it.

In one, from Rycroft, a sub-inspector from Khunti walked into Birsa's room. Birsa was asleep, with turmeric on his body. He fought when the handcuffs were put on his wrists. Then he was taken away without more trouble.

In the other, from Roy, the District Superintendent of Police rode into the village on an elephant, with twenty armed police. Roy says the officer gagged Birsa's mouth with his handkerchief and carried him off before the followers woke.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The two accounts do not match on who made the arrest or how. They give different dates too. Rycroft says the night of 23 August. Wikipedia says 24 August. Rycroft's account comes from the police records through a later writer, K. S. Singh. Roy's is from 1912, and he does not say where he got it. We cannot tell which is right.</aside>

## What followers did

Roy writes that on the morning after, the followers found their leader gone. They remembered something Birsa had said. He had said that if the government took him, he would come home by himself on the fourth day. Roy writes that seven thousand people, "it is said", gathered at Chalkad on that day. This is a rumour, told by Roy long after. We do not know the real number.

Another rumour grew in Ranchi. Roy says that on the day Birsa was brought to the jail, a small mud-walled barn there fell down under the weight of grain sacks. The story that reached the Munda villages was different. It said the jail walls fell the moment Birsa walked in. Roy says this helped keep many followers loyal after the first prophecy failed. He tells it as a rumour, and we do too.

The followers demanded Birsa's release. The government held an open trial, with the crowd watching. The trial began at Khunti on 24 October. It was stopped when some of Birsa's followers seemed to threaten violence. Father Hoffmann, who knew the Mundas well, explained that their words had been misunderstood. Those men were let go.

The trial moved to Ranchi. In November 1895, Roy writes, Birsa was sentenced to two and a half years of hard prison work. Rycroft and Wikipedia say two years.

<aside class="note"><b>Rigorous imprisonment</b>: a prison sentence with hard labour.</aside>

Birsa told his followers about the jail in a way that stayed with them. He called it the "whitewashed house". Munda homes were made of clay. The jail had white walls, as the houses of rich outsiders did.

## Out of jail

Birsa was freed in 1897, before the end of his term. Roy says it was at the time of Queen Victoria's Diamond Jubilee. Then, as Roy tells it, he took his followers to the old Hindu temple at Chutia, which had once belonged to the old rulers. People in the town surprised them at night. Roy says Birsa slipped away in the dark.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Roy says this happened in the last week of January 1897. Joseph Bara and Wikipedia say January 1898. We use no date.</aside>

After that, Roy says, Birsa kept out of sight for nearly two years. His followers said the Bhagwan had left the world for a while.

## Back to the picture

Rycroft looked at the photograph itself. The young man is strong and healthy. He wears a turban and earrings. Roy had said the picture showed him in 1900, when he was ill. A later writer, Suresh Singh, even printed it under the name "Sick Birsa". Rycroft does not think a sick man would look this way.

He also points to a second picture of Birsa, standing between two policemen with a tent behind. He suggests that both pictures were taken in August 1895. He thinks an Anglican missionary, the Reverend Lusty, may have overseen the photographing on the road to Ranchi. This is Rycroft's guess. He says so himself, with the words "I suggest".

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The photograph's date is not settled. Roy's book says 1900. Wikimedia Commons says 3 February 1900 too. Rycroft argues for August 1895. We do not know which is right. No one has found the photographer's own note.</aside>

## The missing handcuffs

Now look at a second picture. In 1990 a museum in London held a show called *The Raj*. It showed a copy of a portrait of Birsa, as a picture of "tribal rebellion". Rycroft says this copy does not show the handcuffs. Without them, he says, the picture no longer shows a man being arrested. It shows only his features.

Others used the handcuffs on purpose. In 1939, a magazine for Adivasis printed a picture of a jailed Birsa with handcuffs on its first page. Bara writes about it. Jaipal Singh, a Munda leader, led the Adivasi Sabha then.

## Why it matters

A photograph feels like proof. But even a photograph has a story behind it. Who took it? When? Who copied it, and what did they leave out?

Here we have a young man in a turban, held by soldiers. We know that much. The date is argued over, and one copy lost his handcuffs. Good history means saying so.
