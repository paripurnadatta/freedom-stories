Is it better to ask politely, or to fight? The Mundas of Chotanagpur tried the polite way first. They tried it for a very long time.

## A new kind of letter

Joseph Bara is a scholar who has studied Birsa Munda. He writes that the British took control of Chotanagpur in 1772. They brought in new kinds of tax, and landlords who worked for the government. The landlords and their men treated the farmers harshly.

The people rose up in arms more than once. After one such rising, in 1831 and 1832, the government set up an inquiry for the first time. It heard what the people were complaining about. A few mild changes followed. But Bara says that after a short while, the old troubles came back.

The Mundas then tried something different. Christian missionaries in the area showed them how to take a case to the courts. Bara says the missionaries hoped to win followers by doing so. In a few cases, farmers did win back land they had lost. This gave people hope.

Schools came too, some run by the government and some by missions. Bara says that education taught people how British government worked. It taught them to read the official papers.

## The Sardars

Out of this came a group of leaders called Sardars. Most of them could read a little. From 1858, Bara says, they ran a long, lawful movement. They wrote petitions to the officials. They filed cases in court. This went on for four decades.

<aside class="note"><b>Sardar</b>: a leader or chief. The Sardars in this story were Adivasi community leaders, long before the name was given to Vallabhbhai Patel.</aside>

<aside class="note"><b>Petition</b>: a written request sent to people in power.</aside>

The Sardars' claims grew stronger over time. They asked for an end to landlords. They wanted the old system back, where village headmen called mundas, and chiefs of groups of villages called mankis, ran things.

How did the British answer? They carried out land surveys between 1859 and 1879. Bara says the work was done badly, and it did not meet the main complaints. It led to a law in 1879, the Chota Nagpore Landlord and Tenant Procedure Act. Bara says it "fell far short" of what the Adivasis expected. By 1890, he says, the movement had left ordinary people "high and dry".

## Other troubles

There were more troubles besides. The Forest Act of 1882 took away the Mundas' rights to use the forest, Rycroft writes. Sarat Chandra Roy says the government's own report for 1897–98 admitted that landlords in the district took "all they can in the way of rent and labour" from farmers. They threatened to throw the farmers off their land if they objected.

Then came a famine in 1897, cholera in 1898, and a failed crop in 1899.

## What changed with Birsa

Bara says the Sardars were tired of their long wait. When Birsa began to speak, many of them joined him. Bara says Birsa went further than the Sardars did in three ways.

First, the Sardars never questioned the British as supreme rulers in their petitions. Birsa named the British, the missionaries and the landlords as enemies.

Second, the Sardars kept to letters and courts. Birsa told his followers to arm themselves.

Third, the Sardars spoke of the Maharaja of Chotanagpur with mixed feelings. Birsa wanted a free Munda land, which he called a "Munda disum", with no outside rulers at all.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Some writers say Birsa's rising was only about farmland, and was not a fight against the British at all. Bara disagrees. He says it was a fight against British rule too. Missionaries and officials of Birsa's own day called him a fanatic. We have tried to read all of them with care.</aside>

## Why it matters

Bara's point is simple. Armed struggle came after a long time of asking. Letters, courts and inquiries had all been tried. In his words, the Mundas turned to arms because their complaints had not been "attended to seriously" for over a century.

You may disagree that fighting was the right answer. But the story is worth knowing in full. It began with petitions, and it did not begin with bows.
