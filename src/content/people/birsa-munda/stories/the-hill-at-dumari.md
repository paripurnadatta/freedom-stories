It was the middle of the night, on top of a hill. About sixty or eighty people were there. A man sat on a stone with a cloth spread over it. He faced east. The others sat round him. After a while the moon rose.

This is how one man described the meeting in a court of law. His name was Ratan Munda, and he was a witness for the government. The man on the stone was Birsa Munda. The time was October 1899.

## Why they were meeting

Birsa asked the people what troubles they had. Some of them answered. They said that zamindars, jagirdars and thikadars were pressing them hard.

Their land was the problem. The Mundas of this country held their villages as the families who first cleared the forest. Over many years, money-lenders and outsiders had bought up the landlords' estates. These new owners tried to take away the old rights and to raise the rent.

The years before had been hard too. Sarat Chandra Roy, who wrote about the Mundas in 1912, says a famine came in 1897. It was the worst anyone could remember. Cholera followed in 1898. In 1899 the winter crop failed completely.

<aside class="note"><b>Zamindar, jagirdar, thikadar</b>: a landlord, a man who held land given by a ruler, and a contractor who collected rent.</aside>

<aside class="note"><b>Khuntkatti</b>: the Munda way of holding land. The families who first cleared a village's forest and fields kept special rights over them, and so did their children's children.</aside>

Since 1858, Munda and Oraon leaders called Sardars had asked the British for fairness. They sent petitions. They filed cases in court. The historian Joseph Bara says this went on for about forty years and brought little. By 1899 many people were out of patience.

## What was said on the hill

The witness said Birsa told them to make bows and arrows, and other weapons. According to this man, Birsa also said that the Rajas and Hakims and Christians would not be able to hurt them. Guns, he said, would not harm them, because the bullets would turn to water.

Please remember who is speaking. This is one man's statement in a trial. He was a prosecution witness, and the government was trying Birsa's followers. We cannot check his words against any other witness. But two writers, Roy and Bara, both print the same statement, so we know it was recorded in court.

Bara adds a point about the place. Birsa, he says, usually met his followers on hilltops. Officials found it hard to climb up there, and it was easy to defend.

## Christmas Eve

Meetings went on for weeks in different villages. On Christmas Eve in 1899 the plan was carried out. Roy says there were attacks in several places at once.

Arrows were shot near a church in Ranchi, and a carpenter was wounded and died later. A German merchant was shot dead in a village. At Burju, a police constable and four village watchmen were killed. Several missionaries were shot at. Father Carbery was hit in the chest by an arrow and survived. Father Hoffmann was shot at twice, and both arrows missed.

These were frightening nights, and people died. We tell it plainly, without praise. Bara says something fair about it. He writes that the target was meant to be the people who took advantage of the Mundas. It was not meant to be every outsider or every Christian. He says Hoffmann himself wrote that the rebels later swore not to harm any Mundas, even Christian ones.

## Soldiers on the road

On 5 January 1900, two policemen were killed at a place called Etkedih. Wikipedia reports this. On 7 January, Roy says, about three hundred Mundas with bows, axes and spears attacked the police station at Khunti. One policeman was killed and some houses were set on fire.

The Commissioner of the division, Mr. Forbes, and the Deputy Commissioner, Mr. Streatfield, went to Khunti at once. They took 150 soldiers from the Native Infantry.

<aside class="note"><b>Native Infantry</b>: soldiers in the British Indian army, who were Indians led by British officers.</aside>

## 9 January 1900

On 9 January the officers found the Mundas on Dumari Hill, three miles south of a place called Saiko. Roy writes that the Mundas had built many stockades, which are fences of wood, to defend the hill.

Roy says that Mr. Streatfield first tried to talk. He explained the position in the Mundas' own language and asked them to put down their arms. According to Roy, they mocked his words and refused. The order to fire was given.

The defenders ran down a deep gully into the jungle. Then the soldiers saw what had been behind the stockades. There were women and children. There were stores of clothes, food and cooking pots.

Roy quotes a government report for the year 1899–1900. It says four Mundas were found killed and three wounded. The bodies of three women and a boy were found in the jungle.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The number of dead is not agreed. The official report that Roy quotes counts four Mundas, plus three women and a boy. The scholar Daniel Rycroft says there were "more than twenty Birsaite deaths". Wikipedia says the number is unknown. The place name is also spelled in different ways: Dumari, Dumbari and Dombari. Rycroft calls the spot "Sail Rakab hill, near Dombari". We do not give one number, because we could not find a source that settles it.</aside>

Birsa got away into the forest. A reward of five hundred rupees was offered for him. Soldiers and police went through the villages and took many people to Ranchi for trial.

## Two ways of telling it

Roy wrote in 1912. He used the government's papers, and his words about the Mundas are often harsh. He calls them "fanatics". Bara, who writes in 2022, says old reports like this were written by officials and missionaries, who had reasons to see Birsa as a danger. He argues that armed struggle was a last step, taken after a century when polite petitions had not worked.

You can see why both writers matter. Roy tells us what the officers saw and recorded. Bara asks what the Mundas themselves were trying to do.

## Why it matters

The hill is a small place, and the fight there was short. But the days around it changed how the government saw the Mundas. Later, the authorities ordered a survey of the land and made new laws. That story comes in another chapter.

What we can say about 9 January is simple. People who had tried petitions for years took up bows. Soldiers with guns met them. Women and children were on that hill. Some of them did not come back.
