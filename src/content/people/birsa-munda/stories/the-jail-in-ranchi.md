After the hill at Dumari, Birsa Munda ran. He went into the forests. The government offered five hundred rupees to anyone who could tell them where he was.

## The arrest

On 3 February 1900, Birsa was arrested in Jamkopai forest, near Chakradharpur, according to Wikipedia. He was traced to the next district, Singhbhum, and brought to Ranchi under guard, writes Roy.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: a photograph caption on Wikimedia Commons says he was arrested near Santara village in West Singhbhum. It may be the same place under another name. Wikipedia and the Commons caption agree on 3 February. Neither Roy nor Rycroft gives the day. We have not seen a police record.</aside>

The Lieutenant-Governor of Bengal sent a telegram to the Home Department on 6 February. On 8 February the news reached Sir Arthur Godley in London.

Birsa's followers sang of that day. Rycroft prints some lines from a song, taken from a book by K. S. Singh: "Oh Birsa, they took you by Ranchi road... Oh Birsa, for the land you suffered."

## The trials

Hundreds of Birsa's followers had been caught too. They were tried in the courts.

A letter from the Deputy Commissioner of Ranchi gives the numbers. 460 people were accused in 15 cases. Sixty-three were found guilty. One was sentenced to death, 39 were sent away for life, and 23 were jailed for up to fourteen years. Six people died in custody. One of them was Birsa.

The trials became national news. Surendra Nath Banerjee, editor of *The Bengalee*, criticised the government for delays and for what he called legal cover-ups, Rycroft writes. A paper called *The Statesman* wrote on 25 March 1900 that the prisoners had no lawyers to defend them. These are newspaper claims. We have not seen the court papers.

Birsa's helper Bharmi Munda was sentenced to five years of hard labour, Bara says.

<aside class="note"><b>Different numbers</b>: Roy says eighty-seven people were sent for trial at the Sessions Court. Two were sentenced to death for murders in the revolt. On appeal to the High Court, a few were let off and some sentences were cut. Rycroft says "300 other Birsaites" were tried. The numbers may be counting different things, such as police cases and court cases. We do not choose one.</aside>

## 9 June 1900

Birsa fell ill in jail. He died on 9 June 1900. He was twenty-four.

The jail's head, Captain A. R. S. Anderson, gave an official cause. Rycroft says it was perhaps dysentery and cholera.

A church group disagreed. The Evangelical Mission of Chotanagpur suggested that Birsa may have been poisoned. They said it could have been done by the police, or by Munda leaders who wanted to keep their plans secret.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: the government said illness. The mission said poison. We found no proof of poison in any source we opened, and no medical report either. So we cannot say what caused his death.</aside>

## What came after

His death ended the last of the Munda risings in Chotanagpur, Roy says. The government decided to find out what the land rights really were. A survey began in 1902. A new law came in 1903, and a bigger one in 1908.

Each year the official event on his birthday is held at his samadhi, his memorial place, at Kokar in Ranchi.

## Why it matters

Birsa lived only about twenty-four years. We cannot even be sure of the year of his birth. But his short life was remembered by people who loved him, and argued over by people who wrote about him.

A young man was held in a jail and he did not come out alive. The reason is still not settled.
