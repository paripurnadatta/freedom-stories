How many times can a family move house while a child grows up? For Birsa Munda, the answer was at least four.

The historian Joseph Bara writes that Birsa's family was always looking for work. They had no land to hold them. Landlords had taken much of what Munda families once held. So they went from village to village, wherever there was a living to be made.

Wikipedia names some of the places. They lived at Kurumbda, near Birbanki. Then they lived at Bamba, and then at Chalkad. At one time, Wikipedia says, Birsa stayed for about two years at the home of his mother's brother, at Ayubhatu. The reason was that the family was so poor.

## A name from a day of the week

Birsa's father was Sugana Munda. His mother was Karmi Hatu. Wikipedia names an elder brother, Komta, and two sisters, Daskir and Champa. A younger brother was Pasna.

Wikipedia says the boy was named after the day of the week he was born. It was a Thursday. That was a Munda custom.

When was he born? Here the sources disagree, and the question is old.

<aside class="note"><b>Three versions</b>: Wikipedia says 15 November 1875, in Ulihatu village. That is the date India keeps as his birthday. Joseph Bara says a scholar named Mathew Areeparampil read church records at the Burju mission. They show a birth on 22 July 1872 and a baptism on 11 August 1872. Bara says Birsa's close helper, Bharmi Munda, also gave 1872. Sarat Chandra Roy, writing in 1912, says Birsa was about twenty-one in the summer of 1895, which points to about 1874. We could not look at the church book ourselves. So we do not choose.</aside>

## School and a new name

Wikipedia says a teacher called Jaipal Nag taught Birsa at Salga. He recommended Birsa for the German mission school.

Bara says that Birsa's family had been drawn to Christianity. His father worked for the mission as a catechist, a teacher of the faith. The boy was baptised and given the name Daud. Wikipedia gives it as Birsa David, or Daud.

<aside class="note"><b>Baptised</b>: made a member of the Christian church in a special ceremony. People often took a new name at it.</aside>

He studied at Chaibasa from 1886 to 1890, say Wikipedia and Daniel Rycroft. There he learnt a little English, Bara says. Wikipedia says his father took him out of the school. Soon after, the family left the mission and went back to their old Munda faith.

Bara says Birsa was also drawn to ideas from other places. For a time he came under the spell of a lower-caste local guru named Anand Panre. Daniel Rycroft adds that Birsa spent time at a Vaishnav ashram, a kind of religious school. There he began to worship the tulsi plant and wore a sacred thread.

So Birsa's faith was a mixture. It had something from Christianity, something from Hindu teachers, and something from Munda belief. Bara says Birsa made his own religion out of them.

## The English words

Missionaries wrote about Birsa while he was alive, and they did not like him. One of them was the Reverend G. H. Lusty, who wrote in 1896. Bara reads Lusty and finds a few small stories.

Once, Lusty says, Birsa counted in English up to ten to cure a sick baby. At other times he used odd words as charms, such as "Pulter, pewter, Walter". This is what Lusty wrote, and Lusty did not like Birsa. We cannot check it. But it shows that Birsa used the English he had learnt at school.

## The lightning

How did Birsa become a leader? Here is a story. Bara calls it a legend.

Sarat Chandra Roy tells it like this. In the early rains of 1895, Birsa was in the jungle with another young man. A storm came. A flash of lightning lit Birsa's face. His friend saw it go red and white. Birsa said it was a message from God.

<aside class="note"><b>Legend</b>: a story passed down by people, which may not be true in every detail.</aside>

Roy wrote this in 1912, long after the event. He gives no source for it.

## Chalkad in the rains

After that, Roy says, people came to Chalkad from everywhere. A mother brought a baby she thought was ill. Birsa breathed on the child and chanted. Roy says the baby "was found all right from that moment".

Soon, Roy writes, the lame, the blind and the sick came in crowds. It was the rainy season. There were no spare huts. People camped under trees and under bamboo umbrellas stuck in the ground. They stayed until their rice ran out.

Roy's words about Birsa are unfriendly. He calls the cures "strange incantations". We are not saying Birsa healed anyone. The sources only tell us what people believed and what they did.

## What Birsa taught

Roy lists the teaching. The Mundas should worship one God only. They should give up the animal sacrifices they made to many spirits. They should keep clean, live good lives, and wear a sacred thread. Roy also says people were told not to eat animal food.

His followers called him "Bhagwan", which means God. They also called him "Dharti Aba". Roy translates this as "Father of the World". *Dharti* is the Hindi word for earth, which is why many people today say "Father of the Earth".

Roy also says that Birsa once named a day when fire would fall from the sky. People were to come to Chalkad in new clothes. Nothing happened, Roy writes. He tells it with scorn. Bara says missionary writers of that time, such as Lusty, called Birsa a "fanatic" and a "young monkey". Bara says these writers were Birsa's opponents, so we should read their words with care.

Roy himself writes that Birsa had "remarkably attractive features for a Munda", a face "intelligent and smiling". He also calls him "pensive and thoughtful". Bara says Birsa was cheerful and sensitive to other people's suffering.

## Why it matters

We know less about Birsa as a child than we would like. The dates are argued over. Some stories come only from people who disliked him. Many come from followers who loved him.

What we do know is clear. He was born poor. He went to a mission school. He came home and told his people to hold on to their own faith and their own land. By 1895, thousands had come to hear him. Soon the government would take notice too.
