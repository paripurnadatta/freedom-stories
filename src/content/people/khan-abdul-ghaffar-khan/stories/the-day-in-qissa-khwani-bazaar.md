Qissa Khwani Bazaar means "the Storytellers' Bazaar". It is a market street in the old city of Peshawar. On the morning of 23 April 1930 it was full of people.

That spring, people all over India were breaking the government's salt law. Almost the whole population of Peshawar city broke it too. In Peshawar, the Congress committee had decided to stand outside liquor shops and ask people not to buy. This is called picketing. It was to begin on 23 April.

## The morning

Early on 23 April the authorities arrested nine leading Congress men. The city closed its shops in protest. At about nine o'clock a police sub-inspector came to the Congress office with two more arrest warrants. The two leaders went with him. A crowd followed them to the Kabuli Gate police station.

The gates of the station were shut. After a while the crowd began to disperse, shouting slogans.

this account of the morning comes from a Congress inquiry committee led by Vithalbhai Patel. The committee was not allowed to enter the province, so it met at Rawalpindi instead and heard 79 witnesses. A summary is printed in D. G. Tendulkar's biography.

## The armoured cars

Then two or three armoured cars came at great speed from behind. They drove into the crowd without warning. Some people were run over. Some were hurt and a few were killed on the spot.

The people carried no weapons: "no lathis, hatchets, stones or bricks". Some stood in front of the car and begged it to stop.

An Englishman came on a motorcycle and crashed into an armoured car, and the car ran over him. One car also caught fire. The Deputy Commissioner then ordered the soldiers in the cars to open fire.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia gives a different picture. It says the crowd was loud and that stones were thrown, and that a British Indian Army dispatch rider was killed. The Congress committee says the crowd was unarmed and the rider was hurt in an accident. Both cannot be right on this point, and the government's own papers were not available.</aside>

Both sides agree on what came next. The crowd was asked to leave. It said it would, if the troops and the armoured cars went away and if it could carry away the dead and the hurt. The authorities refused. The crowd stayed.

The committee wrote that the people "were prepared to receive the bullets". Then firing began again.

## How many?

This is the hardest question, and nobody has a firm answer.

- *The Times* of 28 April 1930 gave the official number: twenty dead.
- The Patel committee said that "a rough estimate given by some witnesses" was two to three hundred killed, and many more wounded.
- Nationalist sources spoke of several hundred. One summary gives 400.
- Another page on Abdul Ghaffar Khan gives 200 to 250.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: the numbers run from 20 to 400, and the firing lasted a different time in each telling. The Patel report says the second round lasted "more than three hours". Wikipedia says six hours. A newspaper quoted by Gene Sharp says it lasted from 11 in the morning until 5. This story gives no single number. Many people were killed, and the real figure is not known.</aside>

Volunteers of the Khilafat movement carried about sixty bodies from the lanes to their office. Some of the wounded were taken to the hospital. The government gave no help for first aid.

Olaf Caroe, an officer of the Frontier government, wrote about it later. He was asked to arrange burials at night, to stop a new riot at a big funeral. He wrote: "During the night in this way we sent away seven or eight bodies in lorries."

## The soldiers who would not shoot

Soldiers of the Royal Garhwal Rifles were sent to Peshawar. They were Indian soldiers in the British army. Their unit "had always been distinguished for its loyalty".

But a platoon of it refused to go against "the unarmed and peaceful crowd". They were arrested and disarmed.

At their court martial, they said: "We will not shoot our unarmed brethren, because India's army is to fight India's enemies without. You may blow us from the guns, if you like."

<aside class="note"><b>Court martial</b>: a trial of soldiers by a military court.</aside>

Seventeen men were sentenced. One was sentenced to be sent away for life. Another got fifteen years. The rest got three to ten years of hard prison.

Wikipedia says two platoons refused, and that their NCOs were sentenced to up to eight years. It names Chandra Singh Garhwali as the leader of one platoon. The court's records were not seen.

You can read more in the story of the Garhwalis at Peshawar on this site.

## Utmanzai, 13 May

Three weeks later, soldiers came to Abdul Ghaffar Khan's own village of Utmanzai. This is told by an eyewitness, whose account is printed in the Patel report. Again, it is one side's account.

The Deputy Commissioner came with troops. He ordered the Khudai Khidmatgars in their office to take off their red uniforms. They refused. Their commander told them to leave the building. They were beaten, and their clothes were torn off.

One of them, Mohamed Naquib Khan, ran towards his house to get a revolver. His commander stopped him. "Is your patience exhausted so soon," he said, "that you are now going to retaliate by violence? You swore to remain non-violent till death!" The man came back. He was arrested.

The Deputy Commissioner then said, "Any more Red Shirts left?" Nobody spoke. Then a Khan of the village, Mohamed Abbas Khan, ran home. He dipped his clothes in a vat of red water and put them on. He walked back with his servants and said, "Here are the Red Shirts."

After this, the red uniform could not be banished, however hard the government tried.

## Why it matters

What happened in the bazaar is told in accounts that are partly one-sided, and the numbers differ. When a government keeps people out and holds back news, the truth is hard to find.

But some things are plain. A crowd was asked to go home and would not leave its dead. Soldiers were ordered to shoot, and some said no. Men who had promised never to hit back kept their promise, even when it was very hard. Many Pakhtuns paid a heavy price for that promise.
