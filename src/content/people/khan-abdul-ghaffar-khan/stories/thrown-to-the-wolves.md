It was a night in early May 1947, in New Delhi. Mahatma Gandhi was about to leave the city. Abdul Ghaffar Khan, whom Gandhi called Badshah Khan, had a fever.

He still sat down to press Gandhi's arms and legs, as he did every night. Gandhi told him not to. Tendulkar records his answer: "It is last day, so let me. It will make me well."

He stayed awake until half past ten. When someone asked him not to tire himself, he said, "Before long we shall become aliens in Hindustan."

To understand why he said this, we need to see what was happening to India that spring.

## A land that might be divided

For years the British had promised to leave India. By 1947 the question was how. The Muslim League, led by Muhammad Ali Jinnah, wanted a separate country, Pakistan, for Muslims. The Congress had opposed this for a long time.

The Khudai Khidmatgars had stood with the Congress for many years. Tendulkar writes that the Muslim League "regarded the Khan brothers as mortal enemies". The brothers were Abdul Ghaffar Khan and his elder brother Dr Khan Sahib, who led the government of the Frontier province.

If India was divided, the Frontier would probably go to Pakistan. And then these Pakhtun leaders would be at the mercy of the Muslim League.

Abdul Ghaffar was very sad in those days. He said, "We shall be outcasts in the eyes of both." He meant India and Pakistan. And then he added, "But I do not worry so long as Mahatmaji is there."

On the last day of Gandhi's stay, he said what we read above. He also said that the end of their long fight would be "to pass under the domination of Pakistan, away from Bapu, away from India". Gandhi heard about it from Manu. Tendulkar says Gandhi replied: "Verily Badshah Khan is a fakir. Independence will come, but the brave Pathan will lose his."

<aside class="note"><b>Fakir</b>: a holy man who lives simply. Gandhi meant it as praise.</aside>

On 7 May Gandhi left for Calcutta. Abdul Ghaffar saw him off at the station. "Mahatmaji, I am your soldier," he said. "Your word is law to me."

## The meeting of 3 June

On 3 June 1947 the Congress Working Committee met. The plan from the British was now known. India would be divided. In the Frontier province, a vote of the people, called a referendum, would decide whether to join Pakistan or not.

Abdul Ghaffar was stunned. For several minutes he could hardly speak. Then he reminded the committee that he had always stood by the Congress. He said that if the Congress deserted him, his enemies would laugh. Even his friends would say that the Congress cared for the Frontier only while it needed it.

Sardar Patel and C. Rajagopalachari strongly favoured the referendum, says Tendulkar. In the end the Working Committee accepted both the partition and the referendum.

Abdul Ghaffar then said, in Tendulkar's words:

> "We Pakhtuns stood by you and had undergone great sacrifices for attaining freedom, but you have now deserted us and thrown us to the wolves... Let it be on Pakhtunistan or Pakistan."

He argued that the Pakhtuns had already won the elections on this very question. Since India had given them up, he said, a vote on India or Pakistan made no sense.

He left the meeting "numbed and dejected", Tendulkar says. He sat on the steps and kept saying "Toba, toba", an expression of sorrow.

<aside class="note"><b>Referendum</b>: a vote in which people answer one question, usually "yes" or "no", or choose between two options.</aside>

## What the others said

This is Abdul Ghaffar's own memory, as Tendulkar quotes it. He says only he and Gandhi opposed the decision. He says Sardar Patel told him he was "worrying over nothing". He says Maulana Azad sat near him and said, "You should now join the Muslim League."

We have not found the other leaders' own account of this meeting in the sources we opened. So we cannot say how they would tell it.

What we do know is why leaders of the Congress accepted partition. Tendulkar says Nehru and Patel accepted it on the understanding that by giving Pakistan to Jinnah they would hear no more of him. He quotes Nehru as saying privately, "By cutting off the head we will get rid of the headache."

In a radio broadcast that evening Nehru said they all disliked dividing India. But they could not let India go on bleeding, and an operation was better.

Gandhi's answer to Abdul Ghaffar is also in Tendulkar. Gandhi was sure that if the Frontier did not get "a square deal", or if the Khudai Khidmatgars were oppressed, India would feel in honour bound to help. He said he would advise the Indian government to treat it as a cause for war. He told Ghani, Abdul Ghaffar's son, not to worry about his non-violence: "I am non-violent, the Government is not."

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Abdul Ghaffar felt deserted. The Congress leaders felt they were avoiding a worse fight. Gandhi promised that India would stand by the Khudai Khidmatgars. We have only Abdul Ghaffar's side in his own words.</aside>

## The vote and the boycott

The referendum was held in July 1947. Tendulkar says the voting began on 6 July and the results came on 20 July. In his account, 289,244 people voted for Pakistan and 2,874 for India. He says this was about half of all the voters in the province.

The Khudai Khidmatgars did not vote. Tendulkar writes that the boycott "took place quietly and in good order". He calls it the last powerful sign of the Khudai Khidmatgars, and says it was futile.

The Khudai Khidmatgars objected to the question itself. The vote had no choice of becoming independent or joining Afghanistan.

Earlier, in June, the movement had passed what Wikipedia calls the Bannu Resolution. It asked that Pashtuns should have a choice to have an independent state of Pashtunistan. Wikipedia says the British refused.

Abdul Ghaffar himself said the vote was unfair. He told Tendulkar about registrations that he said were fraudulent and about an officer who told him his company was taken three times to the polling booth. These are his own claims. We did not find another source to check them.

## After the vote

In September 1947, says Wikipedia, the Khudai Khidmatgars met at Sardaryab. They said they would regard Pakistan as their own country. They also said they would cut their link with the Congress.

Abdul Ghaffar later told the prime minister of Pakistan that Pashtunistan was only "a name" for the Pashtun province inside Pakistan. By the 1960s, support for an independent Pashtunistan had almost disappeared.

## Why it matters

It would be easy to pick one side in this story. A man who had been jailed for years for India's freedom felt abandoned. The leaders who agreed to partition felt they had no good choices.

What the sources let us say is simple. Abdul Ghaffar Khan stood by the Congress for many years. He stayed true to non-violence. And when Partition came, his people were left on the other side of a new border, with little say in what it meant for them.
