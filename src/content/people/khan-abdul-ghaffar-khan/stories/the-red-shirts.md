Imagine a farmer in a field near Peshawar. He is ploughing. Then a line of men comes walking down the path. Their shirts, trousers and turbans are all one deep colour, a dark brownish red.

D. G. Tendulkar, who wrote a long book on Abdul Ghaffar Khan, says the colour "attracted the eye at once". The farmers left their ploughs in the fields and came to look at the red-clad men.

Who were they? And why did they wear red?

## A people who fought each other

The men belonged to the Pakhtuns, also spelt Pathans. They live in the hills and valleys of the North-West Frontier, along what is now the border of Pakistan and Afghanistan.

Abdul Ghaffar Khan was a Pakhtun himself. Tendulkar quotes his own description of his people. He said they had family feuds, quarrels and old enmities. Whatever they earned, he said, was "squandered" on harmful customs and on court cases. He said that the Pakhtuns believed in violence, "and that too not against aliens but their own brethren".

He did not blame the British for everything. He thought his people had to change themselves first.

<aside class="note"><b>Feud</b>: a long quarrel between two families, often handed down from father to son.</aside>

## The Servants of God

Abdul Ghaffar Khan and his friends talked for a long time. Then they started a new group. They called it Khudai Khidmatgar, which means "Servants of God".

The idea was simple. God needs no service. So the way to serve Him is to serve His people, without asking for anything back.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: When did the group begin? Abdul Ghaffar Khan, quoted by Tendulkar, says "in September 1929". Wikipedia's page on him says November 1929. Wikipedia's page on the group says March 1930. We follow his own words but cannot tell which date is right.</aside>

Anyone who joined had to take an oath. In Abdul Ghaffar Khan's words, as Tendulkar prints them, it said:

> "I shall never use violence, I shall not retaliate or take revenge, and I shall forgive anyone who indulges in oppression and excesses against me. I shall not be a party to any intrigue, family feuds and enmity, and I shall treat every Pakhtun as my brother and comrade."

The oath went on. The volunteer would give up bad customs. He would live simply. He would not be idle. He would expect no reward. And he would be "fearless and be prepared for any sacrifice".

Think how hard this was in a land of feuds. A man who was hit would not hit back. A man who was insulted would forgive.

## Why red?

This is how the colour came about. Abdul Ghaffar went from village to village talking to the Pakhtuns. His companions found that their white clothes got dirty easily. So they decided to dye them.

One of them took his shirt, trousers and turban to a local tannery. A tannery is a place where animal hides are turned into leather. He dipped the clothes in a liquid made from pine bark, which the workers used for the hides. The cloth came out a dark brownish red. The others did the same.

So the volunteers became known as the Red Shirts.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Tendulkar gives this practical reason: white clothes got dirty. Wikipedia tells the same dirty-clothes story. It adds that red was also a sign that the movement shared the revolutionary and socialist ideas of other movements against foreign rule. We cannot say which came first. The sources agree that red became the group's colour.</aside>

## How the Red Shirts were organised

Abdul Ghaffar had "a genius for organizing his people". Every village had a committee called a jirga. Villages were grouped into bigger committees. At the top was the Provincial Jirga, which one biographer calls "the unofficial parliament of the Pakhtuns".

The volunteers were a separate body. They were led by a Commander-in-Chief, whom Abdul Ghaffar chose himself. This was because discipline mattered most in this group.

They worked for nothing. They even paid for their own uniforms. Men wore red and women wore black. They had bagpipe and drum bands. They kept order at meetings and helped villagers in trouble. They drilled and went on long marches, like soldiers.

But they carried no guns, no knives and "not even a lathi", says Tendulkar. A lathi is a long wooden stick.

Tendulkar sums up the aim. The movement wanted to teach the Pakhtuns hard work, thrift and self-reliance. It wanted to give them self-respect, and a fear of God that "banishes all fear".

On the march they sang. One song, in Tendulkar's translation, began: "We are the army of God, by death or wealth unmoved."

<aside class="note"><b>Jirga</b>: a council of elders among the Pakhtuns. It meets to talk over a problem and decide it together.</aside>

## "What are you doing? Stop it."

The British officers noticed. Tendulkar says the Deputy Commissioner of Peshawar, Mr Metcalfe, sent for Abdul Ghaffar. Abdul Ghaffar refused to go. Then the Chief Commissioner sent an order, and he went.

The Chief Commissioner said, "What are you doing? Stop it."

Abdul Ghaffar answered that this was basically a social movement, not a political one. He said that the government itself should have started it. "I am doing your work," he said, "you should extend your help and co-operation."

The officer replied, in Tendulkar's account, that he accepted this for now. But what guarantee was there that Abdul Ghaffar would not use the Pakhtuns against the government later?

Abdul Ghaffar said such a guarantee rested on trust. "You trust us and we shall trust you."

Abdul Ghaffar also said he felt "a revolution is in the offing", like a mighty flood. The government did not take his word. In the next stories you will see how the Khudai Khidmatgars and the Congress party came together.

## How big was it?

At first the group was small. Tendulkar says that until April 1930 the Khudai Khidmatgars were "not more than 500". Later the numbers grew a great deal. We have not found a reliable figure for the later years, so we do not give one.

## Why it matters

Most people think of freedom fighters as soldiers or speakers. Abdul Ghaffar Khan did something different. He asked a people known for fighting to give up revenge. He did it before he asked them to face the British.

The Red Shirts began as a group for schools, clean villages and peace between families. History then pushed them into a much bigger struggle. You will read about it in the next stories.
