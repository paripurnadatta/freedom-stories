Some friends in Bombay had an idea in 1934. They wanted Abdul Ghaffar Khan to be the next president of the Indian National Congress.

On 4 September 1934, he told them no. D. G. Tendulkar prints his statement. He said he understood their kind wish. They hoped to help Hindu-Muslim unity by honouring a Muslim, and to honour the sacrifices of his province.

But he wrote: "I am only a humble soldier and it is my ambition to end my days not as a general but as a soldier."

He added that his services were "all too brief" to deserve the honour. He asked the friends to drop the idea, and suggested they find other ways to help his province.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Tendulkar's account shows this statement in September 1934. Another passage in the same book, and Wikipedia, put the offer of the Congress presidency earlier, in 1931. We do not know whether the offer came more than once. The refusal itself is not in doubt.</aside>

## At Wardha

When he made that statement, he and his brother were guests at Wardha, with Gandhi. They had not met Gandhi for three years. Tendulkar says that they lived with him, ate and prayed with him every day, and shared the plain food of the ashram.

Gandhi often asked Abdul Ghaffar to read the Koran at the evening prayer meeting.

## "Do not call me Frontier Gandhi"

A few weeks later, on 2 October 1934, he was in Calcutta. The students of Bengal gave him a welcome at Albert Hall. Tendulkar quotes what he said:

> "I request you not to call me Frontier Gandhi, because there should be only one Gandhi. And if there be two, then inevitably there would be quarrel. Mahatma Gandhi is our general and there should be one general only."

He went on: "I am not fit for the praise you have showered on me." He said the praise was due to the non-violent method, which had changed his people. The Pathans, he said, had once fought for small things every day. Now they had learnt to hold back, even when they were fired on.

<aside class="note"><b>General</b>: the commander of an army. Abdul Ghaffar used it to mean Gandhi was the leader of the whole freedom movement.</aside>

## Where the name came from

Tendulkar says the name was used warmly by admirers and mockingly by rivals. He says it would seem to come from the time when Abdul Ghaffar was in Gujrat jail in the Punjab. There he had read Gandhi's life story with care. He fasted once a week and kept silent one day a week.

Jawaharlal Nehru wrote that Abdul Ghaffar was known by many names: "Pride of the Pathans" and "the Frontier Gandhi", among others. Nehru said the rest of India saw him as the symbol of the courage of a brave people.

Tendulkar says the name "Badshah Khan", meaning King Khan, had been used by his own people since 1921. Mahadev Desai, who visited him in 1938, wrote that they revered him as their king.

## Gandhi comes to the Frontier

In 1938 Gandhi finally visited the Frontier himself. Tendulkar has a whole chapter on this first visit. The picture above shows one of the public meetings.

By then, Tendulkar says, the Frontier had a Congress government under Dr Khan Sahib. It had made the Pakhtu language a compulsory medium for primary schools, released political prisoners and repealed the harsh Frontier Crimes Regulation.

## Why it matters

Abdul Ghaffar Khan had a large following of his own. He could easily have kept the name Frontier Gandhi and used it. He did not. He said the praise belonged to the way of non-violence, not to him.

He also did not claim the top post in the Congress. He followed Gandhi on his own terms. Later, in 1947, he would feel that the Congress had let him down. You can read that in "Thrown to the wolves".
