"What shall I tell Allah," said the jailor, "if He asks me why I made you grind corn?"

The jailor was an old Muslim, a former sepoy. He was talking to a prisoner in a cell in Dera Ismail Khan. The prisoner was Abdul Ghaffar Khan. The jailor had fourteen hundred prisoners in his jail, and this was the only one he called "one man of God".

This story comes from Abdul Ghaffar Khan's own words, as D. G. Tendulkar prints them in his book. It is a memory told long after the events, so it is what he remembered, not a court record.

## Why he was in jail

The British officials had heard about his schools. Tendulkar says they asked him to promise that he would stop. What guarantee was there, they said, that the schools would not be used against the government?

"You must trust me," said Abdul Ghaffar. The officials refused. He was arrested and, on 17 December 1921, sentenced to three years of hard prison under a special law for the Frontier, the Frontier Crimes Regulation.

<aside class="note"><b>Rigorous imprisonment</b>: a jail sentence with hard labour, such as grinding grain by hand.</aside>

## The corn and the chakki

In the first jail, in Peshawar, he was kept alone in a cell. He says the cell faced north and the sun never came in. It was bitterly cold. He had three old blankets and a mat. The jailor was a kind Hindu, who did not make him grind or put him in fetters.

After two months he was moved to Dera Ismail Khan. Here, he says, he was brought in fetters. The next day he was given twenty seers of corn to grind on a stone hand-mill called a chakki. Every prisoner in that cell block did the same.

<aside class="note"><b>Fetters</b>: iron rings put round a prisoner's ankles. <b>Seer</b>: an old unit of weight, about a kilogram. <b>Chakki</b>: a stone hand mill.</aside>

The old jailor came to him. He said, "Don't grind." Abdul Ghaffar stopped. But when the jailor left, he began again.

The jailor looked in through the hole in the door and saw him. He came back and asked why. Abdul Ghaffar pointed to a murderer grinding corn in front of him. He said that this man did it for his crime, and that his own cause was a holy one, so he should grind for it.

The jailor then ordered that he be given flour, which was already ground, instead of grain. He added a request: please tell a lie to the superintendent, or I will lose my job.

Abdul Ghaffar answered: "I do not want you to lose your job. Give me corn to grind. I cannot tell a lie."

He also turned down food the jailor offered from his own house. He would not take milk that was not on his jail diet.

## The offer he refused

The deputy jailor, a man named Gangaram, took bribes from prisoners. Abdul Ghaffar says Gangaram's agents came to him. They said the people of Peshawar were ashamed that he was in a cell, and they would pay a bribe to get him out of it.

He said no. "Bribing is not desirable," he told them. He also spoke to poor constables about taking bribes. One said he could not make ends meet otherwise. Abdul Ghaffar told him it was wrong. The man, he says, resigned.

Later the jail superintendent, an Englishman, came to the cell. Abdul Ghaffar showed him the vegetable and said that even a cat would not touch it. He pointed to a prisoner in chains and asked how people like himself were treated in the superintendent's own country. The next day he was given a different task, making envelopes.

## Dera Ghazi Khan

Gangaram wanted him out of the workshop, since the bribes had dried up. Abdul Ghaffar was moved to Dera Ghazi Khan jail. He was taken by van and train in chains. At one station a Hindu officer took charge of him, took off his handcuffs and said, "Have a stroll."

In this jail most of the prisoners were Hindus and Sikhs. They respected him, he says. There he met political prisoners from the Punjab. He also says that in jail he exchanged views with Lala Lajpat Rai and other workers of the Congress and the Khilafat.

He read the Gita, the Guru Granth Sahib and the Bible there for the first time. He says he did it so that he could understand and value his companions' friendship. He admired the Sikhs' slogan: "Let me lose my head, let my body perish, but not my Sikh faith."

The jail diet at Dera Ismail Khan had made him ill. He lost 55 pounds and had scurvy. In Lahore, a doctor named Dr Premnath treated his teeth. When Abdul Ghaffar offered to pay, the doctor said, "You are jailed because of your love for the country. I cannot match your sacrifice, let me do this much."

## Why it matters

This was before the Red Shirts. Abdul Ghaffar Khan would not lie or pay a bribe to make his own jail life easier. And in a prison full of Hindus and Sikhs, he took the trouble to read their holy books.

His own account is the only source for most of these details. But it shows the kind of man his followers were later asked to follow. He asked people to live by a hard rule, and he tried to live by it himself.
