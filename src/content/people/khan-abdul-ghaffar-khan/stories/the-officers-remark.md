In Utmanzai, the village where Abdul Ghaffar Khan grew up, many people thought schools were dangerous. D. G. Tendulkar says the local mullahs even made up a rhyme about it. In Tendulkar's translation: those who learn at school do it for money, and will find themselves in hell.

His father, Behram Khan, was a powerful and well-liked Khan of the village. He did not listen to the mullahs. Tendulkar says that his elder son, Khan Sahib, was the first youth in the whole of Hashtanagar to be sent to a school. Abdul Ghaffar followed. He joined the Edwardes Memorial Mission High School in Peshawar.

<aside class="note"><b>Mullah</b>: a Muslim religious teacher. <b>Khan</b>: here it means a landowning family head in a Pakhtun village, not just a name.</aside>

## The glamour of the uniform

In 1906, Khan Sahib went to Bombay to study medicine. Abdul Ghaffar stayed at school. He had a servant, Barani Kaka, who kept telling him how splendid it was to be an army officer. Barani Kaka talked about the smart uniform and the sword at the side, and about leading well-trained soldiers.

In the end, says Tendulkar, the boy was impressed. He wrote to the Commander-in-Chief of India and asked for a commission in the army. He did not ask his parents first.

The answer came while he was sitting his matriculation exams. He had finished half the papers. He was told that a commission was granted and that he must report to the recruiting officer the next morning at ten. He left the exams and went.

He was posted to the Guides at Mardan. This was a famous army unit, and rich and respected families fought to get their sons in. Tendulkar says Abdul Ghaffar was taken because he was "a smart and impressive youth". He was six feet three inches tall. His father was "immensely pleased".

<aside class="note"><b>Commission</b>: an official paper that makes a man an officer in the army.</aside>

## One sentence

One day, Tendulkar says, Abdul Ghaffar went to Peshawar to meet a friend who was a cavalry officer. The friend wore no cap, and his hair was parted in the English way.

While they were talking, a British young officer came by. He saw the parting and was angry. He said: "Well, damn Sardar Saheb, you too aspire to be an Englishman!"

The friend's face went pale. He said nothing back.

Abdul Ghaffar felt deeply insulted. He decided he would not join the army and serve the British.

He looked back on his own feelings later. Tendulkar quotes him: "The military was not without glamour." He said he had flattered himself that he was fit to look like Englishmen and stand as an equal beside them. "But Allah had willed it otherwise."

## What it cost him

His father was bitterly against the decision. He was "greatly displeased" that his son threw away such a rank.

Abdul Ghaffar wrote a long letter to his brother. Khan Sahib was then studying in England. Khan Sahib wrote to their father: "You should not coerce him or be displeased with him."

After that he tried several places. He went to Campbellpur, then tried to study Arabic elsewhere, and then went to Aligarh as a day student. His brother suggested he go to England to become an engineer, because he was good at mathematics. His father gave him money for the trip.

But his mother cried and would not agree. She had been told that people who go abroad never come back, and that he might turn Christian. One of her sons had already gone far away. He loved her, and he did not go.

## Why it matters

Abdul Ghaffar "owed much" to a British teacher, the Rev. E. F. E. Wigram, who was headmaster of his school. Abdul Ghaffar said that Wigram and his brother, a doctor, served the people with love. He said that their example taught him to give his life to service.

So his story is not "Indians good, British bad". He admired a British teacher. He refused to serve an army where an officer could mock an Indian for the way he combed his hair. One lesson came from kindness. The other came from an insult. Both shaped the man who later led the Red Shirts.
