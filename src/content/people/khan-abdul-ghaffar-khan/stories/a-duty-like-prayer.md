In some villages of the Frontier, people were told that schools were wicked. D. G. Tendulkar writes that the mullahs first announced that any parent sending a child to a mission school would be thrown out of the community. Later they said: let the boys go to school, but do not let them learn English.

Abdul Ghaffar Khan did not agree. He told the mullahs that the Prophet had said, "Go in quest of knowledge even unto China." It was better for people to study in schools run by the British, he said, than to stay unable to read. "When you ask the people not to go to the schools started by the British, you should open your own schools."

He tried to persuade the mullahs, Tendulkar says, without success. He is quoted as saying: "When God could not make these mullahs understand, how could I?"

<aside class="note"><b>Mullah</b>: a local Muslim religious teacher.</aside>

## A school in Utmanzai

So he and his friends decided to open schools themselves. Tendulkar says that in 1910 he and Maulvi Abdul Aziz opened a school at Utmanzai, his village. Wikipedia says he was twenty at the time.

A prominent religious teacher from a nearby village, the Haji Saheb of Turangzai, had started his own schools in 1911. He helped Abdul Ghaffar and his friends. Together they formed a group to spread schools in the villages.

Between 1915 and 1918 he toured about five hundred villages.

## The Azad School

Soon after the Congress meeting at Nagpur, in 1921, Abdul Ghaffar started the Azad High School in Utmanzai. Tendulkar lists its teachers' names. There was not enough money to pay them well, and trained teachers would not take small salaries. So Abdul Ghaffar taught the students himself.

The lessons were in Pakhtu, the language of the people. Pupils learned the faith of Islam and the history of the Pakhtuns. Weaving was also part of the school day.

The police, says Tendulkar, tried to frighten the teachers. When that failed, they offered them better-paid jobs elsewhere.

## The Chief Commissioner and the old Khan

The school was only six months old when the Chief Commissioner, Sir John Maffey, sent for Abdul Ghaffar's father, Behram Khan.

The officer told the old man that the school was anti-British. He asked why his son was visiting village after village setting up schools. He told Behram Khan to ask his son to stay at home like the others, or "you both will have to face the consequences".

Behram Khan was fond of the British, who called him "chacha". He used to say that the British had come as angels sent by God for people's good. He took his son aside and gently said: "What the others are not doing, you should not do. Sit comfortably at home."

Abdul Ghaffar answered with a question. Suppose all the other people stopped saying their prayers, he asked, would his father tell him to stop too?

"Certainly not," said the father. "I would never have you give up your religious duties, no matter what others may do."

"Well, then, father," Abdul Ghaffar said, "this work of imparting national education is like that. As namaz is a duty, so also the education and service of the people is a duty."

"I see," said his father. "You are right. If it is duty, then do it."

Behram Khan then told the Chief Commissioner that a religious duty could not be given up by his son.

<aside class="note"><b>Namaz</b>: the Muslim prayer said five times a day.</aside>

## The officials' answer

The officials did not give up. According to Tendulkar, the rulers said they wanted a guarantee that the group would not be used against the government. Abdul Ghaffar said, "You must trust me." They said no. They wanted him to apologise and give a security that he would not do it again.

"Give a security that I shall cease to love and serve my people?" he asked.

Shortly after this he was arrested. On 17 December 1921 he was sentenced to three years of hard prison. You can read about that jail time in "The prisoner who ground the corn".

## Why it matters

We know this story from Abdul Ghaffar Khan's own memories as Tendulkar records them. It may have been polished with time. But the main thing is clear. A father who loved and trusted the British took his son's side, because his son gave him a reason he understood.

For Abdul Ghaffar Khan, a school was a part of freedom. He told his father that serving and teaching the people was a duty, just like prayer.
