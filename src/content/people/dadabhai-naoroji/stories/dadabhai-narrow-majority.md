Imagine you want to be a member of Parliament in a country that is not your own. You are sixty years old. And the Prime Minister says in a speech that people like you cannot win.

This happened to Dadabhai Naoroji. This is the story of how he lost, and then won.

## The first try: Holborn, 1886

Naoroji had lived in Britain for many years. In 1886 he went there again, to try for a seat in the House of Commons. The Commons is the part of the British Parliament whose members are chosen by voters.

Dinyar Patel, who wrote a book about Naoroji, gives the dates in his timeline. Naoroji reached London on 12 April 1886. On 18 June, a local Liberal party group chose him as their candidate for Holborn. On 5 July he lost. The Conservative candidate, Francis Duncan, won 3,651 votes. Naoroji had 1,950.

Manu Pillai says that Florence Nightingale was among those who backed him. Naoroji lost heavily, but he did not give up. Patel's timeline says he had first thought of standing for the Commons as early as 1872.

<aside class="note"><b>Liberal and Conservative</b>: the two big parties in Britain at that time. They often disagreed about how to rule the empire.</aside>

## The Prime Minister's remark

In August 1888, Patel says, Naoroji was picked as the Liberal candidate for a different London area, Central Finsbury.

A few months later, on 29 November 1888, the Prime Minister, Lord Salisbury, made a speech in Edinburgh. Salisbury was a Conservative, so he was Naoroji's rival. He said he doubted that a British area would ever elect "a black man". Wikipedia says the "black man" he had in mind was Naoroji.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Dinyar Patel's timeline gives the speech as 29 November 1888. Wikipedia says 30 November. Wikipedia also says that three weeks later, in another speech at Scarborough, Salisbury said the word was not necessarily meant to be rude. Manu S. Pillai writes that Salisbury later regretted it. We have not seen the speech itself.</aside>

Many people disliked the remark. Wikipedia says that the New York Times in 1888 called Salisbury's words "grotesque and foolish". The History of Parliament's article says the remark won Naoroji public sympathy. Manu Pillai says it set off a debate in the newspapers about how "black" Naoroji was.

Salisbury later invited Naoroji to become a governor of the Imperial Institute, and Naoroji accepted.

## Waiting for 1892

Naoroji had a rival inside his own party. Patel says that in January 1891 another Liberal, Frederick A. Ford, challenged him for the Finsbury candidacy. In mid-June 1892 Ford stepped aside.

A newspaper advertisement from 2 July 1892, which Patel reprints, called Naoroji "The Liberal and Radical Candidate" and ended with the words "Complete Radical Unity".

On 6 July 1892, the voting took place. Patel gives the result: Naoroji received 2,959 votes and the Conservative, Frederick Thomas Penton, received 2,956. That was a lead of three. Then Penton asked for a recount. After it, Naoroji's lead was five.

Some books say he won by three votes and some say five. Patel's timeline and Pillai both explain it. It was three at first, and five after the recount. Wikipedia gives only the final majority of five.

His opponents gave him a nickname. They called him "Dadabhai Narrow-Majority". Pillai says they also called him "Mr Nowraggie", which he says was only a little better.

## The oath

New members of the House of Commons have to take an oath. Naoroji was a Zoroastrian, not a Christian. Wikipedia says that he refused to swear on the Bible. He was allowed to take the oath on his own copy of the Khordeh Avesta, a book of Zoroastrian prayers.

<aside class="note"><b>Zoroastrian</b>: a follower of one of the world's oldest religions. Parsis in India are Zoroastrians.</aside>

## What he did in Parliament

He gave his first speech in the Commons on 9 August 1892. On 1 March 1893 he put forward a bill. It asked that the civil service exams be held in India as well as Britain. You can read how that went in "Home in 1893". He also spoke for votes for women, old-age pensions and Irish Home Rule. He argued that British rule in India was "un-British", because it did not follow the British idea of fair play.

He lost his seat in 1895. On 16 July, Patel says, he got 2,873 votes and the winner got 3,588. That same year, Patel notes, another Indian, Mancherji Bhownaggree, won a seat at Bethnal Green. He stood for the Conservatives, the party of Lord Salisbury. Bhownaggree was a Conservative, so he belonged to the party that had opposed Naoroji. Manu Pillai writes that after his defeat he published a book, *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India*. We tell about that in "The forty shillings".

## Friends in Ireland

Naoroji had friends among Irish politicians, who also wanted more self-rule for their own country. Patel's timeline says that on 25 May 1888 an Irish leader, Michael Davitt, urged another, Charles Stuart Parnell, to choose Naoroji for a seat in Sligo, in Ireland. Nothing came of it. Naoroji stood in London in the end. As an MP he spoke for Irish Home Rule, says the History of Parliament's article.

<aside class="note"><b>Home Rule</b>: the idea that Ireland should have its own parliament for its own affairs, while staying in the British Empire.</aside>

## A seat for three years

From 1892 to 1895 he sat in the Commons, as a Liberal member for Finsbury Central. He later served as president of a local football club in his area. He also backed votes for women and Indian self-rule. In his first Commons speech he argued that Indians would be British subjects or British slaves. It would depend on whether they were allowed to govern themselves.

On 27 July 1893, Patel says, he helped to found the Indian Parliamentary Committee, a group of MPs who took an interest in India.

## Why it matters

Naoroji won by three votes. He lost the next time by hundreds. A story about an election can seem small. But in 1892 many people in Britain thought that an Indian could not sit in their Parliament. The voters of Finsbury, by a very small number, said he could.

It is also a story about not giving up. He lost in 1886. He was insulted in 1888. He faced a rival in his own party. He won in 1892.
