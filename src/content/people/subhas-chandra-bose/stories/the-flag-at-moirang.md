On the afternoon of 14 April 1944, in a small town called Moirang in Manipur, a soldier raised a flag.

It was not the British flag. It was the flag of Azad Hind, "Free India". The soldier was Colonel Shaukat Malik of the Indian National Army, or INA.

It was the high point of a march that would soon go terribly wrong.

## An army far from home

The story begins in Singapore, thousands of miles away.

In July 1943, Subhas Chandra Bose arrived there, after a long journey from Germany. On 4 July, he took command of the Indian National Army.

The INA was an army of Indians in South-East Asia. Many of its soldiers had once fought for the British, and had been captured by Japan. Others were Indians who lived in Malaya and Burma, and volunteered. Some estimates say the army reached about 40,000 to 50,000 people. It even had a women's regiment, the Rani of Jhansi Regiment.

Bose was called "Netaji", the leader. His most famous slogan was: "Give me blood, and I will give you freedom!"

In October 1943, Netaji set up a government for India in exile, called Azad Hind. It declared war on Britain and the United States.

<aside class="note"><b>In exile</b>: working from outside one's own country, because it is not safe or possible to be at home.</aside>

## A difficult friend

There was one big problem. The INA could not fight alone. It got its guns, food and transport from Japan. And Japan was at war with Britain.

But others criticised him. Japan's army had done terrible things in the countries it invaded. Wikipedia notes that the INA never protested against these crimes. Some historians call the INA a force that worked with an invader. Others see it as a true freedom army that used the only help it could find.

<aside class="note"><b>Two views</b>: Netaji's alliance with Japan is still argued about today. Both sides have strong reasons, and you can read about them in our other stories about him.</aside>

## Into India

In March 1944, the Japanese army began a huge attack on British India, through the hills of Manipur. They wanted to capture the town of Imphal.

The INA marched with them. On 18 March 1944, the first INA soldiers crossed from Burma into Manipur.

A small INA unit, the Bahadur Group, was led by Shaukat Malik. Their job was to scout ahead. In early April, they reached Moirang, about 40 kilometres south of Imphal, and took it.

## The flag

Shaukat Malik set up his headquarters in the house of a local family, the Nilamani Singhs.

On 14 April 1944, at about five in the afternoon, he raised the flag. That day was also the Meitei New Year. A local man, Mairembam Koireng Singh, translated his Hindustani speech for the people who had gathered. Years later, Koireng Singh became the first elected chief minister of Manipur.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: one account says the flag had a spinning wheel, a charkha, in the middle. Another says it had a leaping tiger. Wikipedia's article on Moirang calls this the first time the flag flew on Indian soil. The same article also says Netaji had already raised it at Port Blair, in the Andaman Islands, in December 1943.</aside>

## Friends in Manipur

Many people in Manipur helped the INA. The historian Sumantra Bose, a grand-nephew of Netaji, has written about them.

Meitei villagers shared rice, vegetables and fish from Loktak Lake with the soldiers. A Muslim man who spoke Meitei, Naki Mohammad, scouted for the Bahadur Group until he was caught. A Kuki village chief, Kolbel of Saikot, welcomed Netaji himself on 2 July 1944. Even today, the villagers look after the tree under which Netaji rested.

## Imphal

The INA had several jobs in the attack. One brigade, named after Subhas himself and led by Shah Nawaz Khan, guarded one side of the Japanese advance. Another unit attacked a British airfield at Palel. The Bahadur Group tried to persuade Indian soldiers in the British army to change sides and join them. Fewer did so than the British had feared.

But the great attack on Imphal failed.

The British had more soldiers and many more aircraft. Their planes flew food and fresh troops into Imphal. The Japanese army had set out expecting to win within three weeks, and had brought too little food. They had planned to eat the cattle they drove along with them, but most of the animals died.

Then, around the middle of May, the monsoon rains came. The mountain paths turned to mud. Supplies could not get through.

Thousands of soldiers fell ill, or starved. Wikipedia says that most of the Japanese army's losses came not from bullets but from hunger, disease and exhaustion. On 3 July 1944, the Japanese commander called off the attack.

## The long walk back

The INA soldiers had to retreat too, back towards Burma, through rain and jungle. Many of the sick and wounded did not survive the journey.

Seventeen young people from Manipur went with them, including Keinya Devi and Randhoni Devi. They walked mostly at night, to hide from aircraft. It took them almost two months to reach Rangoon. There, they met Netaji. One of them, Nilamani Singh, gave him 3,000 rupees, his father's savings.

In the end, about 16,000 of the INA's soldiers were captured by the British.

When the British took Rangoon back in May 1945, the seventeen young people from Manipur were put in prison. They were finally set free in May 1946.

## What remains

At Moirang today, there is an INA memorial and a small war museum, with a statue of Netaji. The Nilamani Singh family's house still stands. Marks from machine-gun bullets can still be seen on it.

## Why it matters

The march to Imphal failed. It cost thousands of lives, many of them lost to hunger and rain rather than battle.

But for a few months, an army of Indians had entered India, calling for freedom, and people in Manipur had welcomed them. The INA's soldiers would soon be put on trial by the British, and that trial would shake the whole country.

The flag at Moirang is a reminder that the freedom struggle reached every corner of India, including its far north-east.
