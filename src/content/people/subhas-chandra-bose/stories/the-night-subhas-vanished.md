In the winter of 1940, a big old house stood on Elgin Road in Calcutta. Day and night, British police spies kept watch on it. They were watching one man.

His name was Subhas Chandra Bose.

## A prisoner in his own home

Subhas was one of the most famous leaders in India. He had twice been elected President of the Indian National Congress, in 1938 and 1939. Crowds cheered wherever he went.

But he did not agree with Mahatma Gandhi about how to win freedom. Gandhi wanted to go slowly and peacefully. Subhas wanted to push harder, and faster. In 1939, after a bitter quarrel, he resigned as Congress President. He started his own group, the Forward Bloc.

That same year, the Second World War began. Britain was fighting Germany. Subhas believed this was India's great chance. While Britain was busy, India should rise.

The British saw him as dangerous. In 1940 they put him in jail. Subhas went on a hunger strike. After seven days without food, he was so weak that they let him go home.

But home was not freedom. The police watched every door. They noted every visitor. If he tried to leave, they would know.

Subhas began to plan.

## The beard behind the curtains

First, Subhas told everyone he wanted to be alone. He stopped meeting people. He stayed in his room with the curtains closed.

Behind those curtains, something was happening. Subhas was growing a beard.

Next came clothes. He got a long brown coat, wide pyjamas and a small black cap called a fez. These were the clothes of a Pathan, a man from the north-west frontier.

He also needed a new name. He became Mohammad Ziauddin, a travelling insurance agent.

<aside class="note"><b>Pathan</b>: a person from the mountains of the north-west, in what is now Pakistan and Afghanistan.</aside>

## The car in the dark

On the night of 16 January 1941, the house was quiet. The spies outside were sleepy.

At about half past one in the morning, a black German car rolled out of the gate. It was a Wanderer sedan. At the wheel was Sisir Kumar Bose, Subhas's young nephew.

In the back sat a bearded Pathan.

There is a clever little story about that night. Sisir slammed his car door loudly. The spies heard only one door close. So they thought only one person was in the car.

The car drove through the night, out of Calcutta and into the countryside. Hours later, it reached a small railway station called Gomoh, in what is now Jharkhand.

There, "Mr Ziauddin" stepped onto the platform. Sisir acted like a nephew seeing off his uncle. Subhas boarded a train going north-west, and was gone.

## The man who could not speak

The train carried him to Peshawar, near the Afghan border. From here, he had to cross the mountains into Afghanistan.

But there was a problem. The people of the frontier spoke Pashto. Subhas did not know a word of it. One wrong sentence, and he would be caught.

A friend in Peshawar, Mian Akbar Shah, had an idea. "Pretend you cannot hear or speak," he said. "Then nobody will expect you to answer."

Akbar Shah also told him to let his beard grow long, like the tribesmen of the hills.

So Subhas became deaf and mute. He walked through the mountain passes with a guide named Bhagat Ram Talwar. Whenever someone spoke to him, he just looked blank.

<aside class="note"><b>A secret inside a secret</b>: Subhas did not know it, but his guide Bhagat Ram was secretly working for the Soviet Union. He was a spy as well as a helper.</aside>

## Across three countries

Subhas reached Kabul, the capital of Afghanistan. He hoped the Soviet Union would help him. But the Soviets were not interested. They quickly passed him on to the German ambassador.

So he turned to Germany and Italy. Both countries were at war with Britain. They gave him a new disguise. This time he was an Italian nobleman, Count Orlando Mazzotta. With an Italian passport, he travelled to Moscow.

From Moscow, he was flown to Berlin. He arrived at the beginning of April 1941.

The man who had been locked in a house in Calcutta was now in the heart of Europe.

<aside class="note"><b>Which date?</b> Most accounts say Subhas left on the night of 16–17 January 1941. Some books give a later date, 26 January. That may be the day the news of his escape came out.</aside>

## A new name

In Germany, Subhas got to work. He started a radio station called Azad Hind Radio. It broadcast messages to India, telling people to rise against British rule.

He also began to build an army. Thousands of Indian soldiers had been fighting for Britain in North Africa. Many had been captured by the Germans. Subhas asked them to join him instead. Some thousands did. They became the Free India Legion.

<aside class="note"><b>How many?</b> Books give different numbers for the Free India Legion: 3,000, 4,000 or 4,500.</aside> In early 1942, the Indians around him started calling him by a new title: Netaji.

It means "respected leader". The name stuck. Today, millions of Indians still call him Netaji.

## The car today

The black Wanderer still exists. It is kept at Netaji Bhawan, the Bose family home in Kolkata, which is now a museum. In 2016 and 2017, the car company Audi restored it to look exactly as it did in 1941.

In January 2017, the President of India, Pranab Mukherjee, unveiled the restored car.

Its number plate reads BLA 7169. Sisir Bose drove it again years later, for a film about his famous uncle. After that it was kept for people to look at.

You can go and see it. It is the car that carried a prisoner to freedom, one cold winter night.

## Why it matters

Think about what Subhas did. He was one of the most watched men in India. He did not know the language of the frontier.

Yet in about ten weeks, he crossed from Calcutta to Berlin, through mountains and three countries, without being caught.

He never saw India again. But he had not finished with it. Two years later, he would be back in Asia, at the head of an army.
