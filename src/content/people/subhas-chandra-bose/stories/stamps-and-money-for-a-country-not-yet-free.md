What makes a country a country?

It has a flag. It has a government. But it also has small, everyday things. It has its own postage stamps. It has its own money.

Netaji knew this. So, long before India was free, he set about making them.

## Stamps from Berlin

In early 1943, Netaji was in Berlin, in Germany. He was planning a government for a free India. He called it Azad Hind, which means "Free India".

He asked for postage stamps to be designed for it. They were printed at the German government printing works in Berlin, the Reichsdruckerei.

Ten different stamps were made. Look at the pictures on them.

- A farmer ploughing his field with oxen, under snowy mountains.
- A woman spinning thread at a spinning wheel, just as Gandhi taught.
- A nurse caring for a wounded man.
- A soldier with a machine gun.
- A map of India with a broken chain across it, between two daggers.

That broken chain was the whole idea in one picture. India's chains would be broken.

<aside class="note"><b>Spinning wheel</b>: Gandhi asked every Indian to spin their own thread. It became a symbol of the freedom movement.</aside>

## A million of each

These were not a few samples. About a million copies of each stamp were printed.

Three of the designs were meant for the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. During the war, Japan took over those islands. In name, it handed them to Netaji's government.

There was also a special, rarer stamp. It showed three Indian soldiers carrying a flag. One stamp collector has suggested the three men are Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru.

## Never posted

But here is the strange thing. The stamps were never used. Nobody ever stuck one on a letter and posted it.

The war turned against Germany and Japan. The government they were made for never ruled India.

Today, these unused stamps are collectors' treasures. Some of them are shown at the museum in Netaji's birthplace, Cuttack.

## A bank in Rangoon

A government needs money too. Indians living in South-East Asia were giving money to Netaji's cause. Someone had to look after it.

So on 5 April 1944, Netaji opened the Azad Hind Bank in Rangoon, in Burma. It had a capital of fifty lakh rupees.

The bank issued its own notes. They were really promises to pay, called promissory notes. And they were usually printed on one side only.

## What it tells us

Netaji was not just planning battles. He was planning a whole country, down to the stamps on its letters.

Those stamps were never posted. That money never filled Indian wallets. But they show how sure Netaji was that freedom was coming.
