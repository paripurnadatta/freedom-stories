A hundred years ago, there was one job in India that almost every family dreamed of for their sons. It was a job in the Indian Civil Service, or ICS.

ICS officers ran the country for the British. They had power, a big salary and great respect. Very few Indians ever got in.

## Trouble at college

Subhas was a brilliant student. But in 1916, his studies hit a bump.

At Presidency College in Calcutta, there was a British professor named E. F. Oaten. Students said he had insulted Indians and pushed some of them. One day, a group of students attacked him on a staircase.

A college servant said he saw Subhas among the students running away. Subhas was thrown out of the college.

<aside class="note"><b>What really happened?</b> We do not know exactly what part Subhas played. The main evidence against him was the word of one servant.</aside>

He later finished his degree at another college, Scottish Church College, in philosophy.

## Off to England

In 1919, Subhas sailed to England. The plan was for him to sit the ICS exam.

Subhas went to Cambridge. He had only about eight months to prepare.

In August 1920 he took the exam. There were only six places. Subhas came fourth. He was in!

Look at his passport from 1919. It is the passport of a young man about to win the best job in British India.

## A hard choice

But Subhas was not happy. India was changing fast. Mahatma Gandhi had started his great movement against British rule. Young Indians were giving up British jobs and schools.

Subhas asked himself: how could he serve India while working for the British?

He wrote to his elder brother, Sarat. In the letter, he said it was not possible to serve one's country "in the best and fullest manner if one is chained on to the civil service".

Chained. That was how the job felt to him.

## The letter

On 22 April 1921, Subhas sat down and wrote to Edwin Montagu, the British minister in charge of India. His letter was short. He asked for his name to be "removed from the list of probationers in the Indian Civil Service".

He was giving the job back.

<aside class="note"><b>When did he quit?</b> His letter is dated 22 April 1921. Some books say his resignation took effect later, in July 1921.</aside>

<aside class="note"><b>Probationer</b>: someone who has been chosen for a job but is still in training.</aside>

His father and others in the family were worried. But a letter came from his mother. She wrote that she supported the ideals of Mahatma Gandhi. Subhas felt she was on his side.

## Coming home

Subhas sailed back to India. He began to work with a great Bengal leader, Chittaranjan Das.

By the end of 1921, Subhas was in a British jail for the first time. He had swapped the best job in British India for a prison cell.
