Every year, the Indian National Congress held a big meeting. Leaders came from all over India. In December 1928, the meeting was held in Calcutta.

Thousands of people would come. Someone had to keep order. Someone had to guide the crowds and look after the leaders.

The job went to Subhas Chandra Bose. He was 31 years old.

## An army without weapons

Subhas did not just gather a few helpers. He built a whole corps of young volunteers. He trained them to march in step, like soldiers. They wore uniforms.

He gave himself a grand title too: General Officer Commanding, or GOC. That is a title used for real army generals.

<aside class="note"><b>Corps</b>: a group of people trained to work together, like a team in an army. It is said like "core".</aside>

Of course, a general needs a uniform. Subhas had one made by Harman's, a firm of British tailors in Calcutta. He wore it with tall boots.

Look at the photograph. It was taken on 29 December 1928. Subhas stands in his uniform. In the centre, the Congress President, Motilal Nehru, takes the salute.

## The telegram

Now here comes the funny part.

Calcutta already had a GOC. He was a real British army general. His headquarters were at Fort William, the great British fort in the city.

One day, someone sent a telegram addressed simply to "GOC". The post office did not know about Subhas's new title. So they delivered it to the British general at Fort William!

We can only imagine the British general's face when he read a telegram meant for the Congress volunteers.

<aside class="note"><b>Who told this story?</b> We know it from the writer Nirad C. Chaudhuri, who wrote about it many years later.</aside>

## Gandhi is not impressed

Not everyone enjoyed the show. Mahatma Gandhi believed in peaceful, simple protest. He did not like the marching, the clicking of boots and the saluting.

According to Chaudhuri, Gandhi later joked that the Calcutta meeting had been like a circus. He named a famous travelling circus of the time, Bertram Mills.

## Why it matters

It might seem like a small, funny story. But it tells us something big about Subhas.

Even in 1928, he was thinking like a soldier. He believed India needed discipline, order and uniforms. Gandhi believed in a very different path.

The volunteers did not disappear after the meeting. They became a group called the Bengal Volunteers. And fifteen years later, Subhas would wear a real uniform, at the head of a real army.
