By 1943, Netaji had been in Germany for almost two years. He had built a small army there, from Indian soldiers captured in the war.

But Germany was very far from India. Netaji knew where the real chance lay. It was in East Asia. Japan was fighting the British there, close to India's borders. Thousands of Indian soldiers had been captured in Malaya and Singapore.

If he could reach them, he could lead them.

In May 1942, Netaji had met Adolf Hitler, the ruler of Germany. It was their only meeting. Hitler agreed to arrange a submarine to carry Netaji east.

<aside class="note"><b>A difficult choice</b>: Hitler was a cruel dictator who did terrible things. Netaji asked Germany and Japan for help because they were fighting Britain. People in India still argue about whether he was right to do so.</aside>

There was one problem. The whole world was at war. Ships were sunk. Planes were shot down. How could anyone travel from Germany to Asia?

The answer was: under the sea.

## A goodbye

Netaji was not alone in Europe. He had a wife, Emilie, and a baby daughter, Anita. She was born in November 1942.

In February 1943, Netaji said goodbye to them. He would never see them again.

## Into the U-boat

On 8 February 1943, a German submarine slipped out of the port of Kiel. Its name was U-180. Hidden inside were two Indian passengers. One was Netaji. The other was his young secretary, Abid Hasan.

Abid Hasan had been training at a camp for Indian soldiers near Berlin. One day he was called to Netaji's office. He was told to hand over his weapons. He did not know why. Then he learned he was going on a secret journey.

<aside class="note"><b>U-boat</b>: the name for a German submarine. "U" comes from the German word for "undersea".</aside>

Life inside was hard. A submarine is like a long metal tube, packed with machines. There was room for only one bed for the two of them. Everyone ate and sat together in a tiny space. The smell of diesel oil got into everything, even the blankets and the food.

Abid Hasan later said it felt like being in solitary confinement. There were no windows. And the trip just to the coast near Madagascar took about two months.

## Khichdi at the bottom of the sea

The German food did not suit them. So Abid Hasan searched the pantry. He found some rice and lentils. He cooked khichdi for Netaji.

Soon the German officers were asking for it too.

Netaji was losing weight. The doctor on board made him do exercises. But mostly, he worked. He dictated letters and plans. He practised what he would say when he met the leaders of Japan. Abid Hasan typed for three or four hours every day.

Netaji was very fussy about punctuation. Every comma had to be right, even at the bottom of the ocean.

He also worked on his book, The Indian Struggle. He went through it again, page by page, as the submarine slid through the dark water.

## "Dive! Dive!"

The journey was dangerous. Near the north of Scotland, they had a close call with the enemy. British ships dropped depth charges into the water, to blow up submarines.

U-180 was a warship, not a passenger boat. On the way it attacked enemy ships. On 18 April 1943, near South Africa, it sank a British tanker called Corbis.

Once, something went wrong with the steering. The submarine rose to the surface, where enemies could see it. The captain shouted, "Dive! Dive!" The boat plunged down so steeply that it scraped the bottom.

Through it all, Netaji kept dictating calmly. The German captain later praised him and Abid Hasan for staying so cool.

<aside class="note"><b>Depth charge</b>: a bomb dropped into the sea from a ship. It explodes deep underwater to damage submarines.</aside>

## The meeting in the ocean

After more than two months, U-180 reached the Indian Ocean, south-east of Africa.

There, on 26 April 1943, it met another submarine. This one was Japanese. Its name was I-29.

Now Netaji had to cross from one submarine to the other, in the middle of the ocean. The sea was rough. The meeting went on for more than twelve hours because of the bad weather.

Finally, Netaji and Abid Hasan crossed over. Two Japanese naval officers went the other way, to the German submarine. They were going to Germany to learn how U-boats were built. The Japanese also handed over two tonnes of gold to the Germans.

Historians say this is the only known time in the Second World War that a passenger was moved between submarines of two different navies.

<aside class="note"><b>Where and when exactly?</b> Sources differ a little. Some say the swap happened on 26 April, some on 27 April. Some say it was off Madagascar, others off Mozambique. Both are near the east coast of Africa.</aside>

## Land at last

The Japanese captain did not go to the big port of Penang. He was afraid British spies there might spot his famous passenger. Instead, on 6 May 1943, I-29 reached the small island of Sabang, at the tip of Sumatra. Netaji stepped onto dry land. He had spent about three months under the sea.

Before they landed, the crew posed for a photograph on the deck. Look at the front row. On the left sits a man in glasses. That is Netaji.

## What came next

From Sumatra, Netaji flew to Tokyo to meet Japan's leaders. Then he went to Singapore. He arrived there on 2 July 1943.

Two days later, on 4 July 1943, at the Cathay Building in Singapore, he took command of the Indian National Army.

The long underwater journey had brought him to the army he had dreamed of.

Think about it. Three months in a metal tube, under the sea, in the middle of a war. Danger above and below. Yet Netaji spent the time working, as if he were sitting at his desk in Calcutta.

## Abid Hasan

Netaji's companion on the voyage became famous too. Abid Hasan is often credited with coining a greeting the Indian National Army used: "Jai Hind". It means "Victory to India".

After independence, Jawaharlal Nehru declared "Jai Hind" a national greeting. Indians still say it today.
