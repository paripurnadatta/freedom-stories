How would you start a newspaper that people could not stop reading? Azad had thought hard about this question. He came home from his travels abroad in 1908. Then he made a plan.

He decided that India needed a good journal to build public opinion. He looked at the Urdu papers of the time. They came mostly from Punjab and the United Provinces. Azad was not impressed. Their printing was poor, he says, and so was their writing. They were made by a process called lithography. That meant the pages were copied from handwriting. They could not carry fine pictures.

<aside class="note"><b>Lithography</b>: a way of printing in which a handwritten page is copied onto a stone or plate. It is cheap, but the pages look rough.</aside>

His paper, he decided, would be set in type. It would look good and speak with force.

## Al-Hilal begins

Azad started the Al-Hilal Press. The first issue of *Al-Hilal* came out in June 1912. The name means "the crescent moon".

It sold fast. Azad says the demand was so great that within the first three months, all the old issues had to be printed again. New readers wanted the whole set from the start.

He says the paper did two things that were new. It was better printed than any Urdu paper before it. And it spoke in a strong voice for the freedom of India. In two years, he writes, the weekly sold 26,000 copies. He says that was a figure nobody in Urdu journalism had seen.

## Not everyone liked it

Some leaders of Muslim politics at that time were close to the Aligarh party. Azad says they believed that Muslims should stay loyal to the British and keep away from the freedom movement. When Al-Hilal said something different, they felt their place was in danger. He writes that they began to oppose the paper. Some even threatened the editor's life. The harder they pushed, he says, the more the community took to the paper.

## The government steps in

The government also took notice. Under the Press Act, it asked the paper for a security deposit of Rs 2,000.

<aside class="note"><b>Security deposit</b>: money that a paper had to give the government. If the government decided the paper had broken the rules, it could keep the money.</aside>

Azad says the government soon took the money. It asked for a new deposit, of Rs 10,000. That was lost too. Then war broke out in 1914. In 1915, he says, the Al-Hilal Press was seized.

Five months later, he started another press and another paper. It was called *Al-Balagh*.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia says Al-Hilal was banned in 1914 under the Press Act. Azad writes that the deposits were lost in turn, and that the press itself was taken in 1915. We do not know the exact month when the weekly stopped. We could not check the government's own records.</aside>

## Sent out of the city

The government now felt, says Azad, that the Press Act alone would not stop him. So in April 1916 it used another law, the Defence of India Regulations. He was told to leave Calcutta.

Other governments did the same. The governments of Punjab, Delhi, the United Provinces and Bombay had already banned him from entering their areas. Only Bihar was left. He went to Ranchi.

About six months later he was no longer free to move. He was interned there, which means he was held in one place under guard. He stayed so until 31 December 1919. Azad says that on 1 January 1920 he and other internees were released under the King's declaration.

## Why it matters

Azad was in his twenties when he started Al-Hilal. In a few years, an editor with a printing press and a new idea had become a danger in the eyes of the government. The press was taken. The money was taken. At last he himself was sent away.

But he was only held, not stopped. In January 1920, soon after he came out of Ranchi, he met Gandhi for the first time. That meeting is another story.
