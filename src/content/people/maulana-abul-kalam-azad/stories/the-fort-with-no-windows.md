On the night of 8 August 1942, Maulana Azad came back to the house of his friend Bhulabhai Desai in Bombay. He was tired. A big meeting had just ended.

His friend was still awake. He had news. He had heard that all the Congress leaders would be arrested the next morning.

Azad was not in the mood to listen to rumours. He says so in his book. If it was true, he told his friend, then he had only a few hours of freedom left. It would be better to have dinner and sleep.

## The meeting before the arrests

That meeting was the session of the All India Congress Committee. It passed the "Quit India" resolution. Azad was the Congress President then. Wikipedia says he opened the struggle with a speech at Gowalia Tank in Bombay on 7 August. His own book says the resolution was passed by a large majority late on 8 August.

<aside class="note"><b>Quit India</b>: a movement started in August 1942. The Congress asked the British to leave India. Gandhi asked Indians to act without violence.</aside>

Azad says there had been another rumour. Someone had heard that the leaders might be sent out of India, perhaps to South Africa. Later, he writes, he learnt there was some truth in it. The British had approached the Government of South Africa. But the plan was dropped at the last moment, and he says he does not know why.

He was arrested in the morning of 9 August 1942.

## Ten men in a fort

Ten members of the Working Committee were taken to Ahmednagar Fort. Azad names them in his book. They were Azad himself, Jawaharlal Nehru, Sardar Patel, Asaf Ali, Shankar Rao Deo, Govind Ballabh Pant, Dr Pattabhi Sitaramayya, Dr Syed Mahmud, Acharya Kripalani and Dr Profulla Ghosh. Gandhi was held separately, in Poona.

<aside class="note"><b>Working Committee</b>: the small group that ran the Congress and made its big decisions.</aside>

Azad says they were brought to a building that looked like a military barracks. There was an open courtyard about 200 feet long, with rooms around it. He heard later that foreign prisoners had been kept there during the First World War.

The jailer checked their luggage. Azad had a small portable radio that he always carried. The jailer kept it. Azad says he did not see it again until he left.

Dinner came on iron plates. Azad told the jailer they were used to eating from china plates. The jailer was polite and promised to get some by the next day. The first cook was a convict brought from Poona. He could not cook the food they liked. A second cook was no better.

## A secret place

The government kept the fort's name a secret. Azad thought this foolish. "It was obvious that the facts could not be concealed for long," he writes. He was right. The public, he says, knew within a week.

After two or three days the Inspector-General of Prisons for Bombay came. He said the orders were strict. The prisoners could not write to anyone, even their families. They could not get letters. And there would be no newspapers.

Azad says the officer was very apologetic. But the orders stood.

Azad also describes what the British did to the building. The barracks had skylights through which you could see the fort's compound. Before the prisoners arrived, these were blocked up. The plaster was so new that it was still damp. And in all the time they stayed, he says, they hardly ever saw an Indian from outside. Even small repairs were not done by Indian workers.

## A wife who was ill

Azad's wife had been unwell for several years. When he was arrested, her health was already a worry. News of his arrest, he writes, must have been a great shock to her.

Early in 1944 he heard she was seriously ill. Her doctors wrote to the government. They asked that he be allowed to see her once, because there was little hope. The government ignored this letter. Azad wrote to the Viceroy too, but the correspondence led nowhere.

Then, one April afternoon, an officer named Cheeta Khan came and handed him a telegram. It said his wife was dead. Azad wrote again to the Viceroy. He said the government could easily have moved him to Calcutta for a short time so that he could see her before she died. He received no reply.

About three months after that, his sister Abru Begum died in Bhopal.

<aside class="note"><b>A word of care</b>: We have only Azad's own book for these months. It does not give a year in the passage about his wife's death. We did not find a second source that dates it.</aside>

Azad also says the long time in prison hurt his health. When he was arrested, he writes, he weighed 170 pounds. When he left Ahmednagar, he weighed 130.

## The letters

In those years Azad wrote a book. It is called *Ghubar-e-Khatir*. The name means "dust of the mind" or "dust of memories."

It is a set of 24 letters. They were written to his close friend, Maulana Habibur Rahman Khan Sherwani. They were never sent, because the prison rules did not allow it. Wikipedia says that after Azad was released, he gave the letters to his secretary, who published them in 1946.

The letters are not about politics. Wikipedia says they cover big questions: whether God exists, how religions began, and the place of music. There are more than five hundred verses in them, mostly in Persian and Arabic. Wikipedia also says Azad wrote a "hypnotic" description of the morning habit of making and drinking tea. We could not read the book itself, so we cannot show you those lines.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia says Azad wrote the letters in Ahmednagar Fort from 1942 to 1946. A journal article says they were written in 1942 to 1945, in Ahmednagar Fort and Bankura Jail.  We do not know the exact dates of each letter.</aside>

## Leaving the fort

By the middle of the war, Azad says, the secrecy no longer served any purpose. British victory looked close. The government moved the Working Committee members to jails in their own provinces. Sardar Patel went to Poona. Asaf Ali went to Batala. Nehru went to Naini and then to Almora.

Azad's own move was done quietly. Cheeta Khan hinted that damp Calcutta would not suit his health. One afternoon he was driven not to Ahmednagar station but to a small village station, several miles away. This was so that nobody would see him leave. A police officer from Bengal and four constables met him there. They travelled to Asansol. There, Azad says, reporters from Calcutta and some friends from Allahabad were waiting, in spite of all the secrecy.

Before Nehru left, he told Azad that their release might be near. He said he wanted a little rest, and time to finish a book. Azad felt the same. He writes that neither of them knew what was coming. They would soon face "immediate and hectic political action" and no rest at all.

## Why it matters

The fort had no windows to the outside, and the government wanted to keep it that way. But Azad's account of those years shows us something else. A man was kept away from the world, from his dying wife, from his friends. He answered by writing letters he could not post.

Many other people in India were also in jail in those years. This is only one man's story, from his own book. But it helps us picture what "going to jail for freedom" meant. It was not only a cell and a gate. It was years without news, and without the people you love.
