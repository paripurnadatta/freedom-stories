Imagine a boy in Calcutta with a thick English dictionary. He cannot speak English. He has never been to a modern school. His father does not trust modern schooling at all.

But the boy has made up his mind. He takes an English reader, and then a Bible in three languages. He reads one line at a time, and looks up every hard word.

This boy later became one of the most important leaders of India's freedom struggle. We know him as Maulana Abul Kalam Azad. Much of this story comes from his own book, *India Wins Freedom*. He wrote the opening chapter about himself. So these are his own words, remembered when he was an older man.

<aside class="note"><b>Maulana</b>: a title of respect for a Muslim scholar. It means something like "our master" or "learned teacher".</aside>

## Born in Mecca

Azad writes: "I was born in Mecca in 1888." Wikipedia gives the full date, 11 November 1888. This is the day India now keeps as National Education Day.

His father was Maulana Khairuddin, a scholar. His mother was the daughter of a scholar of Medina. Azad says his father's family had lived in India for generations, as scholars and officers. Two years before the Mutiny of 1857, his father's grandfather decided to leave for Mecca. The Mutiny broke out while he was still on the way, and he died in Bombay. His father, then about twenty-five, went on to Mecca and settled there.

Azad tells one story about his father that he is clearly proud of. In Mecca, the old canal called Nahr Zubeida was the main water supply. It had fallen into bad shape. Water was short, and the pilgrims suffered most. Azad writes that his father raised a fund of twenty lakh rupees in India, Egypt, Syria and Turkey, and had the canal repaired. The Sultan of Turkey, he says, gave his father a medal for it.

<aside class="note"><b>A word of care</b>: This story about the canal comes from Azad's own book. We did not find a second source for the amount of money, so we give it as he tells it.</aside>

<aside class="note"><b>Nahr Zubeida</b>: an old water channel built to carry water to Mecca. It was named after Zubeida, the wife of the Caliph Harun al-Rashid.</aside>

## A new home in Calcutta

In 1890, Azad says, his father brought the whole family to Calcutta. Wikipedia gives the same year. The father had broken his shin bone in Jedda. It had not healed well, and he was told the doctors of Calcutta could set it right.

He meant to stay only a short time. But his followers in the city would not let him go. A year after they arrived, Azad's mother died. She was buried in Calcutta.

## School at home

Azad's father believed in the old ways. He did not trust modern schools. He thought they would weaken a child's faith. So he did not send his son even to a madrasa.

<aside class="note"><b>Madrasa</b>: a school where Muslim children study religion and old subjects such as Arabic and philosophy.</aside>

The boy was taught at home. At first his father taught him himself. Later, Azad says, he found a different teacher for each subject. He wanted the best scholar in every field.

The old course had a set order. First came Persian. Then Arabic. Then, in Arabic, came philosophy, geometry, mathematics and algebra, along with Islamic theology. Most students took until they were twenty or twenty-five to finish.

Azad says he finished at sixteen. Then his father gathered about fifteen students for him to teach. He taught them higher philosophy, mathematics and logic. In the old system, a young scholar had to teach before he was truly counted as finished.

Some more details about his early years are told. He started a poetry magazine called *Nairang-e-Alam* in 1899, when he was about eleven. He also ran a small library and a debating society.

<aside class="note"><b>One source only</b>: these details were found only on Wikipedia.</aside>

## "I must learn English"

Then something happened that his father had not planned. Azad came across the writings of Sir Syed Ahmed Khan. Sir Syed was a Muslim reformer who wanted Indians to study modern science.

<aside class="note"><b>Sir Syed Ahmed Khan</b>: a nineteenth-century Muslim thinker who started a modern college at Aligarh. He urged Muslims to learn English and modern science.</aside>

Azad says he was greatly impressed. He realised that a person could not be truly educated today without science, philosophy and modern literature. And to read those, he needed English.

He went to a teacher, Maulvi Mohammed Yusuf Jafri. Jafri was the chief examiner of the Oriental course. He taught Azad the English alphabet. Then he gave him a small book, Peary Churan Sarkar's *First Book*.

As soon as Azad knew a little English, he turned to the Bible. He found it in three languages, English, Persian and Urdu. He laid the three side by side. Then he read them together. This, he says, helped him a great deal in understanding the English text.

He also began to read English newspapers, with a dictionary. Soon he could read English books. He spent most of his time on history and philosophy.

## A mind in trouble

Learning new ideas did not make him calm. Azad calls this "a period of great mental crisis."

He had grown up in a family where religious customs were never questioned. Now he found he could not accept everything he had been taught. The thing that troubled him first was the quarrels among Muslim groups. Each one called the others wrong. He could not see why, when they all drew their faith from the same source.

From that, his doubts grew wider. If religion tells one truth, he asked, why do religions fight? Why does each one say it alone is right?

For two or three years, he says, this unrest went on. Then he felt free from all the old ties. He decided to choose his own path.

## A new name

It was about this time that he chose a pen name. He chose "Azad", which means "free". He says it showed he was "no longer tied to my inherited beliefs."

That is the name India knows him by.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Some books say Azad studied at al-Azhar University in Cairo. He says this is wrong. "I was not a student there for a single day," he writes. He says Mahadev Desai, who wrote an early life of him, made the mistake. Azad did visit Cairo in 1908, but only as a traveller. We follow Azad's own account.</aside>

## What he did with his years of study

Before he was twenty, Azad began to publish journals in Calcutta. Among them was a weekly called *Al-Misbah* and another called *Lisan-us-Sidq*, which closed for lack of money in 1905. In his own book, Azad says that his next step was a journey to Iraq, Egypt, Syria and Turkey in 1908.

Those journals and journeys are another story. Here we only note where it began: a home classroom, a dictionary, and a boy who wanted to know more than he was allowed.

## Why it matters

Azad's father wanted a son who knew the old books well. He got that. But Azad also taught himself English at about sixteen and began to read widely, without being asked to.

His story shows two things. One is that learning can come from your own curiosity. The other is that asking hard questions is part of growing up. Azad asked them. In time, he became a man who wrote, argued and led. And he later became the first Education Minister of free India.
