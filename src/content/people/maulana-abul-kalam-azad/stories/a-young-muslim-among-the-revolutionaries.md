Imagine meeting a group of young people who say they are working in secret. They do not know you. They are a little afraid of you. And you want to join them.

That is what Azad says happened to him in Calcutta, about 1905 or 1906. This story comes from his own book. He wrote it many years later, so it is his memory. We have not found a second source for how deep his link with these groups went.

## The year Bengal was divided

In 1905 the Viceroy, Lord Curzon, divided Bengal into two provinces. Azad says Curzon believed this would weaken the Hindus of Bengal and make a lasting split between Hindus and Muslims. Bengal did not accept it quietly. Azad writes of "an unprecedented outburst of political and revolutionary enthusiasm."

<aside class="note"><b>Revolutionary</b>: here, someone who believed that British rule should be ended by secret, forceful action. The groups in Bengal at that time used this word for themselves.</aside>

Azad says that Sri Aurobindo Ghose left Baroda and came to Calcutta. His paper, *Karmayogin*, became a symbol of the awakening.

## A friend named Shyam Sunder

Azad was drawn in through one man. This was Shyam Sunder Chakravarty, whom Azad calls an important revolutionary worker of the day. Through him, Azad met others. He says he met Aurobindo Ghose two or three times.

Soon Azad joined one of the groups. But his new friends were surprised that he wanted to join. At first they did not trust him. They kept him away from their inner talks.

## Why they were afraid

Azad explains why. In those days, he says, the revolutionary groups drew their members only from the Hindu middle class. Many of them thought that Muslims were against the freedom movement.

He gives two reasons. The first was that the British were using Muslims against the Indian cause. East Bengal had become a separate province, and its Lieutenant-Governor had said the government treated Muslims as its "favorite wife." The second was that the government had brought Muslim officers from the United Provinces into the Bengal police's Intelligence Branch, because it did not trust Hindu officers. So the people of Bengal began to feel that Muslims as a whole stood against them.

## What Azad told them

Azad argued with his friends. He told them that they should not judge all Muslims by a few officers. In Egypt, Iran and Turkey, he said, Muslims were working for democracy and freedom. Indian Muslims would join too, if somebody went among them and made friends. And if Muslims stayed against the struggle, or just stayed away, the struggle would be harder.

He says he did not convince them all at first. But in time some of them came round. He also began to work among Muslims, and he found a group of young men ready for new tasks.

He says he also urged the groups to spread beyond Bengal and Bihar. At first they worried that it would be hard to stay secret. Within two years, Azad writes, secret societies had been set up in several towns of North India and in Bombay.

## A journey abroad

In 1908 Azad left India for a tour of Iraq, Egypt, Syria and Turkey, and then France. In Iraq he met Iranian revolutionaries. In Egypt he met followers of Mustafa Kamal Pasha, and a group of Young Turks who published a weekly there. He became friends with Young Turk leaders in Turkey and wrote to them for many years.

They were surprised, he says, that Indian Muslims seemed to be either uninterested in freedom or against it. They believed that Indian Muslims should have been at the front of the national struggle.

Azad came home more sure than ever. Indian Muslims had to join the work of freeing the country. He decided to take up political work with more earnestness.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Some books say Azad went to Egypt to study at al-Azhar University. Azad says this is wrong. He calls al-Azhar's teaching of that time poor and says he was "not a student there for a single day." He was recalled by news that his father was ill, and he came back from Paris without seeing London.</aside>

## Why it matters

This is Azad's own account, told fifty years later. Other historians may weigh it differently. What we can say is clear enough. Azad believed Muslims and Hindus could work together. He tried to say so even in a place where nobody wanted to hear it. That belief stayed with him for the rest of his life.
