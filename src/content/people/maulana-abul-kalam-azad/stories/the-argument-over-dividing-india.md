Some arguments are about small things. This one was about whether a country would be cut in two.

In the spring of 1947, the Congress leaders had to decide. The Muslim League wanted a separate country for Muslims. The British were getting ready to leave. And Maulana Azad, one of the senior leaders of the Congress, believed that dividing India would be a mistake.

Almost everything in this story comes from Azad's own book, *India Wins Freedom*. It was published after his death. It tells the argument from his side. Patel and Nehru did not write their versions of these same talks in that book. So please read it as one person's account, and not as the last word.

<aside class="note"><b>Partition</b>: the division of British India in August 1947 into two countries, India and Pakistan.</aside>

## What Azad believed

Azad had said no to Partition for a long time. He says Jinnah had raised the slogan of "two nations". If the Congress agreed to divide the country, he argued, it would be accepting that slogan. How could the Congress agree to split the country along Hindu and Muslim lines?

He also thought Partition would not heal the quarrel. It would make it permanent. "Instead of removing communal fears," he says he told them, "partition would perpetuate them by creating two States based on communal hatred."

He thought the real problems of the country were poverty and class, not religion. Once India was free, he said, people would see this.

## Patel: "two brothers"

Azad says he talked to Sardar Patel for over two hours. He was surprised and pained by Patel's answer. Patel said that whether they liked it or not, there were two nations in India. Hindus and Muslims, he was now convinced, could not be united into one nation.

Patel used a picture that Azad remembers. If two brothers cannot live together, they divide. After separating with their shares, they become friends. If they are forced to stay together, they fight every day. It would be better, Patel said, to have one clean fight and then separate.

## Nehru: "what other way?"

Nehru, says Azad, did not speak for Partition the way Patel did. Nehru admitted that Partition was wrong by its nature. But he had lost hope. In the Government, the members of the Muslim League could not agree with the Congress members on anything. They quarrelled every day. Azad says Nehru asked him in despair what other way there was.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia tells us that Azad's book says Patel became more in favour of Partition than even the Muslim League. That is Azad's view, not a proven fact. We did not find Patel's own account of those talks.</aside>

## Turning to Gandhi

Azad turned to Gandhi. Gandhi had been staying at Patna, after some months in Noakhali. He came to Delhi, arriving on 31 March. Azad went to see him at once.

Gandhi's first words, Azad says, were: "Partition has now become a threat." Gandhi added that Patel, and even Nehru, seemed to have given up. Then he asked Azad whether Azad would stand by him.

Azad replied that his opposition had never been stronger. "My only hope now is in you," he says he told Gandhi. "If you stand against partition, we may yet save the situation."

Gandhi answered strongly. If the Congress wanted to accept Partition, he said, it would be "over my dead body."

But Azad says that when they spoke again, Gandhi too had changed. He calls it "the greatest shock of my life." Gandhi was still not openly for Partition, but he no longer spoke so strongly against it. Later, at the meeting of the All India Congress Committee, Gandhi told the members that he had always opposed Partition. Yet a situation had come where there was no other choice. He asked them to support the Working Committee.

## The vote

The Working Committee's resolution accepted the plan of Lord Mountbatten, the last Viceroy. Azad says that on the first day there was strong feeling against it. People could see it as a denial of what the Congress had said from the beginning.

Then it was put to the vote. According to Azad, 29 members voted for it and 15 against. Azad says even Gandhi's appeal could not bring more votes.

Wikipedia says that Azad did not vote for or against. He abstained. It says the AICC approved the plan.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia says the British announced the plan on 3 June 1947. The passage of Azad's book we read does not give a date for the AICC vote. We use Wikipedia for the abstention, because Azad does not mention it in the lines we read.</aside>

Azad adds a sad line about how people felt afterwards. All hearts were heavy at the idea of Partition, he says. Hardly anyone accepted it without doubts.

## After the vote

India and Pakistan became separate countries in August 1947. Azad wrote that "rivers of blood flowed in large parts of the country." Many families lost their homes and many people were killed on both sides. We do not tell those stories here.

Azad stayed in India. He became the Education Minister. In the weeks after Partition, Wikipedia says, he worked on refugee relief and the safety of Muslims. It adds that he clashed with Patel over policing and housing in Delhi.

## A speech at the great mosque

After Partition, Azad spoke to a crowd at the Jama Masjid in Delhi. Many Muslims were afraid. Some were leaving for Pakistan.

A website called Janata Weekly prints what it says is the speech, with the date 23 October 1947. It credits The Citizen. The text says that Partition was "a fundamental mistake." It says that "this country is ours, we belong to it." It also says that "a true Muslim can be swayed neither by avarice nor apprehension." And it says: "Brothers, I do not have a new prescription for you."

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The Scroll website writes about Azad's "last speech at Delhi's Jama Masjid in 1948". Janata Weekly gives 23 October 1947. They may be about different speeches, or one of them has the date wrong. The Janata text also has spelling mistakes, and it does not say who translated it. So we cannot be sure these are his exact words. We use only short lines and we label them.</aside>

Scroll quotes some other lines from the same mosque. It reports Azad asking Muslims who were leaving, "Where are you going and why? Raise your eyes."

## Why it matters

People still disagree about Partition. Some think it could not be avoided. Others think a way was missed. Azad's own book says he never changed his mind. Patel and Nehru, as Azad reports them, felt no workable path was left.

What we can say is that Azad made his case to his friends for hours. When he lost, he did not leave the country or the struggle. He stayed in India and went on to do a job that needed doing. In a time when many people were afraid, he told them to look up and stay.
