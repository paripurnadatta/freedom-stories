Bhagat Singh was a college student in Lahore, about sixteen years old. One day his grandmother brought up a big subject. It was time, she said, for him to get married.

In those days, many boys in Punjab were married young. Families chose the bride, and the boy was expected to agree.

Bhagat Singh argued with her. He tried again and again to explain. But his words did not change her mind.

## A letter on the table

So he made a plan. He sat down and wrote a letter to his father. Then he left home.

The letter still exists. In it, he wrote that his life was already given to a higher purpose: the freedom of India. Worldly comforts, he said, no longer pulled at him.

<aside class="note"><b>Worldly comforts</b>: an easy life at home, with money, family and a settled job.</aside>

He reminded his father of something from his childhood. At a family ceremony when he was small, an elder had said this boy would serve his country. Now, he wrote, he was keeping that promise.

He ended with one gentle line: "I hope you will forgive me."

## Why not marry?

Bhagat Singh had seen what the struggle did to families. His uncle Ajit Singh had been sent far away by the British. Another uncle had died young. Their wives had been left alone at home.

He told a classmate he did not want a young bride waiting for him in the same way. He already knew his life would be dangerous.

## The newspaper in Kanpur

He took a train to Kanpur, a city far to the east in what is now Uttar Pradesh. He carried a letter of introduction to a famous editor called Ganesh Shankar Vidyarthi.

<aside class="note"><b>Letter of introduction</b>: a note from someone you both know, saying "please help this person".</aside>

Vidyarthi ran a fiery Hindi newspaper called Pratap. He gave the runaway boy work there.

In Kanpur, Bhagat Singh met young people who wanted to free India by any means. One of them was Batukeshwar Dutt. Six years later, the two of them would stand together in the Assembly in Delhi.

## Going home

After some time, his family sent word. If he came home, nobody would force him to marry. So he went back to Lahore.

He never did marry. He had chosen his path at sixteen, and he did not leave it.
