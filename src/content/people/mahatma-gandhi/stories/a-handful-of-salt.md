What is the cheapest thing in your kitchen?

It is probably salt. Every family needs it. Rich or poor, Hindu or Muslim, everyone puts salt in their food. In a hot country like India, people who work in the sun need it even more.

In 1930, salt became the centre of the biggest protest India had ever seen.

## A tax on salt

Under British rule, Indians were not allowed to make their own salt. Not even from the sea, which was right there on India's long coast.

Salt had to be bought. And the British government put a tax on it. That tax brought the government a lot of money. Out of every hundred rupees the British collected in taxes in India, about eight came from salt.

The poorest people suffered most. A rich man barely noticed the salt tax. A poor farmer felt it every single day.

## Gandhi's strange idea

On 26 January 1930, the Congress declared that India must have complete freedom, Purna Swaraj. Gandhi was asked to start a new campaign against British rule.

Everyone waited to see what he would do. Would he call a strike? Would he lead a march on Delhi?

He chose salt.

Some leaders were puzzled. Salt? It seemed so small. But Gandhi saw something they did not. Salt was the one thing that touched every Indian. Gandhi called it the greatest necessity of life, after air and water. A protest about salt could bring together Hindus and Muslims, rich and poor, people in cities and people in villages.

<aside class="note"><b>Satyagraha</b>: Gandhi's word for peaceful protest. It means "holding on to truth". A satyagrahi breaks an unjust law openly, never uses violence, and accepts the punishment.</aside>

## Seventy-eight companions

Gandhi's plan was simple. He would walk from his ashram at Sabarmati, near Ahmedabad, to the sea at a village called Dandi. There, he would make salt. That would break the law.

He chose seventy-eight people from his ashram to walk with him. Most were young, between twenty and thirty. The youngest, Vitthal Liladhar Thakkar, was just sixteen. Most came from Gujarat, but others came from as far away as Kerala, Punjab and Bengal.

Gandhi was 61 years old. He was thin and walked with a stick. The journey was about 240 miles, or nearly 390 kilometres.

The evening before they left, a huge crowd gathered at the ashram. Gandhi spoke to them. He told them that no one must turn violent, whatever happened.

## Why everyone could join

The clever thing about the plan was that anyone could take part. You did not need to be rich or educated. You only needed to make a little salt.

Old women in villages could do it. Fishermen on the coast could do it. And every person who did it was breaking a British law, openly, without hurting anyone.

## The walk

On the morning of 12 March 1930, the marchers set out.

They walked through the villages of Gujarat. Aslali. Nadiad. Anand. Borsad. Surat. Navsari. Each day they covered around ten miles. In village after village, Gandhi stopped and spoke to the crowds.

The crowds grew. People lined the roads. Reporters came from India and abroad to follow the march.

The British Viceroy, Lord Irwin, was not worried at first. He wrote that the salt campaign did not keep him awake at night.

But the whole world was watching an old man walk to the sea.

## The beach at Dandi

On 5 April, the marchers reached Dandi.

Early the next morning, 6 April 1930, Gandhi prayed. Then he walked down to the shore. He bent down and picked up a lump of salty mud from the beach. Then it was boiled in sea water, to make salt.

It was a tiny thing. But by doing it, he had broken the salt law.

Look at the photograph of that morning. Gandhi is bending over the sand. Behind him stand his son Manilal and a young woman named Mithuben Petit.

He is reported to have said: "With this, I am shaking the foundations of the British Empire."

<aside class="note"><b>5 or 6 April?</b> The marchers arrived at Dandi on 5 April. Most books say Gandhi broke the salt law on the morning of 6 April. The famous photograph of him on the beach is often labelled 5 April.</aside>

## India joins in

That was the signal. All along India's coast, people began to make salt. They boiled sea water in pots. They sold salt openly in the streets. Others refused to pay taxes or boycotted British cloth.

The British struck back. They arrested people in huge numbers. By the end of the campaign, more than sixty thousand people had been put in jail.

Around midnight on 4–5 May, the police came for Gandhi too. He was arrested in his camp.

## Dharasana

But the campaign did not stop. Next, the satyagrahis planned to march on the salt works at Dharasana.

The plan was led first by an old freedom fighter, Abbas Tyabji. He was arrested. Kasturba Gandhi was arrested too, before she could get there. Then the poet Sarojini Naidu took over the lead.

At Dharasana, row after row of marchers walked up to the gates. Police beat them with heavy sticks. The marchers did not raise their hands to hit back. An American reporter, Webb Miller, saw it all. His reports went around the world. He wrote that not one of the marchers even raised an arm to protect himself from the blows. People everywhere were shocked by what they read.

## What the salt did

Did the Salt March free India? Not straight away. India would not be free for another seventeen years.

But something had changed. The world had seen ordinary Indians stand up peacefully against a great empire. At the end of 1930, the American magazine Time named Gandhi its "Man of the Year".

And millions of Indians had learned something too. Even the smallest act, like picking up a handful of salt, could become a weapon of freedom.
