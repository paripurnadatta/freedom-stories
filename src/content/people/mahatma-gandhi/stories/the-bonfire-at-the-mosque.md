Imagine if you had to carry a special card everywhere you went. It had your fingerprints on it. Any policeman could stop you and ask to see it. If you did not have it, you could be thrown out of the country.

Now imagine that only people of your skin colour had to carry it.

That is what happened to Indians in the Transvaal, in South Africa, in the early 1900s.

## The Black Act

In 1906, the government of the Transvaal brought in a new law. Every Indian older than eight had to register, give his fingerprints, and carry a certificate.

Indians were furious. They called it the Black Act.

On 11 September 1906, thousands of Indians gathered at a big meeting in Johannesburg. They made a solemn promise. They would not obey this law, whatever the punishment.

It was the beginning of what Gandhi later called satyagraha: peaceful resistance to an unjust law.

<aside class="note"><b>Transvaal</b>: one of the colonies that later joined together to become South Africa. Its biggest city was Johannesburg.</aside>

## A broken promise

The struggle went on for two years. Gandhi and many others went to jail.

Then, in 1908, the Transvaal leader Jan Smuts made a deal with Gandhi. The Black Act would be removed.

But Smuts did not keep his side of the deal. The Black Act stayed. Gandhi gave him until 16 August 1908 to change his mind.

The Indians felt cheated. So they decided to send back the certificates in the most public way they could.

## The cauldron

On Sunday, 16 August 1908, at four in the afternoon, a huge crowd gathered in the grounds of the Hamidia Mosque in Johannesburg.

On a platform stood an enormous iron cauldron. It was borrowed from an Indian trader's shop. It was the kind of pot normally used for cooking big meals.

The committee had already collected more than two thousand certificates. People brought more during the meeting.

A telegram from the government was read out. It refused the Indians' demands. The crowd cheered. They were ready.

## Into the flames

A man named Yusuf Mian poured paraffin over the certificates. Then he set them alight.

The whole crowd stood up. They cheered and cheered as the papers burned.

One man came forward with his own certificate. His name was Mir Alam. Earlier that year, he had attacked Gandhi. Now he said sorry, and threw his certificate into the fire.

## The Boston Tea Party

A newspaper reporter in Johannesburg wrote about the bonfire for the London Daily Mail. He compared it to the Boston Tea Party. That was when American colonists threw British tea into the sea in 1773, in protest against unfair taxes.

Gandhi felt the comparison was fair.

## Why it matters

The certificates were only pieces of paper. Burning them did not change the law that day.

But it showed something new. Ordinary people, without weapons, could stand together and say "no" to an unfair law, in front of everyone.

Twenty-two years later, in India, Gandhi would do the same thing again. Only this time, it was with salt.
