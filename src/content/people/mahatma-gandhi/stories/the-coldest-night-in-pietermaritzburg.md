In 1893, a young Indian lawyer arrived in South Africa. He was 23 years old. He wore a smart western suit. He had studied law in London.

His name was Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi. Nobody called him Mahatma yet.

## A job far from home

Gandhi's law work in India had not gone well.

Then an offer came. An Indian merchant company in South Africa needed help with a court case. Would Gandhi come for a year?

He said yes. He sailed to the port of Durban. The head of the company, Abdulla Sheth, came to meet him.

## The turban in court

Gandhi soon saw how Indians were treated in South Africa. They were not given much respect. White people called every Indian a "coolie", whether he was a labourer, a clerk or a rich merchant. Gandhi, the London-trained lawyer, was a "coolie barrister".

A few days after he arrived, Abdulla Sheth took him to the court in Durban. Gandhi was wearing a turban. The magistrate stared at him, and told him to take it off.

Gandhi refused. He walked out of the court instead.

It was a small thing. But it was a sign of what was to come.

## The first class ticket

A few days later, Gandhi had to travel to the city of Pretoria for the case. It was a long journey by train.

His company bought him a first-class ticket. Gandhi got into the first-class carriage, with his luggage. He settled down for the trip.

The train climbed up into the hills. It was winter in South Africa, and the nights in the hills were bitterly cold.

## "You must leave this compartment"

At about nine at night, the train stopped at Maritzburg. That is the short name for Pietermaritzburg. It was the capital of the colony of Natal.

A passenger got on. He looked at Gandhi. He saw a "coloured" man, as Gandhi later wrote. And he did not want to share the carriage.

He went away and came back with railway officials. One of them told Gandhi he had to move to the van compartment at the back.

Gandhi said he had a first-class ticket. He had been allowed to sit there in Durban. He would not move.

The official said: "You must leave this compartment, or else I shall have to call a police constable to push you out."

Gandhi said: then let him push me out. I will not leave of my own accord.

<aside class="note"><b>Coloured</b>: under the unfair laws of that time, people in South Africa were sorted by the colour of their skin. Indians and Africans were treated as second-class people.</aside>

## Pushed out

The constable came. He took Gandhi by the hand and pushed him out of the train. His luggage was thrown out too.

Gandhi refused to get into the other compartment. And so the train steamed away into the night without him.

## The dark waiting room

Gandhi went into the station's waiting room. There was no light. It was freezing cold.

His overcoat was in his luggage. But the railway officials had his bags. He did not dare to ask for the coat. He was afraid he would be insulted again.

So he sat in the dark, and shivered.

## A big question

All through that long night, Gandhi thought.

Should he fight for his rights? Or should he go back to India, and forget South Africa?

He realised that what had happened to him was not just about one rude passenger. Indians all over South Africa were treated this way every day. His trouble, he wrote, was "only a symptom of the deep disease of colour prejudice".

By morning, he had decided. It would be cowardice to run back to India. He would stay. He would try to cure the disease, even if it meant suffering himself.

## The next day

In the morning, Gandhi sent a long telegram to the head of the railway, complaining about what had happened.

Then he got on the next train and continued his journey.

The troubles were not over. The next part of the trip was by stagecoach, a big carriage pulled by horses.

The man in charge would not let Gandhi sit inside with the white passengers. Later, he ordered Gandhi to sit on a dirty sack at his feet. Gandhi refused. The man beat him and tried to drag him down.

Gandhi held on tight to the brass rails of the coach. He would not let go. At last some of the passengers objected, and the man gave up.

When Gandhi reached Johannesburg, a hotel told him it was "full up". Gandhi had his doubts. The Indian merchants he stayed with that night were not surprised at all.

## Twenty years in South Africa

Gandhi stayed in South Africa not for one year, but for more than twenty. There he first tried out his way of peaceful protest, satyagraha. He would later bring it home to India.

<aside class="note"><b>Which day?</b> Most accounts give 7 June 1893. One chronology gives 31 May 1893. Both agree it was a cold winter night.</aside>

## A hundred years later

On 25 April 1997, a ceremony was held at Pietermaritzburg railway station. By then, South Africa had ended its unfair race laws. Its President was Nelson Mandela, who had spent 27 years in prison fighting against them.

Mandela gave Gandhi, after his death, the Freedom of the City of Pietermaritzburg. Gandhi's grandson Gopalkrishna Gandhi received it for him.

Mandela called the station "once one of the most notorious symbols of discrimination, intolerance and oppression in the world".

The station where a young man was thrown off a train had become a place to remember him.

In June 2018, 125 years after that night, India's High Commission in South Africa held events at Pietermaritzburg to remember it.

## Why it matters

Gandhi went on to travel all over India by train. After he came home in 1915, he usually travelled third class, with the poorest passengers.

He never forgot that cold night. A young lawyer went into that dark waiting room. A fighter came out.
