Why does an earthquake happen?

Today, you could answer that in your science class. The ground under us is made of huge slabs of rock. Sometimes they slip, and the earth shakes.

In 1934, two of the most famous men in India gave two very different answers to that question. And they said so in public.

## The day the ground moved

On 15 January 1934, at about a quarter past two in the afternoon, a huge earthquake struck. It shook Nepal and the north of Bihar.

Whole towns fell down. Munger and Muzaffarpur were almost completely destroyed. Buildings cracked as far away as Calcutta. More than ten thousand people died.

<aside class="note"><b>How many died?</b> Today's records say between 10,700 and 12,000 people. In a letter written soon after, Gandhi gave a much bigger number, 20,000 to 25,000. In the first weeks, nobody knew for sure.</aside>

## Gandhi's answer

Gandhi was far away, on a long tour of South India. In his speeches, he kept asking people to end untouchability.

<aside class="note"><b>Untouchability</b>: the cruel custom of treating some people as "untouchable" because of the caste they were born into. They were kept out of temples, wells and schools.</aside>

On 24 January, he spoke to a huge crowd in the market of Tirunelveli. He said the earthquake was "a divine chastisement sent by God for our sins". The same day, in Tuticorin, he said it more clearly. The sin, he said, was untouchability.

<aside class="note"><b>Chastisement</b>: a punishment.</aside>

## Tagore's answer

The poet Rabindranath Tagore read the newspaper reports. He was shocked.

Tagore hated untouchability just as much as Gandhi did. But he did not believe God sent earthquakes to punish people. He wrote a statement and sent it to Gandhi, asking him to print it if the reports were true.

Tagore wrote of his "painful surprise". He said that natural disasters like earthquakes come only from natural causes.

He also worried about how many people would believe it, just because Gandhi had said it. Tagore called this an "unscientific view of things".

Tagore wrote that he was "immensely grateful" to Gandhi. Gandhi had helped Indians stop being afraid. That was exactly why these words hurt him so much.

## Gandhi replies

Gandhi did not hide the letter. He printed Tagore's statement in his own paper, Harijan, on 16 February 1934. In the same issue, he printed his reply. He called it "Superstition versus Faith".

"Gurudev and I early discovered certain differences of outlook between us," he began, calmly.

He did not take back his belief. To him, he wrote, the earthquake was not just "blind forces". But he also admitted something honest. He could not prove the link between untouchability and the earthquake. He only felt it.

Some people asked: why punish Bihar, and not the South? Gandhi gave a short answer: "I am not God."

## Into Bihar

Gandhi did more than talk. He broke off his tour and went to Bihar, to comfort the people who had lost everything. In Bihar, local leaders ran relief camps, with food and shelter for families whose homes had fallen.

## Still friends

That was the end of the argument. Tagore did not reply again. The two men did not stop respecting each other. Even in his reply, Gandhi called Tagore "Gurudev", which means respected teacher.

## Why it matters

Who was right? Science agrees with Tagore. Earthquakes come from moving rock, not from anyone's sins.

But look at how they argued. Tagore disagreed with his friend in public, politely. Gandhi printed his critic's words in his own paper, next to his own. Two people can disagree strongly, and still be friends.
