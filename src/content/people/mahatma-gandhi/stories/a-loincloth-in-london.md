In September 1931, a thin old man in a loincloth and a white shawl arrived in London, the capital of the British Empire.

He did not stay in a grand hotel. He chose a plain community hall in the East End, one of the poorest parts of the city.

He was Mohandas Gandhi. And for the next three months, he would surprise almost everyone he met.

<aside class="note"><b>Loincloth</b>: a simple piece of cloth wrapped around the waist. Gandhi wore one with a shawl, like the poorest farmers in India.</aside>

## Why London?

In 1930, Gandhi had led the Salt March against British rule, and he and many other leaders were sent to jail. On 26 January 1931, they were set free. Gandhi made an agreement with the Viceroy, Lord Irwin. As part of it, the Congress agreed to join talks in London about how India would be governed in future. These talks were called the Round Table Conference.

The second round began on 7 September 1931. Gandhi went as the only representative of the Congress party.

## A room on the roof

From 12 September to 5 December 1931, Gandhi lived at Kingsley Hall, in Bow, in the East End of London. It was a Christian community centre, started in 1915 by two sisters, Muriel and Doris Lester.

Gandhi slept in one of four small rooms, like monks' cells, on the top floor. His son Devadas slept in another.

Every morning, he went out walking in the streets around the hall. English Heritage, which runs London's blue plaques, says he enjoyed "living among his own kind, the poor people".

<aside class="note"><b>East End</b>: the eastern part of London, near the docks. In 1931 it was crowded and poor.</aside>

## Tea with Charlie Chaplin

On 22 September 1931, Gandhi met one of the most famous people in the world: the film star Charlie Chaplin.

They met at a doctor's small house in Canning Town, not far from the docks. Chaplin later wrote that reporters and photographers "packed both floors" of the house.

Chaplin asked Gandhi what he thought about machines.

Gandhi explained that machines had made India depend on England. The only way to break free, he said, was to refuse to buy goods made by those machines.

Chaplin was impressed. He called the answer "a lucid object lesson in tactical manoeuvring in India's struggle for freedom". He stayed for Gandhi's prayers too.

<aside class="note"><b>Lucid</b>: very clear.</aside>

## The mill workers

Gandhi also went north, to Lancashire. This was the most surprising visit of all.

Lancashire was famous for its cotton mills. Before the First World War, almost 60 per cent of its cotton cloth was sold to India. But the mills had been in trouble for more than ten years. And in 1929, the Congress had asked Indians to stop buying British cloth.

Many mill workers had lost their jobs. A lot of them blamed Gandhi's boycott, even though there were other reasons too.

<aside class="note"><b>Boycott</b>: refusing to buy something as a protest.</aside>

A mill-owning family in the town of Darwen, the Davieses, invited him. They hoped he would understand the workers' suffering, and perhaps call off the boycott.

Thousands of people waited for him at Darwen station. At one mill, most of the workers were women. According to Gandhi's companion Mirabehn, the manager rang the bell so they could come out to see him. Two women took his arms, and the workers gave him a cheer.

Some old weavers told him about their hardship. Gandhi answered honestly. "My dear," he said, "you have no idea what poverty is." In India, he explained, millions were starving.

But he did not call off the boycott. The spinning wheel, he said, was "the symbol of salvation" for India's poor.

## Tea with the King

The most famous moment of the trip was a tea party at Buckingham Palace.

King George V invited the delegates to the conference. He did not really want to invite Gandhi. One account says the King called him "the little man" with no proper clothes on. But Gandhi had promised not to dress differently while India's poor had so little to wear. In the end, he went to the palace in his loincloth and shawl.

Afterwards, someone is said to have asked if he had been wearing enough clothes to meet the King. Gandhi is said to have replied: "The King had enough on for both of us."

<aside class="note"><b>Is it true?</b> This famous joke comes from a book of Gandhi stories published many years later, in 1969. It may have been polished in the retelling.</aside>

Not everyone in Britain found Gandhi charming. The politician Winston Churchill sneered at him as a lawyer "posing as a half-naked fakir". To Churchill, the loincloth was just a trick. To Gandhi, it was a way of standing with the poorest Indians.

## The talks fail

For all the friendly meetings, the conference itself did not go well.

Gandhi said that the Congress spoke for all of India. Many other Indian delegates disagreed. The biggest argument was with Dr B. R. Ambedkar, the leader of the Dalits, then called the "Depressed Classes". Ambedkar wanted Dalits to have their own separate seats in elections, voted for only by Dalits. Gandhi strongly opposed this. He did not want Dalits to be treated as a separate minority.

<aside class="note"><b>Two sides</b>: Ambedkar believed Dalits needed their own voice in elections. Gandhi believed they should not be separated from other Hindus. In 1932, the two men reached a compromise, the Poona Pact. You can read about it in the story of Gandhi's fast in Yerwada jail.</aside>

No agreement was reached on the big questions. In December 1931, Gandhi left London with nothing settled.

## A plaque in Bow

The East End did not forget him. In 1954, a round blue plaque was put up on Kingsley Hall. It says: "Mahatma Gandhi 1869–1948 stayed here in 1931."

It was the first of London's famous blue plaques to honour an Indian. Usually, a person must have been dead for twenty years to get one. For Gandhi, that rule was set aside.

## Why it matters

Gandhi went to London to argue about India's future, and on that, he failed.

But he also did something else. He showed the British people a different face of India. He chose to live with the poor, to talk honestly to workers who were angry with him, and to meet a King without changing his clothes.
