This is a story about two great Indians who both wanted to help the same people, and could not agree on the best way.

One was Mahatma Gandhi. The other was Dr Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar.

## Who was Ambedkar?

Ambedkar was born into a Dalit family. As a boy, he was treated as "untouchable". At school, he was not allowed to sit inside the classroom with the others. He had to bring a sack from home to sit on. He could drink water only if the school servant poured it for him from a height. When the servant was away, he went thirsty.

But he was brilliant. He earned doctorates from Columbia University in America and the London School of Economics in England.

He spent his life fighting for the rights of Dalits. Later, he would chair the committee that wrote India's Constitution.

## The big question

In the early 1930s, the British were planning new elections in India. A big question came up. How could Dalits make sure their voice was heard?

Ambedkar had an answer. He wanted separate electorates for Dalits. That meant Dalits would vote for their own representatives, in their own separate elections.

He argued that leaders from upper castes could not truly speak for Dalits, however kind they were. Dalits needed their own voice.

<aside class="note"><b>Electorate</b>: the group of people who vote in an election. A "separate electorate" means a group votes on its own, only for its own candidates.</aside>

## The Communal Award

In August 1932, the British government made its decision. It was called the Communal Award. It gave Dalits separate electorates, with 71 seats set aside for them.

Ambedkar was pleased.

## Gandhi's fear

Gandhi was deeply upset. He was in prison at the time, in Yerwada Central Jail in Poona.

Gandhi believed that separate electorates would split Hindu society for ever. He thought Dalits and other Hindus would drift apart, instead of coming together to end untouchability.

So he did what he often did when he felt strongly. He announced that he would fast unto death, unless the award was changed.

## Days of worry

The whole country held its breath. Gandhi was over sixty. Could his body survive a long fast?

Ambedkar was in a terrible position. If he said no and Gandhi died, many people would blame him. If he said yes, he would have to give up what he believed Dalits needed.

Talks went on for days, between Ambedkar and leaders on Gandhi's side.

## The Poona Pact

At last, an agreement was reached. It was signed at five in the afternoon on 24 September 1932, at Yerwada jail.

There would be no separate electorates. Instead, everyone would vote together. But a number of seats would be reserved for Dalit candidates.

The number of reserved seats was much bigger than before: 148 instead of 71.

This agreement is called the Poona Pact. Gandhi ended his fast.

## Two sides of the story

Many people praised the pact. They said it kept Indians together, and gave Dalits more seats.

But others, then and now, see it differently. They say Ambedkar was forced into a corner by the fast. Ambedkar himself later criticised Gandhi sharply in his writings. Some writers think the Dalits lost something important: the power to choose their own leaders by themselves.

<aside class="note"><b>Still debated</b>: historians and writers still argue about the Poona Pact. Some say Ambedkar was content with it. Others say he agreed only under great pressure. It is worth reading both sides.</aside>

## Why it matters

Gandhi and Ambedkar disagreed strongly, all their lives. But both of them wanted to end the cruelty of untouchability.

Reserved seats for Scheduled Castes are still part of India's elections today. They have their roots in that tense week in a Poona jail.
