Think of Gandhi, and you may picture him sitting at a spinning wheel. His hand turns the wheel. A thin thread of cotton comes out.

That picture is so famous that it is hard to believe this. When Gandhi first told Indians to spin, he had never seen a spinning wheel himself.

## A wheel he had only imagined

In 1909, Gandhi wrote a little book called Hind Swaraj. In it, he said the spinning wheel and the handloom could help cure India's poverty.

Years later, he admitted something. He did not remember ever having seen a spinning wheel when he wrote that.

<aside class="note"><b>Khadi</b>: cloth that is spun by hand and woven by hand. Gandhi wanted Indians to wear khadi instead of cloth made in British mills.</aside>

## No wheels anywhere

When Gandhi set up his ashram in India, he wanted to start spinning. But where were the wheels?

Indians had spun cotton by hand for thousands of years. But cheap cloth from British factories had taken over. Most people had stopped spinning long ago. Nobody in the ashram knew how.

Gandhi asked around. Nobody could find a working spinning wheel, or someone to teach spinning.

## Gangabehn

In 1917, at a meeting in Broach, in Gujarat, Gandhi met a remarkable woman. Her name was Gangabehn Majmundar.

She was a widow. Gandhi said she had great courage and plenty of common sense. She had worked against untouchability. She travelled about on her own, without anyone to look after her. She was quite at home on horseback.

Later, Gandhi told her about his problem. He could not find a spinning wheel.

Gangabehn made him a promise. She would search until she found one.

## Found at last!

Gangabehn travelled all over Gujarat. For a long time, she found nothing.

At last, she reached a place called Vijapur, in what was then Baroda State. There she found what she was looking for.

Many families in Vijapur had spinning wheels! But nobody used them. They had put them away up in their lofts, as useless old junk, long ago.

The women of Vijapur said they would gladly spin again, if someone would bring them cotton and buy their thread.

Gangabehn sent Gandhi the happy news.

## More problems

Now there was another problem. To spin, you need cotton that has been cleaned and combed into soft strips, called slivers.

At first, a friend sent slivers from his mill. But Gandhi did not like depending on a mill. So he found people who could card cotton by hand.

Maganlal Gandhi, who helped run the ashram, then got to work on the wheel itself. He made many improvements to it. Soon the ashram was building its own spinning wheels.

## Why it matters

The spinning wheel became one of the great symbols of India's freedom struggle. Millions of people spun their own thread and wore khadi.

But none of it might have happened without a brave widow who would not stop searching, and a forgotten pile of wheels in the lofts of Vijapur.
