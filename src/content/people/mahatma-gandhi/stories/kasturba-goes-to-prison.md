Kasturba Gandhi was born in Porbandar in 1869, the same year as her husband. They were married when they were only children. She was 14. He was 13.

In history books, she often stands in the background. But in 1913, in South Africa, she stepped right into the front line.

## The judge's ruling

In March 1913, a judge in South Africa named Malcolm Searle made a ruling. He said that marriages performed by Hindu and Muslim rites would not be recognised by the law.

That meant something shocking. Thousands of Indian couples in South Africa were, in the eyes of the law, not married at all. Their children could not be their heirs.

When Kasturba heard this, she said something very simple. "Then I am not your wife, according to the laws of this country."

## Women join the fight

Until then, Indian women in South Africa had not taken part in the protests. That changed now. The marriage ruling hurt them directly. They were angry, and they wanted to act.

Kasturba said she would rather go to prison than be called an "unlawful wife".

<aside class="note"><b>Permit</b>: a paper that gave someone permission to enter a place. Indians needed a permit to cross from Natal into the Transvaal.</aside>

## Crossing the border

On 15 September 1913, a small group left Phoenix, Gandhi's settlement near Durban. There were sixteen of them: four women and twelve men. Kasturba was one of the women.

They took the train towards the Transvaal. They had no permits. They knew exactly what would happen.

At Volksrust station, on the border, they were ordered off the train. They refused to go back. Police took them across the border into Natal. They simply walked back again.

So they were arrested.

## Three months of hard labour

On 23 September 1913, they were sentenced. Three months in prison, with hard labour.

Prison was hard. The food was of the worst quality, Gandhi later wrote. The women had to wash clothes all day. Kasturba was not well. She needed special food, but it was not given to her. The oil she was given was rotten.

In prison, she did not give up. She led the other women in prayer. She encouraged women who could read to teach those who could not.

## Coming out

Kasturba was released on 22 December 1913. She was very weak. Her friends had planned a big welcome procession in Durban. It had to be cancelled.

For a while, she was so ill that people feared she might die. Gandhi nursed her back to health.

## What happened next

The women's protest lit a fire. Soon thousands of Indian mine workers went on strike. In November 1913, more than two thousand men, women and children marched together across the border.

In June 1914, the government gave way. A new law, the Indian Relief Act, recognised Hindu and Muslim marriages. It also ended a hated tax on Indian workers.

Kasturba went to prison many more times in her life, in India too. She died in 1944, still a prisoner of the British, at the Aga Khan Palace in Pune.

But it all began in 1913, with one quiet woman who refused to be called anything less than a wife.
