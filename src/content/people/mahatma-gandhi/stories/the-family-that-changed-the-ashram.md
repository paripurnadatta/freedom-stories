In 1915, Gandhi came home to India for good. He had spent more than twenty years in South Africa.

He wanted to start a new life in India. He wanted a place where people would live simply, work hard, pray together and serve the country. Such a place is called an ashram.

## A house in Kochrab

A man named Jivanlal Desai had a bungalow in a village called Kochrab. He offered it to Gandhi. The rent was just one rupee a year.

In May 1915, Gandhi and his companions moved in. The Satyagraha Ashram was born.

Rich people in Ahmedabad gave money to help it run.

## A letter

The ashram was only a few months old when a letter arrived. It came from a social worker named Amritlal Thakkar. Many people called him Thakkar Bapa.

He wrote: "A humble and honest untouchable family is desirous of joining your Ashram. Will you accept them?"

<aside class="note"><b>Untouchable</b>: for hundreds of years, some castes in India were treated as "untouchable". Others refused to touch them, eat with them, or let them use the same wells and temples. Today we say Dalit, and treating anyone as untouchable is against the law in India.</aside>

Gandhi read the letter. He knew it was a test.

## Dudabhai, Danibehn and Lakshmi

The family was Dudabhai, his wife Danibehn, and their little daughter, Lakshmi.

Gandhi said they could come, if they followed the ashram's rules, like everyone else. They agreed. And so they joined.

## Trouble at the well

It did not take long for trouble to start.

The ashram drew its water from a well. The man who worked the water pulley there was angry. He said that water touched by Dudabhai would make him impure. He began to swear at the family and bully Dudabhai.

Gandhi told everyone in the ashram not to fight back. They should put up with the insults, and keep drawing water.

In the end, the man felt ashamed. He stopped.

## The money stops

But a bigger problem came. The rich people who had been giving money to the ashram stopped. All of them.

There were rumours that the whole ashram would be boycotted. Nobody would deal with them.

One day, Maganlal Gandhi, who looked after the ashram's accounts, came to him. The money had run out, he said. What would they do next month?

Gandhi said calmly: "Then we shall go to the untouchables' quarter."

He meant it. If no one would help, the whole ashram would move to live among the Dalit families of the city.

## A car at the gate

Then something unexpected happened.

One morning, a rich businessman drove up to the ashram in a car. He asked if Gandhi needed help. The next day, he came back. He handed over 13,000 rupees, and drove away.

The businessman did not even want his name known. Much later, people learned it was Ambalal Sarabhai, a mill owner of Ahmedabad.

## Why it matters

Gandhi could have said no to Dudabhai's family. It would have been easier. The money would have kept coming.

He said yes. And he stayed with that yes, even when it seemed the ashram might have to close.

Later, when Lakshmi was six, Gandhi promised to raise her as his own adopted daughter. She grew up in the ashram with Gandhi and Kasturba. The girl that people tried to keep out became part of his family.
