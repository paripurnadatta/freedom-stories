What if a court could hear a case, but the usual way to challenge its decision was closed?

That question became important for Shivaram Rajguru. He was accused with Bhagat Singh and Sukhdev in the Lahore Conspiracy Case.

The case followed the killing of police officer John Saunders in December 1928. The three men were later sentenced to death.

## The usual idea of an appeal

Courts hear evidence and make decisions. An appeal asks a higher court to examine a decision again.

An appeal does not mean that a person is innocent. It gives people a way to challenge a legal result.

<aside class="note"><b>Appeal</b>: a request for a higher court to review a decision.</aside>

The British government used a special way to try this case. The Ministry of Home Affairs says the Governor-General issued an ordinance.

<aside class="note"><b>Ordinance</b>: a rule issued by a government authority under special legal powers.</aside>

The ordinance set up a new tribunal. A tribunal is a group of judges given the task of hearing a case.

## Three judges

Legal writer Akash Vajpai describes Ordinance III of 1930. He says the tribunal contained three High Court judges.

The tribunal could carry on some hearings without the accused present. It also replaced the ordinary route of appeal.

That made the new process different from an ordinary criminal trial.

The accused and their supporters questioned the process. The government defended the special court as lawful.

We should not treat either view as a substitute for the original court records. Those full records still need checking.

## A sentence, then another route

The tribunal sentenced Rajguru, Bhagat Singh and Sukhdev to death on 7 October 1930.

Did losing the usual appeal mean that nobody could make any further request? No.

Vajpai describes a special request to the Privy Council in London. This was a high court body for the British Empire.

<aside class="note"><b>Privy Council</b>: a body in London that heard some final legal appeals from parts of the British Empire.</aside>

That request was rejected in February 1931. Other legal efforts continued, according to the same writer.

<aside class="note"><b>Two descriptions</b>: the Ministry of Home Affairs says the special process denied a right to appeal. The later legal account explains that the ordinary appeal was removed, but a special request to London was still attempted.</aside>

## What this tells us about power

A court decides whether people broke laws. But the rules for the court also matter.

Who chooses the judges? Can the accused be present? What can happen after a sentence?

Those are questions about the way a case is heard. They are different from whether someone supported the accused person's politics.

## Why it matters

It is possible to disagree with someone's actions and still ask whether their trial was fair.

Rajguru faced a death sentence. The legal process deserves close study, not slogans.

By asking what courts could hear and what they could not, we learn how power worked under British rule.
