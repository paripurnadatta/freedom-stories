Imagine a trial where the judges can carry on even when the people accused are not in the room.

That was how it worked in the Lahore Conspiracy Case in 1930. The accused were Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru.

## A special court

The case was about the killing of a police officer, John Saunders, in Lahore in December 1928.

In 1930, the Viceroy, Lord Irwin, issued an order called Ordinance III. It set up a Special Tribunal to try the case. LiveLaw, a legal news site, says the aim was "to expedite the trial". That means to make it quicker.

<aside class="note"><b>Ordinance</b>: a rule made by a ruler or government without waiting for a vote in a law-making house.</aside>

<aside class="note"><b>Tribunal</b>: a special court. This one had three High Court judges.</aside>

The three judges were Justice G. C. Hilton, Justice Abdul Qadir and Justice J. K. Tapp.

## Two doors closed

First, the ordinance took away the usual right of appeal. In a normal case, a person who is sentenced can ask a higher court to look again.

<aside class="note"><b>Appeal</b>: a request to a higher court to review a decision.</aside>

Second, it allowed the hearings to go on without the accused. LiveLaw says several hearings were held in this way.

## The witnesses

By 26 August 1930, the people who ran the case against the three men had called 457 witnesses. Then they closed their case.

No witnesses spoke for the accused. LiveLaw also says the case leaned heavily on five approvers.

<aside class="note"><b>Approver</b>: a person who was part of a group, then agrees to give evidence for the government against the others.</aside>

On 7 October 1930, the tribunal sentenced Rajguru, Bhagat Singh and Sukhdev to death. The National Book Trust book gives the same date.

## One door left

There was still one way left. The accused could ask the Privy Council in London to hear their case. This was a special request, not a normal appeal.

<aside class="note"><b>Privy Council</b>: a group of senior judges in London who could hear some cases from the British Empire.</aside>

LiveLaw says the planned hanging was put off while the request waited. The Privy Council turned it down on 11 February 1931.

The three men were hanged on 23 March 1931.

## A last request that was refused

There was one more attempt. On 16 February 1931, a lawyer named Sham Lal asked for the hanging to be put off.

His reason was that the three men were needed as witnesses in a second case, the Second Lahore Conspiracy Case. LiveLaw says the people running that case planned to call about 710 witnesses.

On 20 February 1931, the Chief Secretary of the Punjab government turned the request down.

<aside class="note"><b>Two views</b>: Today's Ministry of Home Affairs calls the trial "farcical" and says the accused were denied the right to appeal. The ordinance said its aim was to speed things up. LiveLaw adds that a request to London was still allowed. We have not read the full court papers.</aside>

## Why it matters

You can disagree with what a person did and still ask whether the court was fair.

Who picks the judges? Can the accused be there? Can anyone check the result? Those questions matter in every trial, in every age.
