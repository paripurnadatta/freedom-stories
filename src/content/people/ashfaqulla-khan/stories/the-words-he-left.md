Many books say that when Ashfaqulla Khan stood on the platform at Faizabad jail, he recited a verse. The National Book Trust book gives it. It says the verse means that every year, fairs will be held on the graves of the martyrs, and that the country will soon be free.

It is a beautiful verse. But it raises a question. Did Ashfaq write it?

## A verse with two homes

The Tribune ran an article on revolutionary poetry. It mentions a couplet with almost the same words. It says the couplet is by a poet named Jagdamba Prasad Hitaishi. In his version, the fairs are held "at the funeral sites" of the martyrs. In the book's version, they are held "on the graves".

<aside class="note"><b>Couplet</b>: a poem of two lines. In Urdu and Hindi it is called a sher or a doha.</aside>

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The National Book Trust book says Ashfaq recited the verse at the gallows. It does not say who wrote it. The Tribune credits a very close version to Hitaishi. We do not know whether Ashfaq spoke an old verse that he loved, or whether the two versions have different roots. We cannot settle it here.</aside>

This does not make the scene less moving. A prisoner who chooses to say a poet's words at the end is choosing something. But it is a good example of why we should ask who wrote a famous line before we repeat it.

## What was surely his

Some of Ashfaq's words are on firmer ground, because more than one source gives them.

English Wikipedia says that at the gallows he said his hands were not stained with any man's murder. It says he said the charge against him was false, and that God would give him justice. Then he recited the Muslim declaration of faith, the Kalima.

<aside class="note"><b>Kalima</b>: the short prayer in which a Muslim says there is one God.</aside>

The newspaper Counterview says that before the hanging, he asked Hindus and Muslims to stop fighting each other and to work together for their country.

ThePrint also reports that three days before the hanging, he wrote an open letter to the public. It says he wrote about his sadness over how politics was going. ThePrint attributes this to a historian, S. Irfan Habib. We have not seen the letter itself.

## His poems

The National Book Trust book says Ashfaq wrote mostly in Urdu, and a little in Hindi. It gives his pen names as Varsi and Hazrat.

It quotes lines in which he says he lives only to serve his motherland. It quotes other lines in which he says life and death do not matter if the country becomes free.

A memorial website gives one more line, which it says he wrote in Faizabad jail three days before the hanging. In English it says: "Worn down by their tyranny, we depart toward nothingness from Faizabad's prison."

## What we can and cannot say

We cannot say for sure that every verse people quote is his. We have not seen his handwriting. Some words come from books written many years later. Some come from websites that do not say where they got them.

But the main picture agrees across the sources. He was a poet. He did not want to be called a murderer. And at the end, he spoke about faith, and about Hindus and Muslims working together.

## Why it matters

Famous lines travel. They are copied, shortened, and given to whoever people admire most. When we repeat one, it helps to say where it comes from, and to say when we do not know.
