Many books say that when Ashfaqulla Khan stood on the platform at Faizabad jail, he recited a verse. The National Book Trust book gives it. The verse means that every year fairs will be held on the graves of the martyrs, and that the country will soon be free.

It is a beautiful verse. But did Ashfaq write it?

## A verse with two homes

A couplet with almost the same words appears in an article in the Tribune on revolutionary poetry. The article says the couplet is by a poet named Jagdamba Prasad Hitaishi. In his version, the fairs are held "at the funeral sites" of the martyrs. In the book's version, they are held "on the graves".

<aside class="note"><b>Couplet</b>: a poem of two lines. In Urdu and Hindi it is called a sher or a doha.</aside>

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: the National Book Trust book says Ashfaq recited the verse at the gallows but does not say who wrote it. The Tribune credits a very close version to Hitaishi. Either Ashfaq spoke an old verse that he loved, or the two versions have different roots. We cannot settle it here.</aside>

This does not make the scene less moving. A prisoner who chooses to say a poet's words at the end is choosing something. But it shows why we should ask who wrote a famous line before we repeat it.

## What he said at the end

Other words rest on firmer ground, because more than one source gives them.

At the gallows, English Wikipedia says, he said that his hands were not stained with any man's murder. He said the charge against him was false, and that God would give him justice. Then he recited the Muslim declaration of faith, the Kalima.

<aside class="note"><b>Kalima</b>: the short prayer in which a Muslim says there is one God.</aside>

The newspaper Counterview adds that before the hanging he asked Hindus and Muslims to stop fighting each other and to work together for their country.

Three days before the hanging, ThePrint reports, he wrote an open letter to the public. It speaks of his sadness over how politics was going. ThePrint attributes this to the historian S. Irfan Habib. We have not seen the letter itself.

## His poems

Ashfaq wrote mostly in Urdu, and a little in Hindi. The National Book Trust book gives his pen names as Varsi and Hazrat.

In some lines, he says he lives only to serve his motherland. In others, he says life and death do not matter if the country becomes free.

A memorial website gives one more line. It says he wrote it in Faizabad jail three days before the hanging. In English it reads: "Worn down by their tyranny, we depart toward nothingness from Faizabad's prison."

## What is sure, and what is not

We have not seen his handwriting. Some words come from books written many years later. Some come from websites that do not say where they got them. So we cannot say for sure that every verse people quote is his.

But the main picture agrees across the sources. He was a poet. He did not want to be called a murderer. And at the end, he spoke about faith, and about Hindus and Muslims working together.

## Why it matters

Famous lines travel. They are copied, shortened, and given to whoever people admire most. When we repeat one, it helps to say where it comes from, and to say when we do not know.
