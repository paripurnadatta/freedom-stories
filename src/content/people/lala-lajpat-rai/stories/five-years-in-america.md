In April 1914, Lala Lajpat Rai left India. He was going to England to join a Congress group. He planned to be away for about six months.

It was more than five years before he saw India again.

## Why he left

Harish K. Puri, a professor who wrote a long study of these years, describes hard years before Rai left. In February 1911, his son Krishna, who was twenty, fell ill with tuberculosis while studying at Cambridge. He was brought home and died.

Then came a court case in Lahore and Delhi. Young men close to Rai were accused in it. Puri says Rai feared that the police would link him to the case. Rai wrote that life had become "intolerable." Puri says Rai left India "in panic". That is Puri's view, and Rai himself may have put it differently.

## Stuck in London, then New York

In England, Rai did not want to go back to Punjab while Michael O'Dwyer ruled it as Lieutenant Governor. In early August 1914 the Great War began. A writer named N. S. Hardikar, quoted by Puri, says the British government banned his return to India "in the name of the war."

In November 1914, Rai sailed from London to New York. He meant to spend six months studying America for a book. He wanted to learn about its schools, its people, and how Indians who had settled there were treated.

Friends opened doors for him. A letter from Sidney Webb led to Professor Edwin Seligman. Another contact led to W. E. B. Du Bois. Rai visited Black schools in Atlanta, Georgia. His book came out in Calcutta in 1916 with the title *The United States of America: A Hindu's Impressions and a Study*.

In America he also met Indian revolutionaries who had links with Germany. Puri names three: C. K. Chakrabarty, Heramba Lal Gupta and Mohammad Barkatullah. Barkatullah put a plan to him that involved the ruler of Afghanistan. Rai turned it down.

## Japan and a sickness

In July 1915, after seven months in America, Rai went to Japan. He met the Prime Minister, Okuma, and the Chinese leader Sun Yat-sen, and gave lectures. But he judged Japan unsafe, because of its treaty with Britain. He went back to California in December 1915.

His health was poor. A professor at Berkeley, Arthur Pope, moved him quietly to a sanatorium near Santa Barbara. Money was short, and Rai earned some from paid lectures. One writer, quoted by Puri, says that Tilak sent him $5,000 about two years later through Annie Besant's circle.

## The Germans and the detectives

Some Indians in America worked with Germany against Britain. Rai said no. He wrote to a friend, Frieda Hausewirth, that German money "has done our people and their cause more harm than good."

That did not protect him. When the United States joined the war in April 1917, Indians in America came under suspicion. In March 1917, after a lecture by Rai at Columbia, plainclothes police seized three men, including M. N. Roy. Puri says Rai was also upset by the quarrels among the leaders of the Ghadar group in California, and he moved east to New York. The Hindu-German Conspiracy Trial began in November 1917. Of 105 people charged, 36 were Indians. Rai wrote that anyone who criticised Britain "is dubbed as Pro-German."

In his last speech in America, Rai said that for a year and a half he had lived in a house partly used by a government detective. He believed his telephone was tapped. He said he had been taken in for questioning six times. He thanked the Department of Justice for its courtesy.

## The League and the magazine

Rai kept working in the open. In October 1917, he started the India Home Rule League of America. Its purpose was to tell Americans about India.

In January 1918 came a monthly magazine, *Young India*. Rai edited it for 24 months. In June 1918 he set up an India Information Bureau, to answer American newspapers' questions. Hardikar was the League's secretary. Rai wrote a book called *England's Debt to India*. He used British government papers for it, and said he had "chosen to speak from the mouths of the English themselves."

In August 1918 Rai changed the aims of the magazine to match the ideas of President Woodrow Wilson, who talked of self-determination. Rai sent Wilson a telegram praising his words. But Rai also asked a hard question of Americans: "Democracy at home without democracy abroad is an impossibility."

Hardikar toured too. A writer named Rathore, quoted by Puri, says that in 1919 he gave 83 lectures in 86 days. After the war, Rai and Hardikar spoke to the Foreign Relations Committee of the US Senate. A lawyer, Dudley Field Malone, helped them. Puri names four senators who supported India's right to decide its own future. One of them, Senator France of Maryland, thanked the editor of *Young India* for informing Americans.

<aside class="note"><b>Self-determination</b>: the idea that a people should decide for themselves how they are governed.</aside>

## The way home

Rai was allowed to leave at last in December 1919. At a farewell dinner, Oswald Garrison Villard, the toastmaster, called him "a wise, brave and sound ambassador."

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: How long was he abroad? Puri gives 1914 to 1919, and says he left in December 1919. But Puri's text also says he returned to India in 1920. Wikipedia says he lived in the United States from 1917 to 1920, which counts only the later part. And Wikipedia lists two books for 1916, called *Young India* and *The United States of America*. Puri's *Young India* is a different thing: a magazine that started in 1918.</aside>

## Why it matters

Rai's time in America shows how a political leader could be stuck far from home, short of money, and watched. He did not stop working. He wrote to Americans, spoke to senators, and edited a magazine. Puri adds that the Congress liked his ideas for information offices in New York, Tokyo and Paris, but did little about them until the 1940s.

In 1920 he was back in India. That same year, he was elected president of the Congress at a special session in Calcutta.
