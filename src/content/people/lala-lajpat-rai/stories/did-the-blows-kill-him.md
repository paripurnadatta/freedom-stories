In 1928 a group of men from Britain came to India to study how the country should be governed. Not one of them was Indian. Many Indians said: if you decide our future, you must let us sit at the table. Lala Lajpat Rai led one of those protests, in Lahore. He did not live to see what came of it.

## The Commission

The group was called the Simon Commission, after its chairman, Sir John Simon. It had seven members, all British Members of Parliament. Wikipedia says the government set it up in November 1927, two years earlier than planned. The Viceroy, Lord Irwin, supported leaving Indians out.

The Congress met at Madras in December 1927. It decided to boycott the Commission. When the members landed at Bombay on 3 February 1928, crowds held black flags that said "Simon Go Back." Wikipedia says similar protests took place in every large city the members visited. In September 1928, Motilal Nehru presented a report of Indian leaders' own plan for a constitution. Wikipedia says it was meant to answer the claim that Indians could not agree among themselves.

<aside class="note"><b>Boycott</b>: to refuse to take part in something, or to have anything to do with it, as a way of protesting.</aside>

## 30 October 1928

The Commission reached Lahore on 30 October. Lajpat Rai led a march against it. Wikipedia and the Congress paper both call the march non-violent. The Swarajya article, which is written by an admirer, calls it silent. Wikipedia's article on the Commission says that the protesters waved black flags and blocked the road, to stop the members leaving the railway station.

The police superintendent was James A. Scott. Wikipedia says he ordered the crowd to be dispersed by a baton charge. A *lathi* is the long wooden stick that Indian police carried. Lajpat Rai was hit. Wikipedia says he was badly hurt.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Who struck him? Wikipedia and the Congress paper say Scott himself assaulted him. A Tribune article says the blows were given by a deputy superintendent named Saunders, on Scott's order. Swarajya says both Scott and Saunders beat him. In London, a Member of Parliament asked about a police officer who had struck him twice, and did not name him in the part we read.</aside>

That evening he spoke to a crowd at Mochi Gate. Wikipedia gives his words as: "the blows struck at me today will be the last nails in the coffin of British rule in India." Other writers give the words differently. That is a separate story.

## 17 November

He died on 17 November 1928, in Lahore. Several sources agree on the date. They do not agree on the cause.

Wikipedia says that doctors believed the blows had hastened his death. A Dawn writer says it was "widely assumed" that Scott's blows had done so. Wikipedia's article on the Commission says he died of a heart attack. It does not link the two.

One more article, also from Dawn, says he died at the Lahore railway station from a blow to his head. That cannot be right, since the protest was on 30 October and he died on 17 November. We list it only to show how stories change as they are repeated.

## The questions in London

On 26 November 1928, nine days after his death, the matter came up in the House of Commons in London. Members asked questions, and the Under-Secretary of State for India answered. The printed record leaves out the speakers' names.

One Member asked if the Under-Secretary knew that Lajpat Rai had said he was struck twice by a police officer, and whether there would be an inquiry. Another asked whether he would look into the circumstances of the death.

The Under-Secretary said "no evidence has been produced to show that the death of Lala Lajpat Rai was due to blows received on that occasion." He said the police did not make "any deliberate or unprovoked attack," and that "no individual was singled out for assault." He said two inquiries had already been held. One was inside the police department. The other was a public one, held by the Commissioner of the Rawalpindi Division.

He added: "my Noble Friend sees no need to hold any further inquiry."

Then came more questions. Had the Punjab Government said sorry to the family? The answer was that a government which has to use force against crowds is not justified in apologising to individuals or their relatives. Would the government say it regretted his death? The Under-Secretary said he did not accept that the death was due to police action, and that no evidence showed it. Had the police order been changed? "No. That is not so." And was there an inquest, a legal inquiry into a death? The answer was that Indian law does not require one.

<aside class="note"><b>Inquest</b>: an official inquiry into how a person died.</aside>

## What we can say

We opened no medical report. We saw no photographs of his injuries. The sources we have are Wikipedia, newspaper and website articles, and the London record. So we cannot settle the cause.

We can say what was claimed. In India, many believed the blows led to his death. In London, the government said there was no proof. The Under-Secretary's words are in the record.

## What came next

The anger did not stop. A memorial meeting was held in Calcutta on 29 November 1928, says the Tribune. The widow of C. R. Das, Basanti Devi, asked whether the youth of the country still existed.

On 17 December 1928, a month after he died, two young revolutionaries shot a police officer in Lahore. Wikipedia says they meant to shoot Scott, and shot his assistant, John Saunders, by mistake. Those men were Bhagat Singh and Shivaram Rajguru. Their story is told elsewhere on this site.

## Why it matters

The Simon Commission has become known as a "no Indians" commission. But in Lahore it became more than that. A protest about who should decide India's future was followed by a death and a quarrel over its cause. Seen from London, there was no proof. Seen from Lahore, there was a man struck down. Both claims were made, and we have set them side by side.
