Open an old Tamil weekly of 1906 and look at the front page. A drawing is there, a joke or a sharp comment about politics, and the writing on the second page explains it.

Why would a newspaper do that? Because a picture can be read by someone who cannot read long articles. India, the weekly in this story, did it before almost anyone else.

## A teacher who became a translator

Subramania Bharati had been a Tamil teacher. Madras Musings says he worked as a Tamil pundit at the Setupati High School in Madurai. There he met G. Subramania Iyer, the editor of a Tamil paper, Swadesamitran. Bharati became a sub-editor. The article says that meant translating the speeches of great leaders such as Vivekananda, Aurobindo and Tilak. The Press Information Bureau gives the month: November 1904.

<aside class="note"><b>Sub-editor</b>: a person on a newspaper who prepares the writing of others for printing. <b>Pundit</b>: a learned person, here a teacher of the Tamil language.</aside>

He also went to the Indian National Congress. The PIB says he took part in the Benaras session of 1905 and the Surat session of 1907. Wikipedia adds that at Surat he backed the side led by Tilak, which wanted firmer action.

## India is born

The PIB says the weekly India "saw the light of the day" in May 1906. Tamil Wiki gives a day, 9 May 1906, in Chennai. It says the owner was Mandayam Thirumalachariyar, and that the paper came out on Saturdays. The first issue came out on a Wednesday.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The PIB and Tamil Wiki say May 1906. The Commons page that holds the picture above says the weekly was first published at Madras in April 1907. Wikipedia says that by April 1907 Bharati was editing India. They may be describing different things: the first issue and the time he took charge. We have not seen the first issue itself.</aside>

Who was the editor? Tamil Wiki says Bharati joined in September 1906 and became its editor. It adds that another man's name, M. Srinivasa Iyengar, was printed as the editor in that period. So the owner, the editor whose name was printed, and the poet who wrote were not always the same person. That is why we say "edited by Bharati" with care. The PIB, for one, says Bharati "launched" India. Tamil Wiki says Thirumalachariyar owned it. Both agree that Bharati was at its centre.

## The cartoon and the red paper

Tamil Wiki says each issue had a political cartoon on the first page and an explanation on the second. It calls India the first magazine to use cartoons for political propaganda. The PIB words it a little differently. It says India was the first Tamil paper in Tamil Nadu to carry political cartoons.

The PIB also says Bharati printed the weekly on red paper. Tamil Wiki does not mention this. We found it in one source only, so we tell it as the PIB's account and nothing more.

The paper had a motto. The PIB says it was the three slogans of the French Revolution: Liberty, Equality and Fraternity. In the picture at the top of this page you can see the French words above the title.

<aside class="note"><b>Propaganda</b>: material meant to spread an idea or win people to a cause.</aside>

## Trouble in Madras

The writing was bold, and the government noticed. Tamil Wiki says the authorities in Chennai effectively forced the magazine out. It stopped publishing in Madras in late September 1908.

TamilNation tells what happened to the people. It says the proprietor of India was arrested in 1908 and that Bharati himself was facing arrest. The PIB says a warrant was issued for the editor. Tamil Wiki says that a warrant was issued for Thirumalachari too, but it does not say he was arrested. It says M. Srinivasa Iyengar, the editor whose name was printed, was sentenced to five years in prison.

So Bharati and Thirumalachari went to Pondicherry. Pondicherry was a French territory then.

## The paper crosses the border

The first Pondicherry issue came out on 20 October 1908. Tamil Wiki says the press stood at 71 Ambalatharu Street. It could be set up only after a French Indian, Villianur S. Lakshmi Narayana Iyer, stood bail.

The British did not let it rest. Wikipedia and TamilNation say that India and Vijaya, another paper Bharati worked on, were banned in British India in 1909. Tamil Wiki says the Pondicherry edition was barred from entering British India. It ceased publishing on 12 March 1910.

One popular website says that copies were smuggled into Madras hidden under hay in bullock carts. It gives no source, and it also says some things "some say". We do not use it as a fact.

## Other papers, other friends

India was not his only work. Wikipedia says that by April 1907 he was also editing an English paper, Bala Bharatham, together with M. P. T. Acharya. The PIB says that in 1908 he published a book of poems, Sudesa Geethangal. Its poems urged people to join the freedom struggle.

He also moved among the leaders of the day. The PIB says he discussed national questions with Bipin Chandra Pal, B. G. Tilak and V. V. S. Iyer. Wikipedia's page on V. O. Chidambaram Pillai, who started an Indian shipping company in 1906, lists Bharati among the political allies of the time. The same page says Bharati appeared in court for questioning in the case against Chidambaram. We have not read what he said there.

## What we cannot say

We do not know how many people read India. Tamil Wiki does not give the number, and it does not give the price. We have not seen a full run of the paper. We do not know what any particular cartoon showed.

## Why it matters

A newspaper is only paper and ink. But the people who made India wrote and drew things that made a government nervous. Bharati was a poet, and he was also a working journalist. He translated, edited and argued, week after week.

The paper's history is also a small map of how the government dealt with the press in those years: a warrant, a prison term, a ban at the border.
