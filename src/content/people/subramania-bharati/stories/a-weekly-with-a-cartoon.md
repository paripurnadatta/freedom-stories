Open an old Tamil weekly of 1906 and look at the front page. A drawing is there, a joke or a sharp comment about politics, and the writing on the second page explains it.

Why would a newspaper do that? Because a picture can be read by someone who cannot read long articles. India, the weekly in this story, did it before almost anyone else.

## A teacher who became a translator

Subramania Bharati had been a Tamil teacher. He worked as a Tamil pundit at the Setupati High School in Madurai. There he met G. Subramania Iyer, the editor of a Tamil paper, Swadesamitran. In November 1904, Bharati became a sub-editor. That meant translating the speeches of great leaders such as Vivekananda, Aurobindo and Tilak.

<aside class="note"><b>Sub-editor</b>: a person on a newspaper who prepares the writing of others for printing. <b>Pundit</b>: a learned person, here a teacher of the Tamil language.</aside>

He also went to the Indian National Congress. He took part in the Benaras session of 1905 and the Surat session of 1907. At Surat he backed the side led by Tilak, which wanted firmer action.

## India is born

The weekly India "saw the light of the day" in May 1906, on 9 May in Chennai. The owner was Mandayam Thirumalachariyar, and the paper came out on Saturdays. The first issue came out on a Wednesday.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: the Commons page that holds the picture above says the weekly was first published at Madras in April 1907. Wikipedia says that by April 1907 Bharati was editing India. The two may be describing different things: the first issue and the time he took charge. The first issue itself was not seen.</aside>

Who was the editor? Bharati joined in September 1906 and became its editor. But another man's name, M. Srinivasa Iyengar, was printed as the editor in that period. So the owner, the editor whose name was printed, and the poet who wrote were not always the same person. Some say Bharati "launched" India. Others say Thirumalachariyar owned it. Both agree that Bharati was at its centre.

## The cartoon and the red paper

Each issue had a political cartoon on the first page and an explanation on the second. India was the first magazine to use cartoons for political propaganda, says one account. Another says it was the first Tamil paper in Tamil Nadu to carry political cartoons.

<aside class="note"><b>Propaganda</b>: material meant to spread an idea or win people to a cause.</aside>

Bharati printed the weekly on red paper, says one source. No other source mentions this, so it is told here as that account and nothing more.

The paper had a motto. It was the three slogans of the French Revolution: Liberty, Equality and Fraternity. In the picture at the top of this page you can see the French words above the title.

## Trouble in Madras

The writing was bold, and the government noticed. The authorities in Chennai effectively forced the magazine out. It stopped publishing in Madras in late September 1908.

The proprietor of India was arrested in 1908 and Bharati himself was facing arrest. A warrant was issued for the editor, and one for Thirumalachari too, but he was not arrested. M. Srinivasa Iyengar, the editor whose name was printed, was sentenced to five years in prison.

So Bharati and Thirumalachari went to Pondicherry. Pondicherry was a French territory then.

## The paper crosses the border

The first Pondicherry issue came out on 20 October 1908. The press stood at 71 Ambalatharu Street. It could be set up only after a French Indian, Villianur S. Lakshmi Narayana Iyer, stood bail.

The British did not let it rest. India and Vijaya, another paper Bharati worked on, were banned in British India in 1909. The Pondicherry edition was barred from entering British India. It ceased publishing on 12 March 1910.

One popular website says that copies were smuggled into Madras hidden under hay in bullock carts. It gives no source, so it is not used as a fact here.

## Other papers, other friends

India was not his only work. By April 1907 he was also editing an English paper, Bala Bharatham, together with M. P. T. Acharya. In 1908 he published a book of poems, Sudesa Geethangal. Its poems urged people to join the freedom struggle.

He also moved among the leaders of the day. He discussed national questions with Bipin Chandra Pal, B. G. Tilak and V. V. S. Iyer. V. O. Chidambaram Pillai, who started an Indian shipping company in 1906, counted Bharati among his political allies. Bharati appeared in court for questioning in the case against Chidambaram. What he said there is not on record in the pages read.

## What is not known

Nobody has written down how many people read India, or what it cost. A full run of the paper was not seen for this story. What any particular cartoon showed is also not known.

## Why it matters

A newspaper is only paper and ink. But the people who made India wrote and drew things that made a government nervous. Bharati was a poet, and he was also a working journalist. He translated, edited and argued, week after week.

The paper's history is also a small map of how the government dealt with the press in those years: a warrant, a prison term, a ban at the border.
