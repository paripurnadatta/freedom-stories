There is a small house on Eswaran Dharmaraja Koil Street in Puducherry. It is door number 20. If you visit it, you will find photographs, letters, handwritten pages and old newspapers. The Government of Puducherry acquired it in 1972 and opened it to the public that December. Since 1984 it has been a memorial museum and research centre.

The man who lived in it for ten years was a poet on the run.

## Why Pondicherry?

Pondicherry was then a French territory, outside British India. The PIB says that Bharati went there in 1908. A warrant had been issued for the editor of India, so he went to Pondicherry, "a French territory at that time", and went on publishing India from there.

<aside class="note"><b>Warrant</b>: an order from a court or an official to arrest a person.</aside>

How he got there is a puzzle. One popular website says some people think he went by boat, but it calls the true method "a mystery". We do not use it.

In Pondicherry he edited *India*, a Tamil daily called *Vijaya*, an English monthly named *Bala Bharatha*, and a weekly called *Suryothayam*. He also started a Bala Bharatha Sangam.

The PIB and Wikipedia say he spent about ten years in Pondicherry. The Puducherry museum says 1908 to 1918. A speech by the Vice-President of India in 2021 says "more than 11 years". We use the museum's dates.

## Pressure from outside

The British could not arrest him in Pondicherry. But they could still make his work hard. TamilNation says that the British stopped remittances and letters going to the papers. India and Vijaya were banned in British India in 1909.

## A friend arrives

In Pondicherry he met other people who were wanted by the British: Aurobindo, Lajpat Rai and V. V. S. Aiyar. Aurobindo arrived in Pondicherry in 1910. Bharati then helped with Aurobindo's journals, *Arya* and *Karma Yogi*.

In November 1910 Bharati brought out a book of poems.

The museum has a list of the papers and books from this time: India, Vijaya, Karmayogi and Chakravartini. It also keeps new editions of older works that he printed from 1908 on.

## The great years of writing

The ten years were short of money, but they were rich in work. TamilNation says that in 1912 he published his Tamil commentary on the Bhagavad Gita. In the same year he published three works: Kannan Paatu, Kuyil Paatu and Panjali Sabatham.

*Kannan Pattu* has twenty-three lyrics on Krishna. *Kuyil Pattu* is a narrative poem of 750 lines, a fable about a koel, a monkey and a bull. He also translated the Vedic hymns, the Yoga Sutra and the Bhagavad Gita into Tamil.

The stories inside the poems are left for other pages.

## Songs and temples

Other writers give small pictures of his days. Madras Musings says that he often visited the Manakkula Vinayaka temple in Pondicherry, and that Ganesa was a favourite god. It says he admired the poetry of the Alvars, the Tamil saints who sang for Vishnu, and that his Krishna songs were inspired by Nammalvar. In his poems, the article says, he pictured Krishna as a servant, which it calls a striking twist.

Another writer, Sujata Nahar, lists two books of his poems from these years. She gives Swadesa Gitangal (1908) and Janma Bhoomi (1909). She adds that his fiery poems appeared in the weekly India.

## Ideas he kept

The sources do not tell us which ideas came in which year. But they agree on what he stood for. The PIB says he opposed caste distinctions and said there were only two castes, men and women. It says he took off his own sacred thread and gave sacred threads to some Dalits. It says he wanted temples opened to Dalits and spoke against child marriage and dowry.

On caste he is quoted as saying: "inter-dining and inter-marrying are the only true solutions to caste inequality." A modern writer says that "Bharati's commitment to women's freedom was without equal." These are the words of a writer and the praise of a modern writer. They are not records of what he did on a certain day.

## Hard years

In Pondicherry he "suffered intense poverty". No numbers are given. During his exile his wife, Chellamma, took the children to her parents' house and gave him an ultimatum to return to British India.

That is one article's account of what happened inside a family, and little more is known.

## The house today

The museum page says the house was "a centre of deliberations aiming at attaining the freedom to the country". It says the government plans to expand it to its original form. This tells us that the building we see today is not exactly the one he lived in.

The museum lists what it shows: photographs of his birthplace in Ettayapuram, of the Puducherry house, of his father-in-law's house in Kadayam, and of the home in Triplicane where he died. It also has a family tree, handwritten letters, manuscripts and books.

It has a letter written to Tilak about the political situation in the South. We have not seen the letter itself.

## The end of exile

In November 1918, when the First World War was over, Bharati left Pondicherry for British India. The PIB says he entered near Cuddalore and was arrested at once. The ten years of exile were over, and the question of what came next was now in the hands of the police. That story is in "The petition from Cuddalore".

## Why it matters

Exile is easy to describe as an adventure. Bharati's exile was ten years of poverty, printing and writing. Several of his best-known works were published in those years.

His wife and daughters lived those years too. A story about a poet should not forget that.
