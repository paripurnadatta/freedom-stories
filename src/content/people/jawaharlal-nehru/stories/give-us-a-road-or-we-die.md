Imagine a place where you cannot get a cart, a car or a bus. A trip of twenty miles takes a hard day. If you fall seriously ill, there is no way to carry you to a hospital.

That was Garhwal in 1938, as Jawaharlal Nehru saw it.

## Five days in the hills

Nehru wrote about the trip in an essay called "A Visit to Garhwal". It is dated 10 May 1938, in Allahabad.

He said he had visited every district of the United Provinces, but not Garhwal. He had spent only a few hours there a year and a half earlier. So he took five days now.

His sister, Vijaya Lakshmi, came with him. So did Raja Hutheesing and some comrades from Garhwal. They visited Gochar, Devaprayag, Srinagar, Pauri and many villages.

<aside class="note"><b>Garhwal</b>: a hill region in the Himalayas. Srinagar here is the old Garhwal town. It is not the Srinagar of Kashmir.</aside>

They travelled "by aeroplane, by car, on horseback, and on foot". Most of it was on horseback, since there were no cart roads.

## Five hours, not five weeks

The aeroplane took them all the way to Badrinath and Kedarnath. They could not land there. They came back to Gochar, where they landed. Nehru says they did by air in five hours a journey that takes as many weeks on foot.

From the air, he saw bare mountains and many valleys with rivers winding through them. He wrote about the Ganga there. He had known it in "its rich and stately maturity". Now he saw it in "its eager and joyful youth and its bubbling and gurgling childhood."

## Where two rivers meet

On the ground, they followed the Ganga from Rishikesh to Devaprayag. At Devaprayag, the Bhagirathi meets the Alaknanda. After that they lose their separate names and become the Ganga.

Nehru described the little town perched on cliffs above the two rivers. The water rushes through gorges "to meet and intermingle".

Then they rode along the Alaknanda on horseback. Pilgrims on their way to Badrinath were walking. The path was good, but sometimes it narrowed, and there was a steep drop to the river.

People cheered and threw flowers. That was not welcome this time. It made the horses shy, and the drop was frightening.

The sun was hot and there was not much shade. But wild jasmine lined the way, and "the smell of jasmine enveloped us."

At Srinagar they stayed two days and took part in a conference. At Pauri, on a hilltop, they looked at the snow peaks: Badrinath, Kedarnath, Chaukhamba, Trishul and even Nanda Devi. In the villages, people came to greet them. Men, women and children came.

## "Give us a road"

What stayed with Nehru was not only the view. It was the isolation and poverty. He wrote that they were so near the rest of the world and "yet how cut off from it."

He said that everywhere "all sorts of people demanded and begged and shouted for a road." He wrote that "swaraj itself took the semblance of a road leading from the heart of the mountains to the plains below."

Their words were, in his report: "Give us a road or we die."

<aside class="note"><b>Swaraj</b>: self-rule, a word the freedom movement used for freedom.</aside>

## The children

Nehru liked to ask school children questions. He found that more than ninety out of a hundred had never seen a railway train. They had not seen a motor car, or even a carriage.

But they had seen an aeroplane flying over them. Nehru notes that his own party had flown across only some days earlier.

## What people wanted

Besides the road, he heard complaints. There was a lack of water for the fields. The tax was heavy. Medical help was scarce, and schools were few.

He noted that the demand for education was strong, "even for girls". And he saw many terraced fields lying empty. People had cut them out of the hillsides with great labour. Now no one farmed them.

## Why no road?

Nehru asked why the road had not come. He said that a railway had been promised during the World War, and a survey was made. But neither the railway nor a road came.

Then he gave his own guess. He wrote, "I imagine this objection was based on the desire on the part of the Government to keep Garhwal isolated". He thought the government wanted to keep the area free of political ideas, because it was an area that supplied soldiers.

It is his opinion. He says "I imagine". We have not read any reply from the government of the time.

## His list of ideas

Nehru did not only complain. He proposed things. There should be a road. There should be an inquiry into using the rivers to make electricity. There should be another into the minerals. Cheap pumps could lift river water to the fields. Forests should be planted. Wool-spinning and weaving, and better bee-keeping, could help.

He also wrote something harsh. He said he found "a certain lack of enterprise" among the people, who seemed resigned. But he said this came from "long and distressing poverty" and that it "will pass". He called the Garhwalis "a brave and sturdy people."

## The soldiers of 1930

At the end, he remembered something else. Eight years before, he wrote, Garhwali soldiers had "performed a gallant deed" in the North-West Frontier Province. A footnote explains. In 1930, a party of the Garhwali Rifles refused to fire on Congress volunteers. They said a soldier's duty was to defend the country against a foreign invader.

The story of the Garhwalis is told in few details. Two platoons of the Royal Garhwal Rifles refused to board buses into Peshawar. Chandra Singh Garhwali led one of them. The soldiers' non-commissioned officers were sentenced to up to eight years in prison. How those sentences were given is not told.

## Why it matters

The essay shows Nehru looking at a place most people did not visit. He saw that freedom needs roads, schools and water too.

It also shows people asking for what they needed. One word, "road", stood for a hope. A road was a way out of the mountains.
