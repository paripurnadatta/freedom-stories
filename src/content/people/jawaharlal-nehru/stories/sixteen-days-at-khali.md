Have you ever been so tired of everything that you wanted to go far away and hide?

In 1938, Jawaharlal Nehru felt this. He did something about it. He wrote an essay about the trip, and we can read it today.

## A city of bamboo

The essay is called "Escape". It starts at Haripura, a village on the banks of the river Tapti. The Indian National Congress held its yearly meeting there in 1938. Nehru's own note says Haripura was "till then unknown to the outside world."

In February 1938 Nehru did not stand for re-election as Congress president. Subhas Chandra Bose followed him.

For the meeting, a whole city of bamboo had been built. Nehru wrote that it was full of "an animated, jostling crowd" a day or two earlier. Then everyone left. Nehru walked to the river bank in the dark. He felt sad that the city would soon disappear. In his words, "Only the memory would endure."

## He wants to run away

Then another feeling came over him. He had wanted for a long time to go to some far-off place.

He tells us it was not that his body was tired. It was "a weariness of the mind." He wrote that political life was "an exhausting business" and that he "had had enough of it for a while."

While he spoke to friends and answered questions, his mind was somewhere else. It was in the mountains of the north, with their deep valleys, snowy peaks and slopes of pine trees and deodars.

<aside class="note"><b>Deodar</b>: a tall Himalayan cedar tree, very common in the hills of north India.</aside>

## Trouble at home

He went to Allahabad first. There he found "some trouble was brewing." It was between communities. Nehru was cross with himself. Was he going to be stopped from going to the mountains because of "fools and bigots"?

He then told himself it was not serious. He wrote that he "argued with and deluded myself". He also called his own choice cowardice: "Like a coward I crept away when my work lay in Allahabad."

We see here that Nehru was honest about his own faults. He did not say he was right to go.

## Up the hills

He climbed the winding road to Almora, in the Kumaun hills. From there he went higher, to a place called Khali. The last part of the journey was on hill ponies.

Nehru had wanted to go to Khali for two years. When he got there, the sun was setting and the hillsides were glowing. He looked for the great peak of Nanda Devi, but light clouds hid it.

Over the days, he enjoyed the mountains. He wrote about the early spring. Red rhododendron flowers made patches on the hillsides. Fruit trees were in bloom. Tiny new leaves were about to open on the bare trees.

In the early morning, he lay in the open air. The cold wind from the snows made him shiver, and then the sun warmed him.

Sometimes he lay under pine trees. He wrote that the wind seemed to whisper to him. It said that the world below was full of strife and bigotry. It asked him if it was worth going back to such people.

<aside class="note"><b>Bigotry</b>: a strong dislike of people because of their religion or beliefs.</aside>

## Binsar

One day he went to Binsar. It was four miles from Khali, and fifteen hundred feet higher.

From there, he saw a stretch of snowy mountains. He wrote that it was six hundred miles long, from the mountains of Tibet to those of Nepal. Nanda Devi stood in the middle. He named Badrinath and Kedarnath among the places in view, and said Kailas and Mansarovar lay just beyond them.

He was "spell-bound" and "awe-stricken". He was also a little cross with himself for having missed this beauty "in a corner of my own province" for so many years.

## Letters and a shock

Even in the hills, the post came. Sometimes a big bundle of letters and newspapers arrived. The post office was ten miles away. Nehru thought of leaving the mail there. But his old habit was too strong. And a letter from "some dear one far away" might be inside.

Then came news that shook him. Hitler was marching into Austria. Nehru wrote that he could "hear the tramp of barbarian feet" over the gardens of Vienna. He wondered whether this was the start of a world war.

Another piece of news came the same days. There had been communal riots in Allahabad. He wrote that heads were broken and a few people were killed. He asked what this "disgusting madness and folly" was that kept dragging his people down.

<aside class="note"><b>Communal riots</b>: fights between groups of people of different religions.</aside>

## No escape

Nehru wrote: "There was no peace for me then even in Khali, no escape."

For a few days he stayed on. Slowly some peace returned, as he looked at the white mountains, "calm and inscrutable and untouched by human folly." They would be there whatever people did. The spring would still come to the hills.

But he knew he had to go back. He wrote: "There was no escape except to some extent in action."

He left Khali "sixteen days after I had come there." The essay is dated 7 April 1938.

## What we do not know

We have only Nehru's own words, in a book printed in 1942. We do not know the exact dates of the trip or whom he travelled with. He does not say. We also do not know how the riots in Allahabad were settled. His essay does not tell us.

We should also be careful about one thing. The essay was written for readers. It is how Nehru chose to describe his holiday.

## Why it matters

Nehru is usually pictured making speeches or sitting in prison. This essay shows another side. He was a man who loved the mountains and was tired like anyone else.

It also shows a choice. He could have stayed. He chose to go back to a world of riots, war and politics.
