What would you say if you were on trial, and you thought the court had no right to judge you?

Jawaharlal Nehru faced this in November 1940. He was in a prison in Gorakhpur. He gave a statement to the court. It was printed in a book soon after. We can read it today.

## Why he was there

It was the second year of the Second World War. The British government had brought India into the war. The Congress had said this was done without asking Indians.

Wikipedia says the Viceroy, Lord Linlithgow, declared India at war in September 1939 without consulting Indians. In October 1939 the Congress asked its provincial ministries to resign in protest.

In 1940, the Congress started a limited form of protest. It was called individual satyagraha.

<aside class="note"><b>Satyagraha</b>: Gandhi's way of peaceful, firm protest. <b>Individual satyagraha</b>: here, a few chosen people, one at a time, spoke out against the war effort.</aside>

Nehru explained how it worked. Gandhi wanted the satyagraha to be "confined to particular persons of his choice". Vinoba Bhave was chosen first. Nehru was chosen to follow. He was to start on 7 November in the district of Allahabad.

The Nehru Memorial Fund says he was arrested on 31 October 1940. The reason was speeches he made in Gorakhpur district. So the plan changed.

## Bad notes

The charge was based on police reports of three speeches. Nehru did not like them. He said the reports were "scrappy and incomplete, confusing". He said they made "little sense".

He wrote: "I am a lover of words and phrases and try to use them appropriately."

But he was fair. He said he did not think the mistakes were deliberate. And he did not want to list all the errors. That "would mean rewriting them completely."

He said he did not know exactly what the charge was. He thought it had to do with the Defence of India Rules and with what he said about the war. If so, he said, "I shall gladly admit the charge."

<aside class="note"><b>Defence of India Rules</b>: wartime rules that gave the government wide powers. They limited what people could say or print.</aside>

## What he said about the war

Most of the statement was about why the Congress would not help with the war. He said no one had asked the Indian people. He wrote that "the hundreds of millions of India" were pushed into "a mighty war which was none of their seeking."

He pointed out a puzzle. The war was said to be fought for freedom and democracy. But he asked why it was fought in the name of those ideas when Indians were not free.

He also said he had spoken against Fascism and Nazism for years. He listed countries that, he said, had been given up to aggressors: Manchuria, Abyssinia, Czechoslovakia, Spain, China and Albania. He said he had seen "how the lamps of liberty were being put out."

He said that the British government had chosen not to give up its empire. And he said that in India, over a year of war government, elected assemblies had been "suspended and ignored." He also said the press had been silenced.

<aside class="note"><b>Fascism and Nazism</b>: harsh governments led by one leader. They ruled Italy and Germany at the time.</aside>

## Fair to the British people

Nehru made a point of saying something different about people in Britain. He wrote that he was "convinced that the large majority of people in England are weary of Empire". His quarrel, he said, was with their government.

This is useful to remember. He was not against the British people. He said his case was against an empire.

## Two views

We have Nehru's words only. The British authorities saw it another way. They charged him under wartime rules. The statement itself is an argument, not a neutral account. We did not open any record of what the magistrate or the government said in reply. So we cannot say how they saw the case.

## "I too am a symbol"

The best-known part is near the end. He said: "I stand before you, sir, as an individual being tried for certain offences against the State. You are a symbol of that State."

Then he said: "But I am something more than an individual also; I, too, am a symbol at the present moment, a symbol of Indian nationalism."

He said that he and Vinoba Bhave "were symbols who spoke the mind of India."

And he said it was not him that was being judged. In his words, it was "the hundreds of millions of the people of India". He added that perhaps "it is the British Empire itself that is on its trial before the bar of the world."

## Counting his trials

He also said something about himself. "Seven times I have been tried and convicted by British authority in India, and many years of my life lie buried within prison walls."

He added that "an eighth time or a ninth, and a few more years, make little difference."

His count is seven. Wikipedia says eight detentions between 1921 and 1945. The Memorial Fund's dates show ten spells, as we show in another story. People count these in different ways. Some stays were split, and a trial is not the same as an arrest.

## The last lines

He ended with a thought about the place. "I am happy to be tried in Gorakhpur. The peasantry of Gorakhpur are the poorest and the most long-suffering in my Province."

He said he was glad that his visit to Gorakhpur, and his attempt to serve its people, had led to the trial.

## What happened next

The book's editor adds a note. On 4 November 1940, Nehru was sentenced to three terms of "rigorous imprisonment", one after the other. Each was sixteen months. That made four years.

He was sent to Dehra Dun jail. For some time, the note says, he was kept in the old "gallow cell". That cell was no longer used afterwards.

He was released on 4 December 1941. The note says the Government of India freed about six or seven thousand prisoners who had been jailed for what it called "Symbolic Civil Disobedience". Wikipedia adds that he came out three days before Pearl Harbor.

So he served about a year of a four-year sentence.

<aside class="note"><b>Rigorous imprisonment</b>: a prison sentence that includes hard labour.</aside>

## Why it matters

Nehru turned a courtroom into a place to speak. He did not argue about the facts of the speeches. He argued about who had the right to decide India's part in a war.

You do not have to agree with all of it. It is worth knowing how he saw the trial: as a question about a country and not only about himself.
