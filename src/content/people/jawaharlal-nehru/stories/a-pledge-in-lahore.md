What does it feel like to ask a whole country to celebrate something that has not happened yet?

That is what the Congress did on 26 January 1930. India was still ruled by Britain. But it was asked to celebrate the goal of independence.

## The president

In 1929, the Congress chose Jawaharlal Nehru as president for its session at Lahore. The Prime Minister's Office says so. The Nehru Memorial Fund says he presided on 29 December.

On that day, he gave a long speech. The Selected Works of Jawaharlal Nehru prints it.

## What he asked for

Nehru said what independence meant to him: "Independence for us means complete freedom from British domination and British imperialism."

He asked the Congress to "declare in favour of independence and devise sanctions to achieve it."

<aside class="note"><b>Sanctions</b>: here, steps a movement can take to make its demand count.</aside>

He also spoke about the country's poor. He said, "It is the peasantry that cry loudly and piteously for relief." And he said plainly, "I must frankly confess that I am a socialist and a republican."

<aside class="note"><b>Socialist</b>: a person who believes wealth should be shared more fairly. <b>Republican</b>: a person who wants a country with no king and an elected head.</aside>

## Violence and slavery

He spoke about non-violence. His words were: "Violence is bad, but slavery is far worse."

He also wrote that the "great apostle of nonviolence" had said it was better to fight than to refuse to fight out of cowardice. The speech's text does not name Gandhi in the lines we read.

We did not open any page that gives the Congress's vote on the day. The speech asks the Congress to choose independence. It does not tell us what the Congress decided. We know from other sources that independence became its goal, and the 6 January 1930 statement below says so.

## The flag

On the same day, Nehru spoke as the flag was hoisted. The Selected Works prints a short speech from *The Tribune* of 31 December 1929.

He said the flag was "a symbol of India's unity". Then he said something about who it belonged to: "The flag under which you stand today and which you have just now saluted does not belong to any community. It is the flag of the country."

And: "All those who stand today under this flag are Indians, not Hindus, not Muslims, but Indians."

## A day to celebrate

On 6 January 1930, Nehru issued a statement from Allahabad. The Congress Working Committee had fixed Sunday, 26 January, as a day for a national celebration. It was to mark the Congress's adoption of complete independence as its immediate aim.

The statement says the message of independence should reach "all the towns and villages of India".

It also set a plan for the day. At 8 in the morning, the flag was to be hoisted. Later, there would be processions. At 5 in the afternoon, there would be public meetings held under the national flag.

## Do not mix up the dates

Three different dates are often mixed up. Here they are in order.

- **29 December 1929.** Nehru's speech and the flag at Lahore.
- **26 January 1930.** A day of celebration across India. India was not yet independent.
- **15 August 1947.** The day India became free.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The Prime Minister's Office page says that at "the Madras Congress" in 1926, Nehru helped to commit the Congress to independence. But Nehru's own letters and speeches from 1928 refer to the Madras Congress of December 1927. We think the 1926 date is a mistake, but we have not found the Congress's own records to be sure.</aside>

## Why it matters

A celebration can be a kind of promise. Millions of people were asked to say aloud that India should be free. They were asked to do it before it was true.

Nehru's words on the flag are still worth hearing: it belongs to the country, and not to any one group.
