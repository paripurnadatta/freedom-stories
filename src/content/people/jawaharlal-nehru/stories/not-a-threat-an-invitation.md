Is a demand a threat? Or can it be an offer?

In August 1942, Jawaharlal Nehru argued that the Congress's demand to the British was an offer. Many newspapers abroad said it was something else.

## The setting

It was the Second World War. Japan was fighting against Britain, and people in India feared a Japanese invasion. The Congress asked for India's freedom at once. This is known as the Quit India resolution.

<aside class="note"><b>Quit India</b>: a demand that British rule end at once. It is told in other stories on this site.</aside>

On 7 August 1942, Nehru spoke at a meeting of the All India Congress Committee in Bombay. He moved the resolution. The Nehru Memorial Fund's chronology says he did so on 7 August.

## What the critics said

The Selected Works footnotes show who was against the Congress.

- The *Washington Post* of 4 August 1942 wrote that Gandhi was "continuing consistently to play the game of a political blackmailer."
- The *New York Times* and the *Sun* also criticised the Congress stand.
- Harold Laski, a British writer, said on 13 July 1942 that Indians should raise an army against Japan. He said independence would follow the war.
- On 5 August, the British minister Sir Stafford Cripps said: "we make no threats but we must assert unequivocally our duty to India".

Nehru answered these in his speech. He said: "We are accused, by some newspapers, that we are blackmailing."

And he said: "Whatever may happen in Whitehall, it is not going to stop us from working for our independence."

<aside class="note"><b>Whitehall</b>: the street in London where the British government has its offices. People use the name for the government.</aside>

## The danger from Japan

Nehru spoke plainly about the war. He said: "If there is internal trouble or an external invasion by Japan, it is we who would suffer."

The next day, he added: "if the Japanese reach this country, you and I will suffer and not the people in London and Washington."

He also said the Allied cause was right only in part: "the Allied cause is only negatively right in the sense that Germany and Japan are worse." But he added that "Indian freedom would change the whole nature of the War and make it right positively."

## The invitation

On 8 August, Nehru spoke again. He said the resolution was not a challenge. His words were: "It is not a threat. It is an invitation."

He explained: "It is an offer of cooperation but of a free India with other free peoples."

At the same time, he did not offer to give up the goal. He said: "We are prepared to pay any price for unity except the price of independence."

He also said the decision was final: "We have decided to take this course on which there is no going back".

He asked for calm: "we shall try to remain calm."

And he said that the Congress might not be allowed to carry on: "Whether we function as Indian National Congress or not, time may come when each individual will represent the Indian National Congress."

## Two views

The critics wrote that the Congress was blackmailing the Allies in a time of war. Nehru said that a free India would change the nature of the war. We have only these speeches and footnotes. We did not open pages on what happened next, so this story stops here.

## The next day

The chronology says Nehru was arrested on 9 August 1942. It also says he was released on 15 June 1945.

## Why it matters

Nehru's speech shows how a leader answers a charge. He did not say he was above the war. He said India would suffer most if it were lost.

He also showed that "free" could mean "working with others". For him, independence and cooperation were not opposites.
