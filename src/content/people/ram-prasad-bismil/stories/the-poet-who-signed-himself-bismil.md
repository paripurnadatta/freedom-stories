In December 1927, a prisoner in Gorakhpur jail was writing as fast as he could. He was writing the story of his own life. He knew he did not have long. In a few days, he was going to be hanged.

His name was Ram Prasad. But on his poems he signed another name: Bismil.

## A boy in Shahjahanpur

Ram Prasad was born on 11 June 1897, in Shahjahanpur, a town in what is now Uttar Pradesh. His father was Muralidhar and his mother was Moolmati.

His father taught him Hindi at home. For Urdu, he was sent to a maulvi, a Muslim teacher. Later he joined an English school, even though his father did not like the idea. So by the time he was a teenager, he could read in three languages.

<aside class="note"><b>Maulvi</b>: a Muslim scholar and teacher. In those days, many children in north India learned Urdu from one.</aside>

He was not always a good student. One account says he fell in with bad company and read cheap novels instead of studying.

Then he found the Arya Samaj, a Hindu reform movement. He began going to its temple in Shahjahanpur every day. There he met a holy man named Swami Somdev, who taught him about religion, and also about politics.

<aside class="note"><b>Pen name</b>: a name a writer uses instead of their real one. Ram Prasad used three: Ram, Agyat ("unknown") and Bismil. Bismil means "wounded".</aside>

## A vow

When Ram Prasad was about 18, he read some news that made him furious. Bhai Parmanand, a friend of Swami Somdev, had been sentenced to death by the British.

Ram Prasad wrote a poem in Hindi called Mera Janm, "My Birth". In it he promised to fight British rule. He also made a vow to take revenge.

According to the book Hanged for Patriotism, Swami Somdev gave him a warning: "It is easy to take a vow but hard to keep it."

## Books, leaflets and a river

Ram Prasad kept his vow. With Swami Somdev's help, he published a Hindi book about how America won its freedom from Britain. The government banned it.

He started a secret group called Matrivedi. He joined hands with a schoolteacher named Genda Lal Dixit, who had his own group. Together they gathered young men from towns like Etawah, Mainpuri, Agra and Shahjahanpur.

On 28 January 1918, Ram Prasad printed a leaflet called "A Message to Countrymen", with his poem "The Vow of Mainpuri". To raise money for the group, they also carried out robberies.

The police hunted them. One later account says that during a gunfight, Ram Prasad jumped into the river Yamuna and swam away underwater. The police thought he had drowned.

<aside class="note"><b>Is it true?</b> The river escape comes from later retellings. No police report of it has been found, so we cannot be sure exactly how it happened.</aside>

The case became known as the Mainpuri Conspiracy. In November 1919, a magistrate declared Ram Prasad an absconder: a wanted man who could not be found. He hid for a long time. In February 1920, after the prisoners in the case were set free, he came home to Shahjahanpur.

## Writer and organiser

Ram Prasad went on writing. He wrote poems and translated books from Bengali and English into Hindi.

He also went on planning. In 1923, in Allahabad, he helped write the rules for a new secret party, together with Sachindra Nath Sanyal and others. In October 1924, at a meeting in Cawnpore, the party was named the Hindustan Republican Association, or HRA. Ram Prasad was put in charge of its weapons.

He also found a close friend in a younger poet from Shahjahanpur, Ashfaqulla Khan. One was a Hindu who prayed at the Arya Samaj, the other a Muslim. Their friendship lasted until their deaths.

## Kakori

The HRA needed money. On 9 August 1925, near Kakori, about eight miles from Lucknow, Ram Prasad and his companions stopped a train by pulling the alarm cord. They broke open the government's safe in the guard's van and took the money.

Something went wrong. A passenger, a lawyer named Ahmad Ali, stepped out of the train. He was shot dead.

<aside class="note"><b>Who fired?</b> The court history says only that Ahmad Ali was shot after leaving the carriage. Wikipedia says the shot was fired by accident by Manmathnath Gupta.</aside>

The police arrested about forty people. The trial began in May 1926. On 6 April 1927, the judge sentenced Ram Prasad, Rajendra Lahiri and Roshan Singh to death. Ashfaqulla, tried separately, was sentenced to death too.

Appeals were made, even to London. They failed.

## The last pages

In his cell in Gorakhpur jail, Ram Prasad wrote his autobiography. The book Hanged for Patriotism says he wrote the last pages on 17 December 1927, and that they were smuggled out of the jail.

On 18 December, his mother came to see him. A later retelling says she told him she was proud of him, and he asked for her blessing. Nobody wrote down their exact words at the time.

<aside class="note"><b>Remembered, not recorded</b>: what Bismil and his mother said comes from books written many years later.</aside>

On the morning of 19 December 1927, Ram Prasad Bismil was hanged in Gorakhpur jail. He was 30 years old. Ashfaqulla Khan was hanged the same day, in Faizabad. Roshan Singh was hanged at Naini jail in Allahabad. Rajendra Lahiri had been hanged two days earlier, in Gonda.

One book says he walked to the gallows calling out "Vande Mataram" and "Bharat Mata ki Jai".

<aside class="note"><b>Last words</b>: different accounts give different last words. All of them were written down later. No record from that morning has been found.</aside>

His body was cremated beside the river Rapti.

## What he left behind

His autobiography was printed after his death. The British police made their own rough English translation, for officers to read in secret.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia says the editor Ganesh Shankar Vidyarthi printed it in 1928. Hanged for Patriotism says it came out in 1929 and was banned at once.</aside>

Today, a railway station near Shahjahanpur carries his name. So does a memorial in his home town.

## Why it matters

Ram Prasad Bismil is remembered as a revolutionary. But he was also a reader, a translator and a poet. He wanted India to be free, and he believed in fighting for it with weapons.

Not everyone in the freedom struggle agreed with that way. And on that August evening at Kakori, an ordinary traveller lost his life. Both things are part of his story.

Many people today call the famous song Sarfaroshi ki Tamanna "Bismil's song". As another story here explains, it was written by a different poet who used the same pen name. Ram Prasad Bismil wrote plenty of poems of his own. The last thing he wrote was his own story.
