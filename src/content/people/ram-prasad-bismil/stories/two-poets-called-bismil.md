"Sarfaroshi ki tamanna ab hamare dil mein hai..."

You may have heard these words sung. They mean, roughly: "The wish to give up our lives is now in our hearts."

Ask people who wrote them, and most will say: Ram Prasad Bismil, the revolutionary who was hanged in 1927.

But there is a puzzle. There were two poets who called themselves Bismil.

## Two Bismils

The first is the one you may know. Ram Prasad was born in Shahjahanpur in 1897. Bismil was one of the pen names he used for his poems in Hindi and Urdu. He was a revolutionary, and he was hanged in Gorakhpur jail on 19 December 1927.

<aside class="note"><b>Pen name</b>: a name a writer uses instead of their real one. Bismil means "wounded".</aside>

The second was born in 1901 in Azimabad. That is the old name of Patna, in Bihar. He was a poet too, and he is known as Bismil Azimabadi: Bismil from Azimabad. He lived a long life and died in Patna in 1978.

## Who wrote the song?

Bismil Azimabadi wrote *Sarfaroshi ki Tamanna* in 1921. The poem first appeared in a journal called *Sabah*, printed in Delhi.

Rekhta, a large online library of Urdu poetry, prints the poem under Bismil Azimabadi's name. It says it comes from his book Hikayat-e-Hasti. It also says that scholars have shown the poem is his.

In 2022, a magazine published by the Government of India, Yojana, carried an article by Professor Chaman Lal. He is an adviser to the Bhagat Singh Archives and Resource Centre. He wrote that the poem, "long ascribed to Ram Prasad Bismil", was written by "another poet, namely, Bismil Azimabadi".

<aside class="note"><b>Ascribed</b>: said to be by someone.</aside>

## Even the government disagreed with itself

Here is the strange part. In June 2021, the government's own press office put out a note about Ram Prasad Bismil. It said he wrote Sarfaroshi ki Tamanna while he was in jail.

One year later, the government's Yojana magazine said the opposite.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: a 2021 government press note says Ram Prasad Bismil wrote the poem. Wikipedia, Rekhta and a 2022 government magazine article say Bismil Azimabadi wrote it. Rekhta says scholars have now settled the question in favour of Bismil Azimabadi.</aside>

## How did the mix-up happen?

Partly, it is the shared name. A poem signed "Bismil" could belong to either man.

But there is more. The revolutionaries loved this poem. Rekhta says that the opening lines were on Ram Prasad Bismil's lips when he was taken to be hanged. Wikipedia says the revolutionary "immortalised" it, turning it into a war cry.

So the poem became his in people's minds, even though it was not his own.

## Why it matters

History is full of small mistakes that get repeated until everyone believes them. Checking who really wrote something is part of a historian's job.

Ram Prasad Bismil does not need a borrowed poem. He wrote many of his own. And a poet from Patna, who is much less famous, deserves to have his name next to his words.
