Imagine you have only a few weeks left to live. You are locked in a prison cell. You have no proper notebook, only scraps of paper. And the guards must not find out what you are writing.

What would you write?

In 1927, in Gorakhpur jail, Ram Prasad Bismil faced exactly that. He had been sentenced to death for the Kakori train robbery. And he decided to write the story of his life.

## A book in secret

Bismil wrote on loose scraps of paper. The pages were smuggled out of the jail. According to the book Hanged for Patriotism, he wrote the last pages on 17 December 1927. Two days later, he was hanged.

The book was printed only after his death. One of its Hindi titles is Nij Jiwan Ki Chhata. In 2025, almost a hundred years later, a new English translation by Awadhesh Tripathi came out, called A Glimpse of My Life.

<aside class="note"><b>Autobiography</b>: the story of a person's life, written by that person.</aside>

Here are some of the things Bismil chose to tell.

## Two hundred banned books

As a young man, Bismil had helped publish a Hindi book about how America won its freedom from Britain. The government of his province banned it and seized copies.

But Bismil's group still wanted to sell it. Their chance came at a big meeting of the Congress party in Delhi.

Bismil wrote that he was there in the uniform of an ambulance volunteer. He hid about two hundred copies of the banned book under his overcoat. Then he walked them out of an Arya Samaj camp, right past the police.

## Over the wall

He also wrote about a narrow escape in Shahjahanpur. One night the police raided, and the young men found a door locked against them. So they climbed over a wall.

On the other side, a police daroga stopped them. They told him they were students, and he let them go. Afterwards, they buried their guns and left for Prayag.

<aside class="note"><b>Daroga</b>: a police officer in charge of a police station.</aside>

## The comrade who fired at him

Not every story in the book is about brave tricks. Some are about trust going wrong.

During the Mainpuri years, some members of his group decided that one of their comrades was too weak. They planned to kill him. Bismil objected. "It is not right to kill another human being," he wrote.

Later, he was sitting on the bank of the Yamuna near Prayag. One of those same men fired a pistol at him, from about two yards away.

Bismil survived. He fled to Lucknow.

## His mother's promise

Bismil was furious. He wanted revenge on the man who had tried to kill him. He fell ill with fever.

His mother came to him. She asked him to make a promise: "Promise me that you will not kill the man who tried to murder you."

At first he did not want to. But in the end, he gave his word. He wrote that after he promised, his fever began to go down.

<aside class="note"><b>His mother</b>: her name was Moolmati. According to Hanged for Patriotism, she came to see him in Gorakhpur jail the day before he was hanged.</aside>

## A farmer in hiding

For a long time after that, Bismil lived in hiding. He worked in the fields like a farmer, and hardly anyone recognised him.

His family paid a heavy price. Because of the warrant for his arrest, his father sold the family house. It had been worth 2,000 rupees. He sold it for only 800. The money went for his sisters' weddings, and his parents were left almost penniless.

Even then, Bismil kept reading and writing. He had learned Bengali in memory of a friend who had died, Sushil Chandra Sen. He even named his younger brother Sushil after him. Now he translated Bengali books into Hindi, and printed them as a series.

He did some of his translating while out herding animals, which he called "rather amusing". The books lost him about 500 rupees.

When the government pardoned the political prisoners, he finally came home to Shahjahanpur. He wrote that he had been sure he would never walk freely in his town again.

## Ashfaq

The book also speaks of Ashfaqulla Khan, his friend from Shahjahanpur. Bismil was a Hindu, and Ashfaq a Muslim. A reviewer of the book, the historian Salil Misra, calls Ashfaq Bismil's "soulmate". He says the book comes back again and again to Hindu–Muslim unity, through that friendship.

Misra is honest about Bismil's other side too. He points out that Bismil also took part in an Arya Samaj campaign called Shuddhi, which Misra describes as generally hostile to Muslims. Real people can hold ideas that pull in different directions.

## A change of mind

The most surprising part of the book comes near the end.

Bismil had spent years in secret groups, planning to fight British rule with weapons. But in his cell, he looked back, and he began to have doubts.

He wrote that it might have been better "if we had directed our efforts towards educating the masses and spreading awareness among them". And he wrote: "I am now convinced that no revolutionary organisation can be successful in India."

<aside class="note"><b>The masses</b>: ordinary people, all over the country.</aside>

He left a message for the young people of India. "My final message to the youth is that they should choose to serve the nation sincerely," he wrote. He hoped villagers would become true patriots, wearing khadi and using swadeshi goods made in India.

<aside class="note"><b>One reader's view</b>: these lines are quoted from the review by Salil Misra. He reads them as a sign that Bismil was questioning violent methods. Other readers may weigh the book differently, so it is worth reading the whole of it.</aside>

## Why it matters

Bismil could have used his last weeks to write only about his bravest deeds. He did write about some of them. But he also wrote about his fears, his mistakes, his mother, his debts and his doubts.

That is what makes his book special. It is not the story of a statue. It is the story of a real young man, thinking hard about his life, with the gallows only days away.
