Kalpana Dutt first met Surya Sen on a dark, drizzly night in June 1931. She was waiting under a mosquito net in a house deep in the countryside. Two men came in and spoke quietly. She could not tell which one was the famous leader.

Later she wrote what she saw. He was a "smallish, short man, very reserved." Nobody would have guessed, she said, that this quiet man was the "King of Chittagong."

His first question was about her exams.

## A boy from Noapara

Surya Sen was born on 22 March 1894, in a village called Noapara, near Chittagong. His father, Rama Niranjan Sen, was a teacher.

He passed his entrance examination in 1912. By 1916 he was studying for a B.A. at Behrampore College, far away in Murshidabad. A teacher there, Shatishchandra Chakrabarti, spoke to him about the freedom movement. This teacher belonged to a secret group called Jugantar, says Banglapedia.

<aside class="note"><b>Jugantar</b>: a secret group in Bengal whose members believed in armed action against British rule.</aside>

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: the sources name the college in different ways. Wikipedia calls it Berhampore College in one place and Krishnath College in another. We use "Behrampore College", as Banglapedia does.</aside>

## The school

In 1918, Surya Sen came back to Chittagong. He taught at the National School there. It was at that school that he became known as "Master-da". Banglapedia writes it as "Mastarda", and it means "teacher".

He was the most popular master at the "Swadesh schools", also called national schools, the Daily Star says. During the Non-Cooperation Movement he also helped lead boycotts of government schools, colleges and courts.

<aside class="note"><b>Boycott</b>: to refuse to use or join something, as a way of protest.</aside>

Later he gave up teaching so that he could work full time for the movement. He became president of the Chittagong branch of the Indian National Congress. In 1929, Subhas Chandra Bose presided over a district Congress conference in Chittagong, and Surya Sen was chosen as its general secretary.

## Jail and a gym

From 1926 to 1928, Surya Sen was in jail. Ambika Chakrabarti was jailed with him. Police arrested the two men after a raid on the Assam-Bengal Railway treasury, says Banglapedia. Wikipedia gives the reason as "revolutionary activities", and its later text mentions the same railway raid.

After he came out, he set up a gymnasium for physical and weapons training. He also set up a branch of the Indian Republican Army.

## What he was like

Kalpana Dutt wrote about him from close up. He did not like slackness in studies. When he heard her exam had gone badly, she could tell from his tone that he minded.

He looked after his comrades very carefully. "My job is to save my cadre, not cash," he used to say. He did not let anyone go short of good food while hiding. He even arranged singing evenings in the hiding places. He believed that keeping cheerful was one of the biggest jobs of a revolutionary.

He wanted quality, not numbers. "It is not our aim to swell our numbers by indiscriminate recruiting," he said, as the Daily Star quotes him.

## The boy with the silver bangles

Before the raid, the group needed about 15,000 rupees. Surya Sen did not believe in political robbery, Kalpana Dutt writes. So the young members brought money from their own homes. Some brought two or three thousand rupees. Some brought family jewellery.

One boy came to him crying. His mother was very poor. He had tried everywhere. In the end he broke open his mother's safe at night, and took a pair of silver bangles worth two or three rupees.

Surya Sen was touched. He put the boy first on the list of those who would go into action.

<aside class="note"><b>A word of care</b>: this story comes only from Kalpana Dutt. She wrote it years later, and her book is a friend's memory, not a government record.</aside>

## Why it matters

When we think of the Chittagong raid, we think of guns and flags. But the man behind it was a teacher who cared about studies, food and cheerfulness. That does not tell us whether the raid was right. It does help us see who the people were.
