In the 1880s and 1890s, Indians who wanted change argued about which change should come first. Should they win freedom from British rule? Or should they first change customs inside their own society, like child marriage? Tilak and his friend Agarkar ended up on different sides.

Many readers today find this part of Tilak's life hard. We tell it with both views, as our sources do.

## Two cases

The first case was Rakhmabai's. She had been married as a child. Her husband went to court and asked that she be made to live with him. The first judge decided for her. The High Court overruled him. N. G. Jog, writing for the Government of India, dates this to 1886. Wikipedia says 1887.

Jog says Tilak welcomed the High Court's decision as upholding Hindu law and custom. He wrote two articles in *Kesari* against the reformer Ranade. Jog calls the articles "a tour de force", a show of skill, but says Tilak "seems to have overlooked the human issues involved." Wikipedia says Rukhmabai refused to go and later earned a medical degree in London.

The second case was a law. On 9 January 1891, a bill was moved in the Imperial Legislative Council. It would make some acts against a wife under twelve a crime. It would also let a bride cancel a child marriage when she grew up. The bill was passed.

Tilak opposed it. He felt the government was meddling in religion. Jog quotes him as saying the government had decided "rather to be wrong with Dr. Bhandarkar than right with myself." Dr. Bhandarkar was a scholar whose reading of the Sanskrit books helped the government.

Wikipedia says that in 1890 an eleven-year-old girl named Phulamani Bai died. It says Tilak, in opposing the bill, spoke harshly about her. We will not repeat his words.

## What Tilak proposed instead

Jog says Tilak offered a plan of his own, to be carried out by choice. Girls should not marry before sixteen and boys not before twenty. Widows should be allowed to marry again. Liquor and dowry should end, and no man should marry after forty. Jog says no reformer supported the plan. Wikipedia reports Tilak saying he would sign a paper for these ages.

## His reasons

Tilak said politics should come first "under the present circumstances." He held that once India was free, other changes would follow. He also said that reform worked best when people agreed to it and were not forced, and that a foreign government should not decide it.

## A school for girls

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia says Tilak opposed girls' schools. Jog says something narrower. Pandita Ramabai, who had become a Christian, ran a girls' school in Poona called the Sharada Sadan. *Kesari* warned that she might be trying to change students' religion. Jog writes that Ranade and Bhandarkar later cut their links with the school after she preached openly, and he says this proved Tilak right. Wikipedia counts Tilak's criticism of Ramabai's school, and of the schools of the Phule family, as part of his record against women's education.</aside>

## Untouchability

Wikipedia says that in 1918 Tilak did not sign a petition to end untouchability. It also quotes him as saying earlier that he would not accept as God any god who allowed untouchability. Wikipedia lists the quote with a note number, so we could not check where it first appeared.

## What historians say

Jog says Tilak's critics called him a narrow-minded bigot. He says that charge rests on "parrot like repetition", and that Tilak was a conservative who wanted the masses to come along with him. Jog also says that in some arguments Tilak took "fanatical" positions. Wikipedia says these positions clash with his image as a leader who united people.

## Why it matters

You can admire a person's courage against the British and still ask hard questions about their views at home. Both are part of the history.
