"Swaraj is my birthright, and I shall have it." The Nehru Centre in Mumbai calls it one of the most powerful cries of the freedom struggle. It is linked with Bal Gangadhar Tilak.

That is probably right. But when you ask where and when he said it, the answers differ.

## The meaning

*Swaraj* means self-rule. Tilak used the word as a goal for India. The High Court in 1916 quoted him from a speech about it: "the demand that the management of our affairs should be in our hands is the demand for swarajya." That speech was given in Marathi.

## First answer: a Congress meeting in 1907

The Tribune's fact file says that in 1907, at a session of the Congress, Tilak declared: "Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it." Wikipedia says that in the same year the Congress divided at Surat, and that Tilak led the group that wanted bolder action. The Tribune does not name the town.

## Second answer: Nasik, 17 May 1917

A newsletter of the Nehru Centre in Mumbai gives a different place and year. It says Tilak spoke at Nasik on 17 May 1917, on the first birthday of the Home Rule League. It gives the headline as "Swaraj is my birthright." But the text of the speech it prints says "Freedom is my birthright", and also says "we must guard this birthright." The newsletter does not explain the change of word.

## Third answer: another man's phrase

Wikipedia says that Tilak took up the slogan of Swaraj, and it adds that, according to its source, the slogan was first coined by his friend Kaka Baptista. Wikipedia also gives the English line "Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it!" and says it comes from Marathi and English sources, but the article does not name the speech.

<aside class="note"><b>Three versions</b>: The Tribune says 1907 and a Congress session. The Nehru Centre says Nasik, 17 May 1917. Wikipedia says Baptista coined the Swaraj slogan. None of these sources shows a recording or a newspaper report of the first speech. We cannot say which is right, and the line may have been said more than once.</aside>

## What kind of Swaraj?

Tilak did not always mean a break with Britain. Wikipedia says that in 1907 he stated that Swaraj would not mean leaving the British Empire. In 1916, his Home Rule League asked for self-government inside the Empire. The High Court noted his many statements of loyalty to the King, and said they did not decide the case.

## Why it matters

A famous line can be better known than its beginning. A careful reader keeps both facts: the words are linked with Tilak, and the first time he said them is unclear.
