"Swaraj is my birthright, and I shall have it." The Nehru Centre in Mumbai calls it one of the most powerful cries of the freedom struggle. It is linked with Bal Gangadhar Tilak.

That is almost certainly right. But when you ask where and when he said it, the answers differ.

## The meaning

*Swaraj* means self-rule. Tilak used the word as a goal for India. A High Court in 1916 quoted him from a speech, given in Marathi: "the demand that the management of our affairs should be in our hands is the demand for swarajya."

## A Congress meeting in 1907

In 1907, at a session of the Congress, Tilak declared: "Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it." That year the Congress divided at Surat, and Tilak led the group that wanted bolder action.

## Nasik, 17 May 1917

A newsletter of the Nehru Centre gives a different place and year. Tilak spoke at Nasik on 17 May 1917, on the first birthday of the Home Rule League, which had been started the year before, in 1916. The newsletter's headline is "Swaraj is my birthright." But the speech it prints says "Freedom is my birthright", and also says "we must guard this birthright."

## A friend's slogan

Tilak took up the slogan of Swaraj. His friend Kaka Baptista is said to have coined it first. The English line "Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it!" comes from Marathi and English sources, but no source names the speech.

The Tribune says 1907 and a Congress session. The Nehru Centre says Nasik, 17 May 1917. Wikipedia says Baptista coined the Swaraj slogan. None of these shows a recording or a newspaper report of the first speech. The line may have been said more than once.

## What kind of Swaraj?

Tilak did not always mean a break with Britain. In 1907 he stated that Swaraj would not mean leaving the British Empire. In 1916, his Home Rule League asked for self-government inside the Empire. The High Court noted his many statements of loyalty to the King, and said they did not decide the case.

## Why it matters

A famous line can be better known than its beginning. The words are linked with Tilak, and the first time he said them is not clear. Both facts can be true together. A good reader keeps them both.
