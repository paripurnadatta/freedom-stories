In the early months of 1897, a deadly sickness spread through the city of Poona. It was bubonic plague.

The government sent in soldiers to stop the plague. Many people hated what the soldiers did. Bal Gangadhar Tilak, who ran a Marathi newspaper called *Kesari*, wrote about it. Within weeks, he was arrested.

## The soldiers come

Plague came to Poona from Bombay in late 1896. By January 1897 it was an epidemic. The government set up a Special Plague Committee. Its head was an officer named W. C. Rand. The committee worked from 13 March to 19 May 1897 and brought in more than 800 soldiers.

The rules were hard. Soldiers entered homes by force. Sick people were taken away to camps. Travel was limited. Houses were searched.

People said the searches were rough and that the soldiers had broken things, including holy objects. Rand's own report, as Wikipedia describes it, said that customs were respected and that there was no believable complaint about insulting women. The sources do not agree, and Wikipedia presents this as a dispute between the two sides.

<aside class="note"><b>Bubonic plague</b>: a dangerous disease carried by fleas that live on rats. It killed many people in India in the 1890s. In those years nobody knew a sure way to cure it.</aside>

Tilak did not say that nothing should be done. A *Kesari* editorial of 16 February 1897 supported keeping patients apart. His complaint was about how it was done. He wrote that the government should not have given the job to "a suspicious, sullen and tyrannical officer like Rand." Wikipedia quotes this line, without giving its date.

## The poem and the speech

In June 1897 Poona celebrated a festival for Shivaji. Shivaji was a Maratha king of the 1600s whom many people admired. On 15 June 1897, *Kesari* printed two things.

One was a poem called "Shivaji's Utterances." It was signed "Mark of the Bhawani Sword." It attacks what it calls British misrule. The other was a report of the festival. It included a speech by Tilak, who was president of the festival. He spoke about Shivaji killing a general named Afzal Khan. He said, "Great men are above the common principles of morality."

The government had both pieces translated into English. One translator's note says that a phrase read as "burn them alive" could also mean "oppress or torment them exceedingly." The note shows how much depended on a translation.

## The shots

A week later, on the night of 22 June 1897, two British officers were shot on Ganeshkhind Road. They were coming home from a party for Queen Victoria's Diamond Jubilee. One was Lieutenant Ayerst. He died on the spot. The other was Rand. He died on 3 July 1897 in Sassoon Hospital.

The men who fired were two brothers, Damodar and Balkrishna Chapekar. Damodar said later that he wanted to take revenge for the way people had been treated during the searches. He was hanged on 18 April 1898.

Was Tilak linked to the shooting? The sources do not agree.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia quotes the historians Barbara and Thomas Metcalf, who think Tilak "almost surely concealed the identities" of the killers. The legal scholar Sukeshi Kamra, quoted by the Indian History Collective, says that by the government's own admission the link was never proved. The court did not try Tilak for the killing. He was tried for what he had printed.</aside>

## Arrest and trial

The date of Tilak's arrest is given in different ways. The Tribune says 27 July. G. P. Pradhan's book gives 27 July in one place and 29 July in another. We cannot tell which is right.

Tilak was let out on bail on 4 August. Justice Badruddin Tyabji granted it. The bail was large: two promises of Rs 25,000 each, and his own bond of Rs 50,000.

The trial was held in the Bombay High Court. It began on 8 September 1897 and ended on 14 September. The judge was Sir Arthur Strachey. The charge was sedition, under Section 124A. Tilak's lawyer, L. P. E. Pugh, argued that the pieces were not against the British government.

Nine jurors heard the case. Six were Englishmen and three were Indians. They talked for about forty minutes. Six voted guilty. The three Indians voted not guilty.

The judge had given the jury a strict view of the law. He told them that to excite disaffection meant, in his words, "simply the absence of affection." He said the accused need not have meant to cause a rebellion. The jury should think about the "natural and probable effect" of the pieces on readers. A modern writer says Strachey misstated an earlier judge's meaning. In 1916, the High Court itself said this definition was too wide. That story is told in "Three speeches, one court."

Asked if he had anything to say, Tilak said: "Whatever be the verdict of the jury, I think, I am not guilty."

The sentence was eighteen months of hard labour. Tilak asked the Privy Council in London for permission to appeal. A column in Bar & Bench says it refused.

## In prison

Tilak was kept in Yerwada prison. He began work there on a book, *The Arctic Home in the Vedas*. A friend named Gopalrao Gogate wrote it down as Tilak spoke. It was published in 1903.

He was set free on 6 September 1898, says Pradhan. The Tribune agrees on the date. Pradhan says he served about eleven months. A Sankalp India page says the government cut the sentence and that he came out at Deepavali. These accounts do not match, and neither explains the difference. We cannot say which is right.

## Why it matters

Historian Sukeshi Kamra argues that Tilak was punished for words, not for any act of violence that was proved. She also notes that newspapers repeated the articles, so more people read them than the government wanted.

You do not have to agree with everything Tilak wrote to see the question this case raised. How far can a newspaper go in blaming a government in a time of fear? The question has not gone away.
