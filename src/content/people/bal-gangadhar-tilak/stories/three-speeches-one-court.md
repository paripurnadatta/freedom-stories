Imagine you give a speech. You say that Indians should run their own affairs. You say it again and again, in town after town. Then the police may come and say you have broken the law.

That is what happened to Bal Gangadhar Tilak in the summer of 1916. He was nearly sixty. He had already spent years in jail for his writing. Now the question was about his words once more.

## The League and the speeches

In April 1916, Tilak started the Indian Home Rule League. It met at the Bombay provincial Congress at Belgaum. Its aim was self-government for India, inside the British Empire. Tilak's League worked in Maharashtra, Karnataka, and the Central Provinces and Berar. Annie Besant started her own League in September 1916 for the rest of the country.

Tilak travelled and spoke. Three of his speeches, all in Marathi, were picked out by the police. He gave one at Belgaum on 1 May 1916. He gave two at Ahmednagar, on 31 May and 1 June. All three were about *swarajya*, which means self-rule.

## The order

On 22 July 1916, a senior police officer named J. A. Guider asked the District Magistrate of Poona to act against Tilak. This detail comes from a newspaper column by Sanjoy Ghose, who names other writers as his sources. We did not find it in the judgment itself.

The Magistrate decided that the three speeches spread sedition. He ordered Tilak to sign a bond for Rs 20,000. Two other people had to promise to pay as well. The bond meant that Tilak must keep the peace for one year. If he did not, the money would be lost.

<aside class="note"><b>Sedition</b>: words or writing that try to make people hate or dislike the government. It was a crime under Section 124A of the Indian Penal Code.</aside>

<aside class="note"><b>Bond</b>: a written promise to pay money if you break a rule. A person who agrees to pay if you fail is called a surety.</aside>

The Magistrate also leaned on an old idea. He said that "disaffection" simply means the lack of affection. If you did not feel warm towards the government, that was enough.

That idea was nearly twenty years old. In 1897, Judge Strachey had told the jury at Tilak's first trial the same thing. One modern writer says Strachey got an earlier judge's meaning wrong.

## The High Court

Tilak asked the Bombay High Court to cancel the order. The column says that Muhammad Ali Jinnah filed the case for him and argued it. The two judgments we read do not name the lawyers. So the column is our only source for that part.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: English Wikipedia says Jinnah was Tilak's lawyer at his 1908 trial. The Bar & Bench column says Jinnah was only linked with the 1908 case "at some stage", and that other lawyers led it. For 1916, the column says Jinnah argued the case. The judgments do not say. We cannot settle this from the sources we opened.</aside>

Two judges heard the case. They were Justice Batchelor and Justice Shah. On 9 November 1916, they gave their answer. They set the order aside. Tilak did not have to sign the bond.

## What the judges said

Their reasons were careful. Here are the main ones.

First, they threw out the old idea. Justice Batchelor said that disaffection does not mean the mere lack of affection. The law itself said so, in an explanation attached to Section 124A.

Second, they looked at the aim. Tilak had said, in the judge's words: "the demand that the management of our affairs should be in our hands is the demand for swarajya." The judge said that asking for this was not against the law in itself.

Third, they looked at the means. In the speeches Tilak had said: "I do not say to any of you that you should do unlawful things in order to acquire these rights." The judges read the speeches as an argument for lawful and constitutional ways.

Fourth, they said the speeches must be read as a whole. A few hard sentences did not decide the matter. Justice Batchelor found that the general effect was to disapprove of the government. It was not to stir up hatred.

Fifth, they spoke about the Civil Service. Tilak had attacked it in strong words. The judges said that blaming the whole service could count as sedition if it made people hate the government. Whether it did was a question of fact, to be judged case by case.

## Where the judges were hard on Tilak

The judgment was not a compliment to Tilak. The judges did not like everything he said.

Justice Batchelor named passages that spoke of Indians as being kept in servitude. He said they were impossible to justify. Counsel did not try to defend them. But he thought their sting was softened by the words around them. Justice Shah went further. He called the talk of "slavery" and of an alien government "unfair and improper."

They also said something that cut against Tilak. Tilak had often said he was loyal to the King-Emperor. The judges said that loyalty to the King was no answer. The question was whether he tried to make people hate the Government. So loyalty to the Crown could not save him by itself. Reading the speeches as a whole did.

<aside class="note"><b>Disaffection</b>: a feeling of dislike or hostility. The court said that merely not being fond of a government was not the crime. The crime was trying to stir up hatred or contempt.</aside>

## What came after

The Home Rule League grew. English Wikipedia says its members rose from about 1,400 in April 1916 to about 32,000 in 1917. It marks this as a report, so treat the numbers with care.

On 20 August 1917, the British government said its policy was the "progressive realization of responsible government in India." Annie Besant was largely satisfied by the promise, and some moderates left the League. Later, Tilak went to England for a court case of his own, and the movement lost its leader for a time. Gandhi's rise changed Indian politics after that.

## Why it matters

In 1897, a judge had told a jury that the lack of affection was enough for sedition. Tilak had gone to prison for eighteen months. In 1916, two judges of the High Court said that this was not the law. Asking for self-rule, by lawful means, was not a crime.

That did not end sedition cases. They went on for many years. But on this one point the High Court had spoken clearly.
