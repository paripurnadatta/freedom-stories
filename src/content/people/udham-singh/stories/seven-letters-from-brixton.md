In 1940, a prisoner in Brixton Prison in London had little to do while he waited for his trial. He could read, if he had books. He could write, if someone would carry his letters out.

Udham Singh wrote at least seven letters from Brixton. The Tribune later reported on them. They are handwritten, and today they are kept in the Bhai Gurdas Library at Guru Nanak Dev University in Amritsar.

## Asking for books

Most of the letters went to a friend and helper in London, Shiv Singh Jouhal. The Tribune says that most of them ask Jouhal for more books. One of them asks for the Heer of Waris Shah, a work by Waris Shah.

The books tell us something about him as a reader. We do not know what other books he asked for.

## The name and the number

The letters are signed "M Azad". It is the short form of the name he used in court, which another story on this site, "A name on the arm", explains.

They also carry the same prison number, 1010. The Tribune notes this.

## Bhagat Singh

In a letter of 30 March 1940, the Tribune says, Udham Singh speaks of Bhagat Singh as his best friend. He also mentions someone "waiting for him on 23rd". The Tribune reads this as a hope to be hanged on the same date as Bhagat Singh.

<aside class="note"><b>Not copied here</b>: the same letter describes death in a way that is hard to read. We do not copy the words. A prisoner facing a trial may write many things he feels in the moment. They are not a plan for anyone else, and this story does not turn them into a lesson.</aside>

## The last letter

The last letter is dated 7 June 1940. His trial had begun three days earlier, and the death sentence had come two days earlier, on 5 June, according to Counsel. In the letter, he asks for a "Gutka Saheb". That is a small prayer book of Sikh hymns.

Did he get it? The Tribune says nobody knows.

## How the letters came home

Jouhal, his friend and aide in London, kept the letters until 1972. A man named M. S. Gill then persuaded him to give them to the university. So the letters were in a friend's keeping for thirty-two years after Udham Singh died.

## What the letters leave out

The letters do not say whether he thought the court was fair. They do not describe his hunger strike. They are short, and they say little about the shooting itself.

Counsel says that Singh also wrote letters claiming that the police had made up evidence. Those letters were not in the Tribune's account.

## Why it matters

When a person's story is told mostly by courts and newspapers, his own letters are precious. These ask for books and for a prayer book.

They show that in his last weeks he reached out to a friend and kept reading. The Tribune says his last request may never have been granted. A good story should not skip a small unanswered thing like that.
