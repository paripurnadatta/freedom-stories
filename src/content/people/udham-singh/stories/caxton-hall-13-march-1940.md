This is a story about a killing. We tell it plainly, without praise and without gore. A man died, and others were hurt. The story is hard, and it is part of the history of India's freedom struggle.

## The meeting

On the afternoon of 13 March 1940, a meeting took place at Caxton Hall in London. It was organised jointly by two societies, the East India Association and the Royal Central Asian Society. Sir Percy Sykes spoke on Afghanistan. This is how the barrister Paramjit Ahluwalia describes the day in Counsel magazine.

Sir Michael O'Dwyer was one of the speakers on the platform. He was seventy-five. He had once been the Lieutenant Governor of Punjab.

## Who was Michael O'Dwyer?

He held that office from 26 May 1913 to 26 May 1919. On 10 April 1919 he had two nationalist leaders, Saifuddin Kitchlew and Dr Satyapal, arrested. The Jallianwala Bagh massacre came three days later, in his time. Martial law was imposed on 15 April.

O'Dwyer did not order the massacre. But he did not reprimand General Dyer, and he thought Dyer's actions were right. Dyer was told by a message that his action was "correct" and that the Lieutenant Governor approved. O'Dwyer argued that the events at Amritsar "cleared the air."

So O'Dwyer was not the man who gave the order to fire. Many Indians, and the scholar Vinay Lal, hold that he shared responsibility. People at the time and since have disagreed about how much.

O'Dwyer was relieved of his office after the Punjab disturbances. In 1924 he won a libel case against Sankaran Nair and was given £500 in damages.

## The shots

At about half past four, six shots were fired from a Smith and Wesson revolver. Sir Michael O'Dwyer was killed, with two bullets, says Lal. Three other people were wounded: Lord Zetland, Lord Lamington and Louis Dane. Lord Zetland was the Secretary of State for India.

The man who fired did not try to run away. He told people that he had done his duty.

## Who was he?

He gave his name as Mohamed Singh Azad. We tell how he chose that name in "A name on the arm". The police later knew him as Udham Singh. He had come to Britain with the aim of killing O'Dwyer, and waited for a moment when it would have the greatest effect.

He was charged on 1 April 1940. The court tried him for murder. Counsel's article calls the case "the murder of Sir Michael O'Dwyer".

## Three stories that did not hold up

Over the years, three stories have grown around that afternoon. The Tribune looked at them and called them myths.

**The hollowed-out book.** A book is said to have hidden the revolver, with pages cut out. But Paul Bickley, a curator at the Crime Museum at New Scotland Yard, says the museum has his pocket diary, and "It's far too small to hide a gun." The Tribune says there is apparently no truth in the big-book story.

**The wrong man.** Some books say that he meant to shoot Dyer and made a mistake. One writer, Steve Fielding, points to a diary in which O'Dwyer's name is written "O'Dyer". But another scholar, Dr Navtej Singh, says Dyer died in 1927, thirteen years earlier, and Udham Singh would have known. The story is weak, because Dyer was long dead.

**The chauffeur.** The Times of 14 March 1940 reported that O'Dwyer was killed by his chauffeur. That gave rise to a story that Udham Singh had worked for him. The writer Roger Perkins says Udham Singh drove for a retired Indian Army officer. He "certainly did not" go near O'Dwyer, and O'Dwyer's family confirmed this.

## How people reacted

People did not agree about the act.

Jawaharlal Nehru condemned the killing as senseless in March 1940. In 1962 he praised Udham Singh as "Shaheed-i-Azam". Some members of the Punjab Congress refused to vote for a motion condemning it. The Times of London called him a "fighter for freedom".

At the same time the court called it murder. The man who died was a real person, with a family and a funeral. He is buried at Brookwood Cemetery, near Woking.

## Why it matters

The question of when violence is justified is a hard one, and people have answered it differently. A fair story shows what the killer did, what the court said, and what others thought. It does not make a hero out of a killing, and it does not hide that he was honoured afterward. The historian Kim Wagner says he was at once given the status of a martyr.

What it can do is keep the facts straight. The man killed was O'Dwyer, not Dyer. The gun was not in a book. And the story of 13 March 1940 is not the story of the whole of Udham Singh's life.
