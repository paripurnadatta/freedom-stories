This is a story about a killing. We tell it plainly, without praise and without gore. A man died, and others were hurt. The story is hard, and it is part of the history of India's freedom struggle.

## The meeting

On the afternoon of 13 March 1940, a meeting took place at Caxton Hall in London. It was organised jointly by two societies, the East India Association and the Royal Central Asian Society. Paramjit Ahluwalia, a barrister who wrote about the case in Counsel magazine, says that Sir Percy Sykes spoke on Afghanistan.

Sir Michael O'Dwyer was one of the speakers on the platform. Wikipedia says he was seventy-five. He had once been the Lieutenant Governor of Punjab.

## Who was Michael O'Dwyer?

Wikipedia says he held that office from 26 May 1913 to 26 May 1919. On 10 April 1919, it says, he had two nationalist leaders, Saifuddin Kitchlew and Dr Satyapal, arrested. The Jallianwala Bagh massacre came three days later, in his time. Martial law was imposed on 15 April.

Vinay Lal says that O'Dwyer did not order the massacre. But Lal says that O'Dwyer did not reprimand General Dyer and thought Dyer's actions were right. Wikipedia says that Dyer was told by a message that his action was "correct" and that the Lieutenant Governor approved. It adds that O'Dwyer argued the events at Amritsar "cleared the air."

So O'Dwyer was not the man who gave the order to fire. Many Indians, and Lal himself, hold that he shared responsibility. People at the time and since have disagreed about how much.

Wikipedia says that O'Dwyer was relieved of his office after the Punjab disturbances. It adds that in 1924 he won a libel case against Sankaran Nair and was given £500 in damages. We have not read the case itself.

## The shots

Counsel says that six shots were fired at about half past four. The gun was a Smith and Wesson revolver. Sir Michael O'Dwyer was killed. Three other people were wounded.

South Asian Britain names the three as Lord Zetland, Lord Lamington and Louis Dane. Lord Zetland was the Secretary of State for India. Lal says O'Dwyer was killed with two bullets.

The man who fired did not try to run away, says Lal. The NBT book says that he told people he had done his duty.

## Who was he?

He gave his name as Mohamed Singh Azad. We tell how he chose that name in "A name on the arm". The police later knew him as Udham Singh. The South Asian Britain site says he had come to Britain with the aim of killing O'Dwyer, and waited for a moment when it would have the greatest effect.

He was charged on 1 April 1940, says Wikipedia. The court tried him for murder. Counsel's article calls the case "the murder of Sir Michael O'Dwyer".

## Three stories that did not hold up

Over the years, three stories have grown around that afternoon. The Tribune looked at them. It called them myths.

**The hollowed-out book.** The NBT book says he hid the revolver inside a big book. Wikipedia repeats it, saying the book had pages cut out. But the Tribune quotes Paul Bickley, a curator at the Crime Museum at New Scotland Yard. Bickley says that the Crime Museum has his pocket diary, and "It's far too small to hide a gun." The Tribune says there is apparently no truth in the big-book story.

**The wrong man.** Some books say that he meant to shoot Dyer and made a mistake. The Tribune says one writer, Steve Fielding, points to a diary in which O'Dwyer's name is written "O'Dyer". Open magazine also mentions the diary. But another scholar, Dr Navtej Singh, says Dyer died in 1927, thirteen years earlier, and Udham Singh would have known. We think this story is weak. Dyer was long dead.

**The chauffeur.** The Tribune says that The Times on 14 March 1940 reported that O'Dwyer was killed by his chauffeur. That gave rise to a story that Udham Singh had worked for him. The writer Roger Perkins says Udham Singh drove for a retired Indian Army officer. Perkins writes that he "certainly did not" go near O'Dwyer, and says that O'Dwyer's family confirmed this.

## How people reacted

People did not agree about the act.

Wikipedia says that Jawaharlal Nehru condemned the killing as senseless in March 1940. It says that in 1962 he praised Udham Singh as "Shaheed-i-Azam". It also says that some members of the Punjab Congress refused to vote for a motion condemning it. It says that The Times of London called him a "fighter for freedom". These are claims from one page. We could not open the original statements.

At the same time the court called it murder. The man who died was a real person, with a family and a funeral. Wikipedia says he is buried at Brookwood Cemetery, near Woking.

## Why it matters

The question of when violence is justified is a hard one, and people have answered it differently. A fair story shows what the killer did, what the court said, and what others thought. It does not make a hero out of a killing, and it does not hide that he was honoured afterward. The historian Kim Wagner says he was at once given the status of a martyr.

What it can do is keep the facts straight. The man killed was O'Dwyer, not Dyer. The gun was not in a book. And the story of 13 March 1940 is not the story of the whole of Udham Singh's life.
