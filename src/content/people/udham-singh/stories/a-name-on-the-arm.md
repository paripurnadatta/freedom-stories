A police officer asks a prisoner his name. The prisoner holds out his arm. The name is written there, in tattoo ink: Mohamed Singh Azad.

This is the account that the barrister Paramjit Ahluwalia gives in Counsel magazine. He studied the trial papers. He says that Singh gave his name as Mohamed Singh Azad and that the name was tattooed on his forearm. He says that Singh showed it to the police.

## What the name says

It is a name with several parts. Mohamed is a Muslim name. Singh is the name of Sikhs. Azad means freedom. South Asian Britain says he gave this name to the police and in court as "a symbol of Hindu–Muslim unity in the fight for Indian freedom."

Vinay Lal gives the name in a slightly different form: Ram Mohammad Singh Azad. He reads it as a deliberate statement about the unity of people of different religions. The Commons note on his signature says that he wrote "Mohammed Singh Azad", and that "Ram" was added later by other writers. This is a note on a picture, and we have not seen a record that proves it.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Counsel and South Asian Britain give the name as Mohamed Singh Azad. Vinay Lal and the Tribune give Ram Mohammad Singh Azad. Wikipedia lists other names too. We say Mohamed Singh Azad, as the court record in Counsel does.</aside>

Three days after the shooting, on 16 March 1940, he wrote a letter to the investigating superintendent. Counsel quotes a line from it: "do not try to change my name whatsoever."

## Prison

He was held at Brixton Prison. Wikipedia says he was charged on 1 April 1940. Counsel says that on 19 April the prison doctor found him sane and fit to stand trial.

Then he stopped eating. Counsel says that letters from Brixton dated 24 May 1940 reported that he had been fed artificially since 1 May. That is feeding by a tube when a person will not eat.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: South Asian Britain says he went on a hunger strike for 36 days. Wikipedia says 42 days. We did not find the prison doctor's records. Counsel gives only the date when force-feeding began. So we do not say how long it lasted.</aside>

## The trial

The trial began on 4 June 1940, says Wikipedia, at the Old Bailey. Counsel says that on 5 June, in Court 3, Mr Justice Atkinson sentenced him to death, at the end of a two-day trial. The prosecution was led by G. B. McClure. South Asian Britain and Wikipedia say that the defence team included V. K. Krishna Menon.

Counsel names more of the lawyers. The prosecution had a junior counsel, Christmas Humphries, beside McClure. For the defence, it names St John Hutchinson and Krishna Menon. We do not know who spoke for Singh in which part of the trial.

Counsel says that Singh was physically affected by the hunger strike when he gave his evidence on the second day.

Vinay Lal says that Udham Singh never denied the killing. Lal calls the trial "but a formality". That is his view of how it ended. It is not a quote from the court. The question for the court was the killing itself, and on that the sources do not describe any doubt.

## The notebook

One part of the trial concerned a notebook. It was the pencil notebook of a detective, Sergeant Sidney Jones, listed as Exhibit 24. Counsel says that the entries had no times written in them.

Singh said in letters from prison that the police had made up evidence. At the trial, says Counsel, the judge himself said that the pencil marks showed "at least 3 writings". We do not know what the court made of this in the end. Counsel does not say that the verdict was in doubt.

Counsel also says that an official, Sir Philip Game, who spoke Hindi, talked with Singh for fifteen minutes. He wrote nothing down and was not called as a witness.

## The speech that was not heard

After he was found guilty, Singh wanted to speak. Counsel says that he said: "I say down with British imperialism in India and let us have peace." The judge, it says, refused to hear what he called a political speech. He told Singh he was "not going to hear any more of that". He ordered that nothing be published, under the Emergency Powers Act.

Wikipedia says that his courtroom statement was published only in 1996, after a campaign.

Counsel says that as he was led away he shouted "Inquilab" three times. It means "revolution". The official transcript leaves this out. But it appears in the notes of the Director of Public Prosecutions, the prosecuting lawyer.

<aside class="note"><b>Emergency Powers Act</b>: a law passed during the Second World War that let the government make strict rules, including limits on what newspapers could print.</aside>

Wikipedia also says that in court he said he had acted from a grudge and was "dying for my country". We have only that one page for those words.

## The end

We do not have a full record of the last weeks from the prison itself. What survives is mainly letters, which we describe in "Seven letters from Brixton".


The Court of Criminal Appeal turned down his appeal, says Lal. He was hanged at Pentonville Prison in London on 31 July 1940. We tell about the date problem in "After 1940".

The British Library holds a file on him, IOR/L/PJ/12/500. Its title covers his "activities outside India; assassination of Sir Michael O'Dwyer; trial, appeal and execution in London". It runs from 28 May 1936 to August 1940. The record gives no more description. We have not read the file.

## Why it matters

We do not know what Udham Singh thought on that morning. We know what he wrote on his arm. We know that a judge would not hear him. And we know that for fifty-six years the full text of what he said in court stayed out of the public eye.

That is a reason for historians to go back to the original papers. It is also a reason for readers to be careful with the words people put in his mouth. A name that a man chose for himself is the safest thing to repeat.
