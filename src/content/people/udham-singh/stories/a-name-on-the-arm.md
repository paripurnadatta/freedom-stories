A police officer asks a prisoner his name. The prisoner holds out his arm. The name is written there, in tattoo ink: Mohamed Singh Azad.

The barrister Paramjit Ahluwalia, who studied the trial papers, tells this in Counsel magazine. Singh gave his name as Mohamed Singh Azad. It was tattooed on his forearm, and he showed it to the police.

## What the name says

It is a name with several parts. Mohamed is a Muslim name. Singh is the name of Sikhs. Azad means freedom. Singh gave this name to the police and in court as "a symbol of Hindu–Muslim unity in the fight for Indian freedom."

Vinay Lal reads the name as a deliberate statement about the unity of people of different religions.

the court record in Counsel gives Mohamed Singh Azad. Lal and the Tribune give Ram Mohammad Singh Azad. A note on Wikimedia Commons says he signed "Mohammed Singh Azad" and that "Ram" was added later by other writers. Wikipedia lists still other names. This story uses Mohamed Singh Azad.

Three days after the shooting, on 16 March 1940, he wrote a letter to the investigating superintendent. It contained a line he meant: "do not try to change my name whatsoever."

## Prison

He was held at Brixton Prison. He was charged on 1 April 1940. On 19 April the prison doctor found him sane and fit to stand trial.

Then he stopped eating. Letters from Brixton dated 24 May 1940 reported that he had been fed artificially since 1 May. That is feeding by a tube when a person will not eat.

South Asian Britain says he went on a hunger strike for 36 days. Wikipedia says 42 days. The prison doctor's records were not seen, and the letters give only the date when force-feeding began.

## The trial

The trial began on 4 June 1940 at the Old Bailey. On 5 June, in Court 3, Mr Justice Atkinson sentenced him to death, at the end of a two-day trial.

G. B. McClure led the prosecution, with a junior counsel, Christmas Humphries. The defence team included St John Hutchinson and V. K. Krishna Menon. On the second day Singh gave his evidence. The hunger strike had left its mark on him.

Singh never denied the killing. Vinay Lal calls the trial "but a formality". That is Lal's view of how it ended, not a quote from the court. The question for the court was the killing itself, and nothing suggests doubt on that.

## The notebook

One part of the trial concerned a notebook. It was the pencil notebook of a detective, Sergeant Sidney Jones, listed as Exhibit 24. The entries had no times written in them.

Singh said in letters from prison that the police had made up evidence. At the trial the judge himself said that the pencil marks showed "at least 3 writings". The verdict did not change.

An official, Sir Philip Game, who spoke Hindi, talked with Singh for fifteen minutes. He wrote nothing down and was not called as a witness.

## The speech that was not heard

After he was found guilty, Singh wanted to speak. He said: "I say down with British imperialism in India and let us have peace."

The judge refused to hear what he called a political speech. He told Singh he was "not going to hear any more of that". He ordered that nothing be published, under the Emergency Powers Act.

a law passed during the Second World War that let the government make strict rules, including limits on what newspapers could print.

His courtroom statement was published only in 1996, after a campaign. Another page says that in court he said he had acted from a grudge and was "dying for my country". Only that one page gives those words.

As he was led away, he shouted "Inquilab" three times. It means "revolution". The official transcript leaves this out, but it appears in the notes of the Director of Public Prosecutions, the prosecuting lawyer.

## The end

The Court of Criminal Appeal turned down his appeal. He was hanged at Pentonville Prison in London on 31 July 1940. We tell about the date problem in "After 1940".

What survives from his last weeks is mainly letters, which we describe in "Seven letters from Brixton".

The British Library holds a file on him, IOR/L/PJ/12/500. Its title covers his "activities outside India; assassination of Sir Michael O'Dwyer; trial, appeal and execution in London". It runs from 28 May 1936 to August 1940. It was not opened for this story.

## What is not known

Some things about the trial are not on record. The hunger strike is given as 36 days in one place and 42 in another, and the prison doctor's own notes were not seen for this story. The official transcript leaves out the shout of "Inquilab". The full text of his courtroom speech stayed unpublished for decades. Where the papers are silent, this story stays silent too, and the reader is left to wonder.

## Why it matters

Nobody knows what Udham Singh thought on that morning. We know what he wrote on his arm. We know that a judge would not hear him. And we know that for fifty-six years the full text of what he said in court stayed out of the public eye.

That is a reason for historians to go back to the original papers. It is also a reason for readers to be careful with the words people put in his mouth. A name that a man chose for himself is the safest thing to repeat.
