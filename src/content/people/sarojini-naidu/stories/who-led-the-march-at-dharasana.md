On 21 May 1930, thousands of men walked towards a salt works at Dharasana, in Gujarat. They carried no weapons. Police with long wooden sticks, called lathis, waited for them.

A reporter named Webb Miller watched. His story was printed in newspapers around the world. Many accounts say that one of the people who led the march that day was Sarojini Naidu, the poet. Miller did not name her.

## Why salt?

The government taxed salt. On 2 March 1930, Gandhi wrote to the Viceroy, Lord Irwin. He offered to stop the Salt March if Irwin met eleven demands, including an end to the salt tax. Irwin ignored the letter. "On bended knees, I asked for bread and I have received stone instead," Gandhi said.

Gandhi made salt at the seashore at Dandi on 6 April 1930.

## Gandhi is arrested

The plan was to go on to the Dharasana Salt Works, about 40 kilometres south of Dandi. Then, around midnight on 4 May, officers arrived at Gandhi's camp. The Surat District Magistrate came with two Indian officers and thirty armed constables. Gandhi was sleeping on a cot in a mango grove. He was arrested and held without a trial, near Poona.

Abbas Tyabji, a retired judge aged 76, took over. He led the march with Gandhi's wife Kasturba beside him. Both were arrested before they reached Dharasana, and sentenced to three months.

was Kasturba arrested with Tyabji? Wikipedia says so. GKToday names only Tyabji and 58 marchers, arrested at Untadi on 12 May 1930.

## The march of 21 May

After those arrests, Sarojini Naidu led the march. She told the volunteers: "You must not use any violence under any circumstances." Another account gives her words as a call to stay "completely non-violent." She led more than 2,500 people, working with Manilal Gandhi, the Mahatma's son, and a man named Imam Sahib.

the Wikipedia article on Dharasana says that Naidu and Maulana Abul Kalam Azad led the protest after Gandhi was arrested, and does not mention Imam Sahib or Manilal. GKToday names Manilal and Imam Sahib. Webb Miller, who was there, names no leader at all.

## What the reporter wrote

Webb Miller was a correspondent for the United Press. His dispatch is dated 22 May 1930, from the camp at Dharasana. The Satyagraha Foundation prints it.

Miller wrote that more than 2,500 volunteers advanced on the salt pans. They did not hit back. An unnamed leader said: "These men have done a great work for India today." The leaders stood in front of the ranks and urged the volunteers to remember that Gandhi's spirit was with them.

"Not one of the marchers even raised an arm to fend off the blows," Miller wrote. The police, about 400 of them under six British officers, struck the volunteers with their sticks.

## How many were hurt?

The numbers differ.

- The government's own statement of 22 May, as Miller printed it, said that Congress itself counted 170 injured, and that only three or four were seriously hurt.
- Miller wrote that around noon, he counted more than 200 injured in the Congress camp hospital.
- British-owned newspapers the next day gave 320.
- A later summary gives 320 injured and two dead, and takes the figure from Miller.

Lord Irwin wrote to the King that the police had been forced to use "sterner methods." He said: "A good many people suffered minor injuries in consequence." That does not match what Miller described.

## The news travels

Miller's story was held back at first. The telegraph operators did not send it until he threatened to tell the world about the censorship. The story then appeared in about 1,350 newspapers. An American senator, John J. Blaine, had it read into the official record of the Senate.

Vithalbhai Patel, a former Speaker of the Assembly, said: "All hope of reconciling India with the British Empire is lost forever."

## And Sarojini Naidu?

She was arrested in the Salt March campaign, say several sources, and jailed for several months. The Congress says she was arrested with Gandhi, Nehru and Malaviya. Banglapedia and New World Encyclopedia say she and Gandhi were freed on 31 January 1931.

no page we read gives the day of her arrest. Gandhi's arrest was in the night of 4 and 5 May, and the accounts of it name none of the others. One article calls her the first woman arrested in the Salt March, but marks that claim as needing a source. So it is not clear whether she was still free on 21 May or already in jail.

## Where the accounts stop

Miller's dispatch is dated the day after the raid, but it is a reporter's account of what he saw, not a list of names. The Wikipedia articles and GKToday are later summaries, written by people who were not there. Between them they name Naidu with Azad, Naidu with Manilal Gandhi and Imam Sahib, and, in Miller's own words, nobody at all. All agree on the rest: a long line of unarmed men, a row of policemen with sticks, and a reporter who wrote down what happened.

## Why it matters

The march was meant to be peaceful, and the world read about it because a reporter described what he saw. That is the one fact on which all the accounts agree.

Who walked at the front is harder to say. It was named in three ways, as Naidu with Azad, as Naidu with Manilal Gandhi and Imam Sahib, and, by the man who was there, as no one. The later summaries are not eyewitnesses. A careful reader keeps the three apart.
