The Indian National Congress chose a new president every year. In 1925 it chose Sarojini Naidu. The session was at Kanpur, which was spelled Cawnpore in those days.

## Which number was she?

Wikipedia and Britannica call her the first Indian woman to be president of the Congress. EBSCO calls her the first woman to hold the post. But the Congress's own page on its women presidents calls her the second.

The Congress page lists Annie Besant first. It says Besant was elected in 1917 and presided over the session at Calcutta. It lists Sarojini Naidu second, in 1925 at Kanpur. After her come Nellie Sen Gupta in 1933 and later Indira Gandhi and Sonia Gandhi.

The same page says Annie Besant was born in London in 1847. Britannica and Wikipedia say Naidu was the first *Indian* woman. Put together, the facts fit: Besant was the first woman to be president, and Naidu was the first one born in India. That is our reading of the sources, and none of them says it in so many words.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: In what year was Besant president? The Congress's list of women presidents says 1917. Another Congress article on Naidu, and an article in The Wire, both say 1916. We use 1917, which comes from the Congress's own list of presidents. We did not find a source that settles the difference.</aside>

## What she said

The Congress article and The Wire both quote her presidential address. "In the battle for liberty, fear is the one unforgivable treachery and despair the one unforgivable sin."

The Wire says her speech spoke of sacrifice and of the struggle to free India from colonial rule. It calls her uneasy about the growing quarrel between Hindus and Muslims, and says she admired both Hindu and Islamic culture. It says Hindu-Muslim unity was her lifelong aim.

It also gives words she spoke to a women's conference: "No Indian could be loyal to the country and yet be narrow and sectarian in spirit."

The Wire article was written by a columnist and author, Praveen Davar. It is warm and admiring, and does not mention any critic.

## What it meant

The Congress's own article says her election "became an inspiration for women to join public life." That is the Congress's description, and we have no outside account of how women at the time received it.

## Why it matters

When two reliable-looking pages give different numbers, a good reader does not just pick one. Here the sources agree on the year and place of her election, and on her place in the list. They differ only on how to count the word "first."
