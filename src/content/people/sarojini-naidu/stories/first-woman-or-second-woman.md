The Indian National Congress chose a new president every year. In 1925 it chose Sarojini Naidu. The session was at Kanpur, which was spelled Cawnpore in those days.

Was she the first woman to lead the Congress, or the second?

## Which number was she?

She is often called the first Indian woman to be president of the Congress. Wikipedia and Britannica say so, and EBSCO calls her the first woman to hold the post. But the Congress's own page on its women presidents calls her the second.

The Congress page puts Annie Besant first. Besant was elected in 1917 and presided over the session at Calcutta. Sarojini Naidu comes second, in 1925 at Kanpur. After her come Nellie Sen Gupta in 1933 and later Indira Gandhi and Sonia Gandhi.

Annie Besant was born in London in 1847, the same page says. Britannica and Wikipedia say Naidu was the first *Indian* woman. Put together, the facts fit: Besant was the first woman to be president, and Naidu was the first one born in India. That is our reading of the sources, and none of them says it in so many words.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: in what year was Besant president? The Congress's list of women presidents says 1917. Another Congress article on Naidu, and an article in The Wire, both say 1916. We use 1917, which comes from the Congress's own list of presidents. We did not find a source that settles the difference.</aside>

## What she said

In her presidential address, she said: "In the battle for liberty, fear is the one unforgivable treachery and despair the one unforgivable sin." Both the Congress article and The Wire quote this.

Her speech spoke of sacrifice and of the struggle to free India from colonial rule, says The Wire. She was uneasy about the growing quarrel between Hindus and Muslims. She admired both Hindu and Islamic culture, and Hindu-Muslim unity was her lifelong aim.

At a women's conference she said: "No Indian could be loyal to the country and yet be narrow and sectarian in spirit."

<aside class="note"><b>A friendly writer</b>: the Wire article was written by a columnist and author, Praveen Davar. It is warm and admiring, and does not mention any critic.</aside>

## What it meant

Her election "became an inspiration for women to join public life", says the Congress's own article. That is the Congress's description. We have no outside account of how women at the time received it.

## Why it matters

When two reliable-looking pages give different numbers, a good reader does not just pick one. Here the sources agree on the year and place of her election, and on her place in the list. They differ only on how to count the word "first".
