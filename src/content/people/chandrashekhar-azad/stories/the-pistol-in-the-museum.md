Most museums are full of very old things. Stone statues, ancient coins, painted pots.

The Allahabad Museum has those too. But many visitors walk in and go straight to one small object near the entrance.

It is a pistol. It belonged to Chandrashekhar Azad.

## What kind of pistol?

It is a Colt pistol, made in America. Its exact model is the Colt Model 1903 Pocket Hammerless. It is small enough to fit in a coat pocket. It weighs less than a one-kilogram bag of sugar.

Azad was a very good shot. He had practised for hours in the forests near Jhansi.

## A pistol with a name

Azad gave his pistol a name. He called it Bamtul Bukhara.

Nobody is quite sure why he chose that name. But it shows how he felt about it. For a man always on the run, his pistol was like a companion.

<aside class="note"><b>Why "Pocket Hammerless"?</b> Old pistols had a little hammer sticking out at the back. This one has its hammer hidden inside. So it does not catch on cloth when it is pulled out of a pocket.</aside>

## The last morning

On 27 February 1931, Azad was surrounded by police in Alfred Park, in Allahabad. He had this pistol in his hand. He used it to hold the police back while his comrade escaped.

He had promised never to be taken alive. He kept that promise in the park that morning.

## Where it is now

Today it sits in the entrance hall of the Allahabad Museum.

The museum was set up in 1931, the same year Azad died. It stands inside the very same park where he fell. The park is now called Chandrashekhar Azad Park. So the pistol rests only a short walk from the place where its story ended.

## The journey to England and back

The pistol did not stay in India. It went to England with Nott-Bower, the police officer who led the attack in the park. A 1931 record suggests it was presented to him when he left India.

For about forty years it stayed with him.

Then, in 1972, an officer of the Uttar Pradesh government wrote to Nott-Bower. He asked for the pistol back. The Indian government's office in Britain also asked him. Nott-Bower agreed. In return, he asked for a photograph of Azad's statue in the park, and a letter of thanks.

The pistol came home, first to Delhi and then to Lucknow. In 1976 it moved to the Allahabad Museum.

<aside class="note"><b>Small differences</b>: one account says the pistol came back in 1977, not earlier. And one of Azad's comrades remembered his gun as a Mauser, a different make. The museum and the police inquest of 1931 both call it a Colt.</aside>

## Other pistols, other stories

Azad's pistol is not the only one with a story. Many young revolutionaries of that time carried Colt pistols.

Bhagat Singh had one too. His Colt is now kept in a museum in Indore, run by the Border Security Force.

So two friends who fought side by side are remembered in two different cities, by two small pistols.

## Why do people come to see it?

It is only a small piece of old metal. But many people stand in front of it for a long, quiet time.

They are not really looking at a weapon. They are thinking about a young man of 24. He chose to stay free, whatever it cost him.
