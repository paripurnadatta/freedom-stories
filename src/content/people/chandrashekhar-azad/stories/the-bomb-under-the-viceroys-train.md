On 23 December 1929, a special train was rolling towards New Delhi. It carried Lord Irwin, the Viceroy of India, and his wife.

<aside class="note"><b>Viceroy</b>: the most powerful British official in India. He ruled on behalf of the British king.</aside>

Lord Irwin was moving into his grand new home that day. It was the Viceroy's House, which we now call Rashtrapati Bhavan.

He had no idea that someone was waiting for his train.

## The hidden wire

Between the rails, someone had buried a bomb. A thin cable ran from it, hidden in the ground, to a small battery some distance away.

As the train came near, the battery was connected.

There was a loud blast. One carriage was badly smashed. It was the restaurant car, where food was served.

But the Viceroy was sitting in another part of the train. He was not hurt at all. Nobody was in the restaurant car at that moment. One attendant was slightly injured.

The newspapers said that many people on the train did not even realise what had happened.

## Who did it?

The bomb was the work of the HSRA. That was the secret group led by Chandrashekhar Azad.

The plan was made with Azad. Two of his comrades carried it out. One was Bhagwati Charan Vohra, a quiet thinker and writer. The other was a young man named Yashpal, who later became a famous Hindi writer.

One account says the bomb missed its target by just one carriage.

## Gandhi speaks

The news shocked the country. Mahatma Gandhi thanked God that the Viceroy was safe. Then he wrote an article against the attack. He called it "The Cult of the Bomb". Gandhi believed violence would only hurt India's fight for freedom.

The Congress party also passed a resolution against the attack.

## The answer

The revolutionaries did not stay silent. They wrote a reply.

It was written by Bhagwati Charan, working closely with Azad. They met in a small room above a shop in Aminabad, Lucknow. There they wrote a long paper called "The Philosophy of the Bomb".

In it, they explained why they had chosen their path. They asked young Indians to join them. Copies were spread secretly across the country.

<aside class="note"><b>Two paths</b>: Gandhi and the revolutionaries wanted the same thing, a free India. They disagreed deeply about how to get it. Both sides wrote openly about why they thought they were right.</aside>

## What it tells us

The train attack failed. The Viceroy carried on with his day as if nothing had happened.

But the argument it started went on for years. Could a country win freedom with bombs? Or only without them? Indians are still discussing that question today.
