On the evening of 10 March 1922, a police officer drove up to Sabarmati Ashram. He was Daniel Healy, the Superintendent of Police. He had two warrants. One was for Gandhi. The other was for Shankerlal Banker.

Banker published Gandhi's weekly paper, *Young India*.

## The charge

Both men were charged with sedition. The law was Section 124A of the Indian Penal Code, which punished anyone who stirred up hatred or disaffection against the government. The charge was based on three of Gandhi's essays in *Young India*. Their titles were "Tampering with Loyalty", "The Puzzle and its Solution" and "Shaking the Manes". A fourth essay was dropped before the trial.

<aside class="note"><b>Disaffection</b>: a feeling of not being loyal.</aside>

## The courtroom

The trial was held on 18 March 1922. It was not in a court building. The hearing took place in the Circuit House at Ahmedabad. The officials had stationed a battalion of infantry nearby. They feared trouble.

The judge was Robert Broomfield. The prosecutor was the Advocate General, Thomas Strangman, who had come from Bombay that morning. Strangman had also taken part in Tilak's sedition trial of 1897. About 200 people were in the room. They stood when Gandhi came in.

On the morning after the arrest, Gandhi and Banker told the magistrate that they would plead guilty. They did not need time to prepare a defence.

When the judge asked Gandhi his profession, he said he was a farmer and a weaver.

## What Gandhi said

Gandhi read a written statement. He said he had "been playing with fire" and would go on doing so. He called Section 124A the "prince" of the political sections of the Penal Code.

## The sentence

The judge gave Gandhi six years in prison. He said he would be glad if the government released him sooner. Banker got one year and a fine of 1,000 rupees, or six more months if he did not pay.

The judge admitted that many people thought Gandhi a "great patriot and a leader". But the law, he said, was no respecter of persons. He could only look at the charges. The whole hearing took about 100 minutes.

## What came after

The Viceroy, Lord Reading, said privately that Gandhi's career was "in the ditch". He added that not a dog had barked at his arrest. The author of the article thinks the mood in the courtroom told a different story. Strangman himself wrote later that many people wept.

Gandhi had an emergency operation for appendicitis and was released early in 1924. The British did not try him in court again. When they held him later, they used older laws that allowed prison without trial.

## Why it matters

Reading's guess was wrong. Gandhi's influence did not end. The non-cooperation movement had stopped as a mass campaign after Chauri Chaura. But it had not been undone. One assessment, quoted in the article, says it "hollowed out" British rule. The article puts that next to the plain fact that British rule did not end.
