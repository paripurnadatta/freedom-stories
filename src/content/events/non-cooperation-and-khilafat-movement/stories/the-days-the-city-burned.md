This is a sad story, and it is told gently.

In the autumn of 1921 the Prince of Wales, who was later King Edward VIII, toured India. The Congress and the Khilafat leaders called for a boycott of his visit and for a hartal, a general strike, wherever he went.

## 17 November

The Prince landed at Apollo Bunder in Bombay at about half past ten in the morning. Congress and Khilafat supporters stayed at home. Parsis, Jews and Anglo-Indians came out to welcome him.

Around the time he landed, Gandhi lit the first bonfire of foreign cloth in the city. Workers of the Elphinstone Mills, who were on strike, burned trams and cars.

Then a group of hartal supporters attacked passengers on the trams. The passengers were Parsis, Europeans and Anglo-Indians. By dusk a European had been killed.

## 18 November

The next day mill workers from Parel joined in. Three Parsi temples were attacked, among them the one that holds the Atash Behram. Shops, trams and cars were set on fire. Liquor shops owned by Parsis were targeted.

<aside class="note"><b>Atash Behram</b>: the highest grade of sacred fire kept in a Zoroastrian fire temple.</aside>

## 19 November

At half past three in the morning Gandhi began a fast. One page calls it his first hunger strike. He said he would neither eat nor drink until peace came back.

## 20 to 22 November

The violence began to fade on 20 November. Order returned to most of the city. That day a crowd of about 500 Parsis, armed with sticks, faced Hindus and Muslims on Princess Street.

On 21 and 22 November Gandhi ended his fast after his helpers told him the city was calm. On 22 November he met 150 city and community leaders. He told them to stop picketing liquor shops. He arranged payment for Parsi victims.

<aside class="note"><b>Two-sided</b>: On the first day most of the violence came from the side of the hartal. From 18 November it was both sides. Parsis and Anglo-Indians also attacked. Gandhi said the Parsis were less to blame. Some readers may find that lenient.</aside>

## The cost

At least 58 people died. They included five policemen, three Europeans, an American and two Parsis. Hundreds were hurt. Of the city's 600 licensed liquor shops, four were destroyed and 135 were damaged. People filed claims worth about 2.5 million rupees, mostly Parsis.

The authorities took 400 people to court. More than 100 went to prison. Two were sentenced to transportation for life, and two were executed.

## Gandhi's grief

Gandhi was shocked. He was most troubled that the Hindu and Muslim majority had attacked minorities. He took up a new slogan, calling for unity among Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs, Parsis, Christians and Jews. He thought of calling off the movement, but decided not to.

The British newspapers made little of the riots. One correspondent, Sir Percival Phillips of the *Daily Express*, did not. The Prince's own view of the riots is not known.

## Why it matters

Three months later, Chauri Chaura made Gandhi stop the movement. The Bombay riots were a first warning. A boycott that began in peace could be turned by a crowd in a day.
