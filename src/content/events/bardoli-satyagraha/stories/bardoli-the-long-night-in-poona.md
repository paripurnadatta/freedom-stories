This story is told by Mahadev Desai, who was there. He was Gandhi's secretary, and he wrote the history of the Bardoli struggle in 1929. He admired Patel, and he does not hide it. But he heard the words spoken that night.

## Six hard months

Since February 1928, the farmers of Bardoli had refused to pay a new, higher land tax. They had promised to hold out until the government accepted the old amount, or set up a fair inquiry.

The government struck back hard. Officers seized cattle, carts and goods. Notices were sent out saying that land would be taken. Some farmers' land was sold. Groups of Pathan men were brought in to help with the seizures. Village officials who sided with the farmers resigned their posts, and some people went to jail.

The farmers did not give in. They kept their doors shut against the seizure parties. They took their carts to pieces so they could not be taken away. They agreed among themselves not to work the land of outsiders who bought seized fields. Only a few buyers came forward.

## The Governor's terms

On 23 July 1928, the Governor of Bombay, Sir Leslie Wilson, made a speech to the Legislative Council. The government would agree to an independent inquiry, he said. But first, the tax now due had to be paid, and the struggle had to stop completely.

He asked the members of the Council from Surat district to say, within fourteen days, whether their people accepted these conditions. If not, the government would take whatever action it thought necessary.

The Surat members would not promise anything on the farmers' behalf. The government had begun to see that, in the end, it would have to deal with Patel himself.

## Invited to Poona

Patel then got a telegram from Dadubhai Desai, one of the Gujarat members of the Council. It invited him to Poona. Sir Chunilal Mehta, the Senior Member of the Governor's Council, had agreed to the invitation. Patel was to be his guest.

Patel reached Poona on 3 August 1928.

Talks went on through 3 and 4 August. Everyone, it seemed, wanted a settlement. The problem was how to let the government give way without seeming to give way. Everyone was looking for words that would protect the government's pride.

## One sentence

In the evening, Sir Chunilal came with a draft. It was a short letter for the Surat members to sign and send to the government. It said they were glad to inform the government that the Governor's conditions of 23 July "will be fulfilled".

Patel objected at once. The conditions had to be met before any inquiry. And only the farmers could meet them. "We say that it is impossible for us to pay even the old assessment until after the inquiry is announced," he said.

Sir Chunilal told him that was not his concern. If the members were willing to send the letter, Patel need not worry how, when or by whom the conditions would be met. The farmers would pay the old tax after the inquiry was announced.

Two other men, K. M. Munshi and Shivdasani, offered other drafts. Sir Chunilal would accept only the government's draft.

## Agreement on the rest

So Munshi turned to the other demands. Late that night they reached agreement on them. The seized land would be given back. The prisoners would be freed. The village officials who had resigned, the Talatis, would get their jobs back. Once these points were agreed, Patel let go of one smaller demand, a clause about indemnity, or payment for losses.

<aside class="note"><b>Talati</b>: a village accountant who kept the land records and helped collect the tax.</aside>

But the main sentence was still stuck. Sir Chunilal went to bed sure that the talks would fail. Patel, Desai and Patel's secretary, Swami Anand, sat up until the small hours. They wrote a letter to the Governor explaining why the talks had broken down. Then they went to rest.

## Two hours later

About two hours later, Desai woke up. He was still thinking about the sentence. He woke Patel.

After all, Desai said, the sentence promised nothing. Neither they nor the Surat members were giving any promise. If the government needed those empty words, it could have them.

Desai wrote down what came next:

"But it is a compromise with truth," said Patel.

"I agree," said Desai, "but it is a compromise on the part of Government."

"But won't we be party to it?"

"No. Not if we plainly tell Government what they are doing."

"Well, then, are you prepared to tell Sir Chunilal that Government are compromising with truth?"

"Certainly. I will do so forthwith."

Patel still was not easy. "I frankly do not see this game," he said. "Why don't they face facts? What do you think Bapu would say? What do you say, Swami?"

<aside class="note"><b>Bapu</b>: "father", the name close followers used for Gandhi.</aside>

Desai wrote that he never thought more highly of Patel than at that moment. The great leader wanted the opinion of his humble companions.

## The settlement

Desai went to Sir Chunilal and told him plainly that the government was compromising with the truth. "It is well that you make your position clear," Sir Chunilal replied. "I shall make it all clear to Government."

Patel said that if the Surat members had no objection to signing, neither did he. With that, the settlement was complete.

What did Patel get? An inquiry, announced in the very words he had suggested, even including a small grammar mistake in his draft. Only one clause was left out, about the harsh measures used against the farmers. The land, the prisoners and the Talatis would be dealt with by government orders.

## Racing the clock

Some farmers' land had been sold to buyers. It had to be handed back before the end of 6 August. The old Collector, Mr Hartshorn, had often said the sold land would never be returned. He had been moved away. The new Collector, Mr Garrett, persuaded the buyers to give it back.

The inquiry was announced on the same day the Surat members sent their letter.

## The old tax, paid

Patel told the farmers: "Now we have to pay up the old assessment, not the enhanced one."

There was one more hitch. The prisoners were not released at once. The government was not sure Patel had really accepted the settlement. The Collector came to ask him. Patel said he had already said so in a bulletin to the farmers. The Collector sent a telegram, and the next morning all the prisoners were freed.

The farmers began paying the old tax. In about a month, nobody owed anything.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia says the settlement cancelled the farmers' tax payment for that year. Desai, who was there, says the farmers paid the old tax, and that within about a month there were no arrears left.</aside>

## Why it matters

The government kept its pride: on paper, the Surat members had said the conditions "will be fulfilled". The farmers got almost everything they had asked for: an inquiry, their land, their jailed friends and the old tax.

But for Patel, the hardest question that night was not about land or money. It was whether he could sign words that were not quite true. He would not, until his companions agreed that he was not the one bending the truth.
