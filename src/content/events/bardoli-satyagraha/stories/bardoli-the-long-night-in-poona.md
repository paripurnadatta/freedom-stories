The talks had lasted late into the night. Vallabhbhai Patel and his companions were still studying a short proposed letter. Much of a settlement seemed possible. Yet these few lines threatened to undo it.

The place was Poona, now Pune. The time was early August 1928. For months, Bardoli farmers had refused to pay a disputed revenue demand. Officials had seized property and arrested people. Both sides were now discussing an end.

Patel had arrived on 3 August. Sir Chunilal Mehta, a senior member of the Bombay government, was helping the talks. Legislators from Gujarat also wanted an agreement. They had invited Patel to come.

The farmers wanted an inquiry into the tax increase. They also needed land restored, prisoners released, and village officials reinstated. Ending the refusal without dealing with those losses would leave many participants behind.

The government wanted payment and an end to the campaign. It also wanted the agreement to appear consistent with its earlier demands. Finding words acceptable to both sides became part of the negotiation.

<aside class="note"><b>Negotiation</b>: Discussion in which people try to reach an agreement.</aside>

Sir Chunilal produced a draft letter for the legislators to sign. It would say that the governor's conditions would be fulfilled. Patel questioned what that meant. Who was making the promise? When would the farmers have to pay?

He insisted that the inquiry must be announced before they paid even the old assessment. They could not accept wording that seemed to reverse that order. The dispute concerned an unfair increase. The settlement needed to preserve that central point.

Sir Chunilal said that the farmers would pay the old amount after the inquiry was announced. The legislators' letter, he argued, need not change that arrangement. Patel remained troubled by the language.

K. M. Munshi and others tried different drafts. These did not resolve the difficulty. They moved on to discuss the other conditions. By late night, agreement was possible on land, prisoners, and the reinstatement of village revenue workers.

The wording of the first letter still blocked the way. Mahadev Desai and Swami Anand were with Patel. They prepared a draft explaining why the talks might fail. Then they tried to rest.

Desai later wrote about what happened next. After a short sleep, he returned to the proposed formula. Perhaps the letter could be accepted if the farmers' own position remained completely clear. He woke Patel to discuss it.

Patel worried that accepting unclear language might compromise the truth. Desai argued that they were not making the disputed promise themselves. They should plainly explain their understanding to Sir Chunilal. Nothing should be hidden between the parties.

Patel asked for his companions' judgment. He also wondered what Gandhi would think. The account shows him seeking advice during a difficult decision. He was not simply issuing orders to everyone in the room.

<aside class="note"><b>Remembered by a witness</b>: The private discussion follows Mahadev Desai's account in <i>The Story of Bardoli</i>. It represents the campaign's view of the negotiations.</aside>

Desai spoke again with Sir Chunilal. Patel also repeated his concern. The farmers would pay the old assessment after the inquiry was announced. If the legislators accepted the proposed letter with that understanding, Patel would not block it.

The agreement could now move forward. The inquiry would examine the revenue dispute. Separate arrangements would deal with the return of land, the prisoners, and the village officials who had lost their posts.

Even then, a signed paper could not perform every task. Some confiscated land had been sold. The purchasers had to be found and persuaded to return it. Munshi and others went to Surat to help complete those arrangements.

The new collector, Mr Garrett, took part in that work. Returning sold land was more complicated than cancelling an unsold attachment. The settlement required action beyond the room where it had been discussed.

The inquiry was announced. Orders concerning restoration and release followed. Patel publicly expressed satisfaction and thanked those involved, including the government. He told the farmers to prepare to pay the old assessment.

But the prisoners were not released at once. Another misunderstanding remained. Officials wanted to know whether Patel had really accepted the settlement. He had already said so in a Gujarati bulletin to the farmers.

The collector checked with him and sent a message to the government. The misunderstanding was cleared. Desai records that the prisoners were released the next morning. Applications for reinstating the village officials were also accepted. Patel drafted the form they would use. The collector accepted it and ordered the men to be taken back. Restoring their posts required this further exchange after the broad agreement had been reached.

Now the farmers had their part to carry out. They began paying the old demand. According to Desai, the arrears were cleared in about a month. The refusal had been conditional, and the settlement brought that condition into effect.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Some summaries describe a cancellation of the year's revenue. Desai's account says the old assessment was paid after settlement; the disputed increase awaited inquiry.</aside>

The later inquiry was important in its own right. R. S. Broomfield and R. M. Maxwell examined the evidence. They visited villages, checked records, and heard the farmers' case. The arguments did not end when the protest stopped.

Their work supported a much smaller increase than the one originally imposed. Patel still challenged parts of their proposals. Agreement on an inquiry had not required him to accept every finding without question.

The August settlement had done something immediate. It opened a path to restore land and release prisoners. It also provided a process for testing the figures behind the larger demand.

The long night in Poona was spent over words. Those words mattered because people outside the room would have to live with them. A family waiting for its land needed an agreement that worked after the ink dried.
