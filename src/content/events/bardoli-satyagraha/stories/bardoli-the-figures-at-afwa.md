The farmers of Bardoli had won their main demand in August 1928: a proper inquiry into their new land tax. Now someone had to check the government's figures. Were they right?

## Two officers

The inquiry was given to two officers of the Indian Civil Service. R. S. Broomfield was a judicial officer. R. M. Maxwell was a revenue officer. They began work on 1 November 1928.

The farmers' case was put to them by the lawyer Bhulabhai Desai. Mahadev Desai, Narahari Parikh and Ramnarayan Pathak sat in on the inquiry for the farmers.

## What the tax was based on

The government had raised the tax mainly by looking at rents. If farmers paid high rents to lease land from each other, the reasoning went, the land must be worth more, and could be taxed more.

The farmers said this was wrong. Very little land in Bardoli was leased, they said. And the officer who made the report, Mr Jayakar, had not checked his figures properly.

The rules said Jayakar should have thrown out some kinds of deals that were not real leases. He said he had done so. If he had, there was a simple test. The total of all leases in a village should be bigger than the total of the "checked" leases. It could never be smaller.

## The first village

The officers asked each village's record-keeper, the Talati, to make tables from the village records. The first village they examined was Afwa.

In Afwa, the result was a puzzle. The "checked" area in Jayakar's report was much bigger than the whole area that was leased at all.

For one kind of farmland, the village records showed about 63 acres leased. Jayakar's checked figure for the same kind of land was about 127 acres. That is about twice as much land as was leased in the whole village. Something was badly wrong.

<aside class="note"><b>Lease</b>: an agreement to rent land from its owner for a payment.</aside>

## Bhatlav Moti

At first, Mahadev Desai wrote, Broomfield seemed to think the farmers were exaggerating. That, he said, was the way of peasants all over the world.

About a week later, the officers came to Bhatlav Moti. It was a village of mostly Koli families, and one of the poorest in the taluka. Yet its rent figures had pushed it up into a higher tax group.

The officers asked the village officials if Mr Jayakar had ever visited.

"No, never," they said.

Did they know who Jayakar was? They had heard he was a Deputy Collector, they replied. But they had never had the good fortune of seeing him, so how could they say?

The farmers' side had checked all the leases in the village. Nearly three-quarters of them, they found, were not real leases at all. They were loans, written down as leases. Jayakar's total almost exactly matched the total of all these papers. He had not taken out the false ones.

## The officers change their minds

After that, Desai wrote, the officers were no longer surprised when villagers said there had been no proper inquiry before. Wherever they had time, they dug deep, and Desai praised them for doing it carefully and honestly.

In their report, they wrote that one could not really be sure of anything in those tables of figures.

<aside class="note"><b>Result</b>: Wikipedia says that after the inquiry, the rise in tax was cut to 6.03 per cent, from the 22 per cent the government had fixed.</aside>

## Why it matters

The Bardoli farmers had said all along that the figures were wrong. The government had not listened. In the end it was the government's own officers, checking village by village, who agreed with them.

The lesson of Afwa is a simple one. Before a government takes money from people, its numbers should add up.
