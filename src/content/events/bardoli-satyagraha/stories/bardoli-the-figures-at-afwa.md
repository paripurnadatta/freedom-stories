At Afwa, the numbers did not fit. Officials examining Bardoli's tax dispute asked for fresh calculations from village records. The result raised a question about figures used in the earlier assessment.

This was after the 1928 settlement. The farmers had won an inquiry into the increased land revenue demand. R. S. Broomfield and R. M. Maxwell were carrying it out. Their work took them beyond speeches and into records.

The dispute concerned how much land had been rented and at what rates. Rent figures had helped support the tax increase. If those figures were unreliable, the reasoning built upon them needed another look.

Some kinds of transaction were supposed to be left out of the calculation. Once unsuitable entries were removed, the selected total should not exceed the original whole. Yet at Afwa, the supposedly checked area was larger than the gross area.

<aside class="note"><b>Gross total</b>: The whole amount before anything is removed.</aside>

The inquiry officers had their head clerk prepare the figures again. This was not simply a contest between two confident speakers. The village books could be checked against the tables in the earlier report.

The farmers' representatives helped with the inquiry. Bhulabhai Desai presented their case. Narahari Parikh, Ramnarayan Pathak, and Mahadev Desai worked through the investigation. Local workers assisted them.

The officers began their preparations in November 1928. Village visits continued into the following year. Bardoli and neighbouring Chorasi both came within the inquiry. The argument over a tax increase required months of further work.

The new findings supported a much smaller overall increase. However, Patel did not agree with every proposal. In June 1929, he wrote again to the government. He argued that some recommendations still rested on insufficient evidence.

That later criticism did not cancel the importance of the inquiry. It showed why winning a chance to be heard was different from agreeing forever with the answer. Records and calculations could still be questioned.

Mahadev Desai included the Afwa example in his book. The book strongly supported the farmers. It also gave readers a concrete calculation to examine. A large dispute had reached a small, testable point.

The campaign had asked for an inquiry on the ground. At Afwa, that meant returning to the village record and adding the figures again.
