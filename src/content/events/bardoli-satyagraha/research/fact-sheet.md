# Fact sheet: bardoli-satyagraha

One page, for writing. The six stories in `stories/` already exist in an older form: rewrite them
from this sheet, and do not treat the old text as a source. Everything below comes from Mahadev
Desai's *The Story of Bardoli* (1929), which was opened in full on 27 October 2026, plus the
Wikipedia article for cross-checking only. Source keys are at the end. If a fact is not here, do
not write it.

Six stories: two flagship (900+ words), four short (350+).

Who is telling this: Mahadev Desai was Gandhi's secretary and was in the room during the Poona
negotiations. He writes as a participant and an admirer of Patel, and he says so. That is a
strength (he heard the words) and a limit (he is not neutral). Do not present his account as a
court record.

The spine: 4 February 1928 (first meeting), 6 February (letter to the Governor), 12 February
(resolution passed in silence), 15 February (first instalment due), 3–4 August (Poona), 6 August
1928 (settlement). The campaign ran about six months.

---

## 1. Seven Days to Decide — FLAGSHIP

- The place: Bardoli taluka in Surat district, "the bulk of the population of something over
  87,000" are agriculturists. [D p. 3]
- The grievance: a revision of land revenue. The Settlement Officer recommended 30 per cent, the
  Settlement Commissioner 29 per cent, and the Government fixed **22 per cent**, partly to allow
  for a fall in cotton prices. But regrouping pushed some villages up by "about 50 to 60 per cent"
  on top of that. [D pp. 16, 32]
- The people had been agitating since the middle of 1926 against Jayakar's report. They had
  petitioned, met the Revenue Member early in 1927, sent long representations, and held a
  conference at Bardoli in September 1927 presided over by Dadubhai Desai, M.L.C. All of it
  failed. [D pp. 27–28]
- 4 February 1928, the day before the first instalment fell due. Patel took the presidentship of
  the conference. Before it opened he met the workers, who "were anxious not to precipitate
  matters, and wanted the President to examine and cross-examine the people before he recommended
  any course of action". [D p. 30]
- So the meeting was not a rally but a questioning. **79 villages** sent representatives —
  "Kanbis, Anavlas, Kaliparaj and Parsis". "They were men with something to lose and they knew
  what Satyagraha would mean for them." Some paid Rs 300 to Rs 500 in land revenue; one Parsi paid
  Rs 700 a year. [D p. 30]
- The split, in their own words: five or six villages wanted to tender the **old** rate and make
  the Government sue for the balance. The rest wanted to refuse the whole revised assessment.
  [D pp. 30–31]
- The exchange to build the story on. Each representative was asked: are you speaking for the
  whole village, and what will you do if the others fall back?
  - "I will stand alone."
  - "My village will stand together, so long as the taluka stands together, not afterwards."
  - "We are all determined, Hindus and Musalmans, but I must say that 25 per cent of the Musalmans
    are rather shaky."
  - "Sir, if four true people could be found to stand firm, come what might, I am sure of success."
    Asked what he meant by four: "By four I mean four of the top men." Asked whether he was one of
    them: "No, sir, I am the fifth. I will follow the four."
  - Four men then stood up. The five villages that had wanted to pay the old rate "announced their
    decision to go with the taluka". [D pp. 30–31]
- Patel's answer was not to accept the offer. He said he had "not the slightest doubt" about the
  justice of the case but was "not sure of their strength". He asked them to search their hearts
  and say whether they could carry on "a non-violent and truthful struggle for any length of time"
  against a Government that might use all its forces. He warned them against a hasty decision and
  **gave them seven days to think it over**. [D pp. 31–32]
- 6 February 1928, letter to the Governor. He asked him "to afford a fair opportunity to the
  people to place their case before an impartial tribunal clothed with adequate authority", and
  said that without it he would have "no alternative but to advise the people to refuse to pay the
  assessment and peacefully and quietly suffer the consequences". He added: "Should your
  Excellency think it necessary that we should meet in the matter, I would gladly wait on you."
  [D p. 32]
- The legal argument in the same letter, which is easy to miss: the Settlement Commissioner had
  abandoned the old grouping of villages and regrouped them, which raised some by 50–60 per cent,
  and 31 villages were served notice in the last week of July to object within two months to what
  was in reality a final order (G.R. No. 7259/24 of 19 July 1927). [D pp. 32–33]
- The reply was "a curt reply through his Private Secretary" that the letter "had been sent to the
  Revenue Department for official consideration and disposal". Desai's comment: "a reply which is
  no reply". The Governor would not see him. [D pp. 33–34]
- 12 February, the meeting again, larger. In the week between, the villagers had gone home and
  begun taking signatures on a tentative pledge. [D p. 34]
- The reports from the villages, which are the heart of the scene:
  - "58 men have given their signatures in our village, 12 have not yet. But that does not matter."
  - "All excepting the village headman have signed the pledge, but the headman is not hostile to us."
  - "Our Patel has already paid up and a Vania from a neighbouring village also, but we never
    counted on them."
  - "There is a section among us which is recalcitrant, but we believe as though they were not of
    us. Our section will never pay, come what may, and trust the rest to the will of God."
  - "All have given their signatures. There is no fear of any one flinching."
  - "Hold me responsible for the whole of the village. My village will stand through thick and
    thin." [D p. 34]
- Patel's speech: "I still ask you to think twice before you take the plunge. Do not derive comfort
  from the feeling that you have as your leader a fighter like myself. Forget me and forget my
  companions, fight if you feel that you must resist oppression and injustice. Do not take the
  plunge lightly. If you miserably fail, you will fail not to rise again for several years, but if
  you succeed you will have done much to lay the foundations of Swaraj." [D p. 34]
- Then the device that makes it a story about consent: "Now I am going to ask you to take charge of
  the resolution, you will move it and you will second and support it. None of us will speak on it.
  It will be the expression of your own free will and choice." [D p. 34]
- The resolution: the settlement is "arbitrary, unjust and oppressive", and occupants should
  "refuse payment of the revised assessment until the Government is prepared to accept the amount
  of the old assessment in full satisfaction of their dues, or until the Government appoints an
  impartial tribunal". [D p. 35]
- How it passed: after readings from the Koran and a Hindu hymn and the repetition of Ramanama by
  the whole conference, "the resolution was passed in solemn silence". [D p. 36]
- Watch: do not turn the "fifth man" into a named hero; he is not named. Do not print the whole
  resolution. Do not make Patel modest — he says plainly that he is a fighter, and then asks them
  to forget him.

## 2. The Long Night in Poona — FLAGSHIP

- The campaign ran from February. The first month went on signatures; then came attachment of
  cattle and land, forfeiture notices, and the social boycott. [D ch. VI, XX]
- The way in: a telegram from Dadubhai Desai inviting Patel to Poona on behalf of the Gujarat
  members, sent "with the consent of Sir Chunilal Mehta, the Senior Member of the Governor's
  Council, if not at his instance". Patel was also invited to be Chunilal's guest. **He reached
  Poona on 3 August 1928.** [D p. 170]
- The position: Government had sent an ultimatum to the Surat members, but had realised it would
  "ultimately have to treat with Sjt. Vallabhbhai". The Surat members refused to commit him in any
  way. Everyone was looking for "a formula that would preserve Government's prestige". [D p. 171]
- The formula, drafted by Chunilal for the Surat members to sign and send to the Revenue Member:
  "We are glad to be able to say that we are in a position to inform Government that the
  conditions laid down by His Excellency the Governor in his opening speech to the Council dated
  23rd July will be fulfilled." [D p. 171]
- Why Patel objected: the conditions had to be fulfilled *before* an inquiry was granted, and "we
  say that it is impossible for us to pay even the old assessment until after the inquiry is
  announced". Chunilal: "That is not your concern. If the members are agreeable to addressing the
  letter, you need not worry as to how, when and by whom the conditions will be fulfilled. You will
  pay the old assessment after the inquiry is announced." [D p. 171]
- K. M. Munshi and Shivdasani put up alternative drafts; Chunilal "swore by the Government draft".
  [D p. 171]
- What did get agreed, late that night: restoration of lands, release of prisoners, reinstatement
  of the Talatis. Patel waived the clause about indemnity once the rest was agreed. [D pp. 171–172]
- The scene to build the story on: Desai could not sleep, got up after about two hours, woke Patel,
  and argued that the formula was "innocuous, if meaningless" — nobody gives an undertaking, so
  Government is the one compromising. [D p. 172]
  - Patel: "But it is a compromise with truth."
  - Desai: "I agree, but it is a compromise on the part of Government."
  - Patel: "But won't we be party to it?"
  - Desai: "No, not if we plainly tell Government what they are doing."
  - Patel: "Well then, are you prepared to tell Sir Chunilal that Government are compromising with
    truth?"
  - Desai: "Certainly. I will do so forthwith."
  - And then Patel: "I frankly do not see this game. Why don't they face facts? What do you think
    Bapu would say? What do you say, Swami?"
- Desai's own comment, worth one sentence: "Never was the Sardar higher in my estimation than that
  day" — because he wanted the opinion of "humble companions". [D p. 172]
- Desai told Chunilal. Chunilal: "It is well that you make your position clear. I shall make it all
  clear to Government." Patel then said that if the Surat members had no objection to signing, he
  would have none. "The settlement was complete." [D p. 173]
- What Patel got: the inquiry "in the very words suggested by Sjt. Vallabhbhai including an
  unconscious grammatical slip — minus the clause about coercive measures". Restorations were to
  follow by executive order. [D p. 174]
- The deadline: sold lands had to be re-transferred "before the fateful 6th of August had expired".
  The old Collector, Hartshorn, who had "often declared that sold lands would never be restored",
  had been transferred; the new Collector, Garrett, persuaded the purchasers to return them.
  [D p. 174]
- Then: inquiry announced the same day the letter went to the Revenue Member; orders to restore
  lands, release prisoners and reinstate Talatis, the Talatis "if they applied in the proper form".
  [D pp. 174–175]
- Patel's instruction to the peasants: "Now we have to pay up the old assessment, not the enhanced
  one. I hope you will keep yourselves in readiness to pay the old assessment." [D p. 175]
- A snag worth telling: the prisoners were not released at once, because Government was not sure
  Patel had accepted the settlement. The Collector was sent to ask him. Patel said he had already
  said so in a Gujarati bulletin to the peasants; the Collector wired Government; "the next morning
  all the prisoners were released". [D p. 175]
- The end: the people began paying the old assessment, and "in about a month's time there were no
  arrears of revenue". [D p. 175]
- Watch: the peasants paid the **old** assessment. They did not have the year's revenue cancelled.
  Wikipedia says the revenue for the year was cancelled; that is wrong on Desai's account. Say so
  in a "Two versions" note if the story needs it. Do not describe a signed treaty or a ceremony.
  The "settlement" was a letter, an announcement and executive orders.

## 3. News from the Villages — SHORT

- The problem: "The peasants could not be asked to subscribe to daily papers or even to the weekly
  Navajivan, and outside papers could at best give an outside view of the campaign." [D p. 36]
- The answer: a publicity office with **Jugatram Dave** at its head, "with an artist's pen and with
  a knowledge of the whole taluka at his fingertips". [D p. 36]
- The arrangement: a daily news bulletin; Patel's speeches published as pamphlets and "distribute[d]
  ... free to the agriculturists all over the taluka". [D p. 36]
- The technical detail that makes it concrete: "For four or five days cyclostyled copies were
  issued, but arrangement was soon made to get them printed daily at Surat, and a start was made
  with 5,000 copies." [D p. 36]
- The result: "the villagers waiting anxiously for the patrikas every morning and devouring the
  contents with avidity. All the Gujarati and almost all the English dailies of Bombay reproduced
  them verbatim". [D p. 36]
- Why it mattered: the campaign was fought in 79+ villages at once, by people who could not travel
  to hear him. The bulletin is how one man's speeches reached a taluka, and how the taluka's news
  reached him.
- Watch: no invented office, no invented printer, no scene of a boy on a bicycle. The only facts
  are the office, the man, the cyclostyle, the Surat printing, the 5,000 copies and the reading.
  Do not give circulation figures beyond 5,000, and do not say how often it appeared beyond "daily".

## 4. The Opponent Patel Helped — SHORT

- The campaign used social boycott. Desai is frank that it was the weapon "that exasperated the
  Government", and that it was not an offence under the Penal Code. [D pp. 110–111]
- Patel's own limit, in his words: "in no circumstances should a Satyagrahi refuse to minister to
  the physical needs of the party boycotted. Eschew by all means molestation or oppression. We may
  not refuse any one milk, water, foodstuffs, help in case of illness or worse. We cannot afford to
  prosecute boycott at the expense of our humanity." [D p. 110]
- The case: a Parsi in **Manekpur** complained of being "molested in various ways". Patel "rushed
  to the spot" and called an informal meeting to which he invited the Parsi. [D p. 111]
- What was true and what was not: the Parsi "confessed that the complaint about refusal of medical
  aid was false", but said intending customers were enticed away from his liquor shop, that he was
  refused the services of a barber, and that he could get no agricultural labour. [D p. 111]
- Patel's ruling: "Of course you cannot compel any one to go to his liquor shop, but it would be
  wrong if you asked any one to prefer some other shop to his shop. The services of the barber
  should not be refused, and even agricultural labour. He is penitent and he asks for help, you may
  not refuse it." [D p. 111]
- The distinction to draw: a boycott of a shop is one thing; refusing a barber, a labourer's work or
  medical help is another. Patel kept the first and forbade the second. He also insisted on
  relaxation of the boycott for "men in the taluka belonging to small communities like the Parsis".
  [D p. 111]
- Watch: this is one case, not a general acquittal of the campaign. Desai records it because it
  shows a limit being enforced; he does not claim there were no other abuses. Do not invent the
  Parsi's name, his shop, or the meeting's size. Do not have Patel apologise — he rules.

## 5. The Figures at Afwa — SHORT

- Background: the people said the Settlement Officer's rental statistics were wrong, and that the
  leased area in the taluka was very small. So the enquiry officers asked each village Talati to
  supply tabulated figures from the village records. [D p. 188]
- The inquiry: the **Broomfield Committee** — R. S. Broomfield, I.C.S. (Judicial Officer) and
  R. M. Maxwell, I.C.S. (Revenue Officer). [D p. 187]
- The rule they were testing: under the Settlement Manual the Settlement Officer must exclude
  certain transactions from his statistics, and Jayakar had claimed he had excluded them. If he
  had, the gross total of all leases should have been **larger** than the total of scrutinised
  leases, never smaller. [D p. 188]
- Afwa, the first village examined, produced "a result which was a perfect puzzle. There the
  so-called scrutinized area considerably exceeded the gross area." [D p. 188]
- The figures, exactly as Desai prints them (acres–gunthas; rupees–annas–pies):
  - Gross totals for Afwa: Jirayat 62–36, assessment Rs 239–8–0, rent Rs 937–0–0; Kyari nil; Mixed
    66–15, assessment Rs 401–13–0, rent Rs 1,805–7–0.
  - The "scrutinised" figures in Jayakar's appendices: Jirayat 127–16, assessment Rs 574–4–0, rent
    Rs 1,821–8–0; Kyari 49–14, assessment Rs 397–1–0, rent Rs 2,346–0–0.
  [D pp. 188–189]
- The next village, **Bhatlav Moti**, mostly Kolis and "one of the poorest in the taluka", had been
  promoted from Group II to Group I on its rental statistics. The village officers said Jayakar had
  never visited: "No, never." Asked whether they knew who Jayakar was: "We have heard that Jayakar
  was Deputy Collector, but having never had the good fortune of seeing him, how can we say who he
  was?" [D p. 189]
- There, "nearly 75 per cent" of the leases were "no genuine leases but simply interest
  transactions", and Jayakar's total "nearly tallied with the gross total of all leases", showing
  he had not eliminated them. [D p. 189]
- The Committee's own conclusion, paragraph 42 of their report, quoted by Desai: "Indeed one cannot
  really be sure of anything in connection with these appendices, and as soon as one realizes that
  the Talatis were mainly responsible for them it is hardly necessary to look for any other
  explanation of their deficiencies as material for a settlement." [D p. 189]
- Also fair to record: Desai says that once the officers saw the problem, they probed "with an
  earnestness and conscientiousness that was creditable to them", and that they reached their
  conclusion "deliberate[ly]". Broomfield began by thinking peasants exaggerate; he changed.
  [D p. 189]
- Watch: this is a mistake in the statistics, exposed by the Government's own inquiry. It is not
  proof that every official invented records, and Desai does not say so. Do not accuse Jayakar of
  fraud; say the figures did not add up. Keep the arithmetic simple — one village, one contradiction.

## 6. The Neighbours Who Waited — SHORT

- Chorasi taluka had "suffered the same injustice as Bardoli at the hands of the same Settlement
  Officer", and had been agitating "in the orthodox way". Some of its people came to Bardoli and
  asked Patel to join Chorasi with Bardoli. [D p. 34]
- Patel refused, and gave his reasons: "I know very well that you have suffered the same injustice,
  but I am afraid it is beyond my power today to organize two talukas." [D p. 34]
- The reason was not modesty but arithmetic and knowledge of the ground: "A very large percentage
  of the occupants in your taluka are non-cultivating residents of Surat and Rander. You cannot
  expect them to join you. They would be the first to pay up and place you in a sorry predicament.
  No, I should dissuade you strongly from thinking of Satyagraha." [D p. 34]
- What he promised instead: "There is no doubt that if Bardoli succeeds, you will also be treated in
  the same way; you had better watch and wait... Rest assured that if Bardoli succeeds in getting
  justice, Chorasi will not be denied it." [D p. 34]
- What happened: when the Broomfield Committee came, it "moved to Chorasi. They camped in that
  taluka", and 21 villages in Chorasi were inspected alongside 49 in Bardoli. The Chorasi inquiry
  "was an offshoot of the Bardoli" one. [D pp. 187, 190]
- Watch: this is a story about a leader saying no to people with a good case, and being proved
  right later. Do not make it a rejection. Do not invent a Chorasi delegation's names or a
  conversation after the refusal. The promise and the inquiry are the two ends of the story.

## Do not say

- Do not write that the year's revenue was cancelled, or that the peasants paid nothing. They paid
  the old assessment, and within about a month there were no arrears.
- Do not give the campaign a single start date. The resolution was 12 February 1928; the first
  instalment fell due on 15 February; the refusal played out over the following months.
- Do not call the 22 per cent the whole increase for every village: regrouping pushed some up by
  50–60 per cent.
- Do not name the "fifth man", the Manekpur Parsi, or any villager. Desai does not.
- Do not print Desai's long resolution or his long quotations from Patel's speeches. One or two
  sentences each.
- Do not describe the Poona talks as a treaty, a signing ceremony or a victory parade.
- Do not make Sir Chunilal Mehta a villain. Desai praises his patriotism and says he knew
  Government's mind better than anyone there.
- Do not invent weather, crowd sizes, or what anyone was thinking. Desai gives hours ("until the
  small hours", "in about two hours") and rooms, not atmosphere.
- No flag, no chakra, no tricolour in any scene.
