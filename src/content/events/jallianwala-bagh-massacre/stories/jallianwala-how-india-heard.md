The first message reached Lahore at about a quarter past eleven on the night of 13 April 1919. The firing in Amritsar was only a few hours old. The message came from the Deputy Commissioner of Amritsar, and it was "much mutilated" on the way.

It was passed by telephone to the Chief Secretary of the Punjab government. He wrote down what he could make of it. The sense seemed to be that seven arrests had been made that day and that a banned meeting had been "dispersed". The army's General at Amritsar, he noted, had sent no report. The message ended with a single line: "Rumours heavy casualties in Amritsar to-day."

## Three in the morning

At about three o'clock on the morning of 14 April, two men arrived in a motor car from Amritsar. They carried a message from the District Magistrate. The meeting at the Jallianwala Bagh, it said, had been dispersed by force. About 200 people were dead.

The Lieutenant-Governor of Punjab, Sir Michael O'Dwyer, was told. He asked for more details from a general, who telephoned Dyer's report the next morning. O'Dwyer later told the committee that the general said he "fully approved" of what Dyer had done, and asked if he might pass on O'Dwyer's approval too. O'Dwyer agreed. "It was not for me to say that he had gone too far," he said, "when I was told by his superior officer that he fully approved."

## The wrong number

The figure of 200 was the number the public was given. Newspapers carried it the next morning. The Punjab government kept to it. When a senior official was asked by the committee later, he admitted the true figure might be higher.

It was much higher. The government counted about 379 dead. But it did so only later, after officials asked families to come forward.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia says the Hunter commission confirmed 337 men, 41 boys and a baby of six weeks among the dead. At the Imperial Legislative Council on 12 September 1919, Madan Mohan Malaviya said his inquiry had found 42 boys, the youngest only seven months old. This story says "about 40 boys".</aside>

## Why the numbers were so hard

Nobody had counted the dead on the day. Dyer had marched his men away at once. In July, officials asked people to come forward and tell them who had died. But the list was not complete. People feared they would be marked as having been at the meeting. Some of the dead may also have had no close family in the city.

The Congress held its own inquiry. It put the number of dead at about 1,000.

## Slow news

The British government tried to keep news of the firing from spreading. But it spread anyway, and Indians were outraged. Details did not become known in Britain until December 1919.

Meanwhile, in Punjab, martial law was in force. Gandhi wrote that leaders were arrested, and that special tribunals passed sentences. He asked the Viceroy for permission to go to Punjab. He was refused.

## Why it matters

For months, the people of India and Britain knew less than the facts. The first message was broken, and the first number was wrong. It took reports, debates and families coming forward before the story became clear.
