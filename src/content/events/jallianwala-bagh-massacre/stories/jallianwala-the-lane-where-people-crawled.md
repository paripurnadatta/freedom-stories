In Amritsar there was a street that was narrow but very long. Houses stood on both sides of it. The people who lived there used it to get food and other things they needed.

In April 1919, an English missionary teacher, Marcella Sherwood, was cycling through this street on her way to one of her schools. She ran schools for more than 600 girls. A mob chased her and beat her. They left her in the street because they thought she was dead.

Some Hindus picked her up and carried her to safety. The committee that later studied the riots wrote that this helped her to get medical help in time to save her life.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The Hunter report says Miss Sherwood was attacked on 10 April. Wikipedia says 11 April.</aside>

## The order

On 19 April, Dyer went to see Miss Sherwood, according to Wikipedia. Then he inspected the spot where she had fallen. He ordered a triangle to be put up there, a post for whipping people. He also posted two pickets of British soldiers, one at each end of the street. Their orders were that no Indian should pass between them, but that anyone who had to pass must go on all fours.

The guards stood there from six in the morning until eight at night.

Dyer later wrote in his report that he "never imagined that any sane man would voluntarily go through under those conditions". He said he was still looking for "some fitting punishment" when "Providence stepped in".

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The main part of the Hunter report says the order was given on 10 April. That cannot be right, because that was the day Miss Sherwood was attacked. The Indian members' section says 19 April, and so does Wikipedia. This story uses 19 April. The report says the order lasted until 26 April.</aside>

## Who had to crawl

Dyer thought that every house on the street had a back door. When he was told that many did not, he said the families could go over their roofs. The three Indian members of the committee could not see how families could cross from roof to roof. The houses were all different heights. Dyer also said he did not think it was "a very great inconvenience".

Within a few minutes of the order, about a dozen men were arrested nearby for what Dyer called "insolent" behaviour. The police took them along the street, and the guard on duty made them crawl for about 150 yards.

The next day, six men were taken to the whipping post. They were under arrest for the attack on Miss Sherwood, and they had not yet been tried. Later they were found guilty of it. On their way to the post and back, they too were made to crawl.

Altogether, about 50 people crawled along the street.

## What the committee said

The committee said the order "caused unnecessary inconvenience to a number of people". It said it "unnecessarily punished innocent as well as guilty". And, it wrote, "in subjecting the Indian population to an act of humiliation, it has continued to be a cause of bitterness and racial ill-feeling long after it was recalled".

The Punjab government did not approve of the order, and it was withdrawn on 26 April.

## Gandhi's view

Gandhi wrote about this in his autobiography. He wrote and telegraphed to the Viceroy to ask for permission to go to Punjab. He was refused.

Of the crawling, he wrote: "Before this procedure the Jallianwala Bagh tragedy paled into insignificance in my eyes." For Gandhi, being made to crawl hurt more than the massacre that drew the whole world's attention.

<aside class="note"><b>Another voice</b>: Miss Sherwood herself later took Dyer's side. A letter from her was read in the House of Commons. She wrote that she was sure there had been a real rebellion and that Dyer had saved India.</aside>

## Why it matters

The order lasted about a week. But the committee wrote that it remained a cause of bitterness long after it was withdrawn. The committee noted that nobody said the people who lived in that street had attacked Miss Sherwood. They were still the ones who had to find a way past the guards.
