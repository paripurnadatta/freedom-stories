Sunday, 13 April 1919, was Baisakhi, a spring festival. Amritsar was full of visitors. Farmers, traders and pilgrims had come for the festival and for a cattle fair. Some had been praying at the Golden Temple, which is close to the Bagh.

The city was also tense. Three days earlier, a crowd had gone to the Deputy Commissioner's house to ask for two leaders to be freed. They were Dr Saifuddin Kitchlew and Dr Satyapal, and both had been arrested. Soldiers fired on the crowd. After that, angry mobs burned banks and killed several Europeans. A woman missionary, Marcella Sherwood, was beaten and left in the street.

Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer was the army commander in Amritsar. He was the man who would decide what happened next.

## The proclamation

That Sunday morning, Dyer went through Amritsar with officials and a drummer. At many places, a man read out a proclamation. No one could leave the city without a pass. No one could be out after 8 at night. No procession was allowed. And any gathering of four men "would be looked upon and treated as an unlawful assembly and dispersed by force of arms if necessary".

The proclamation was not read everywhere. Important parts of the city were missed. The Indian members of the Hunter Committee, which later looked into all this, said only 8,000 to 10,000 people could have heard it. The city had about 160,000. Some who did hear it thought it was only a threat and that the General would not fire.

Only the evening before, a meeting had been announced for the Jallianwala Bagh. While Dyer's proclamation was being read, a counter-announcement said the meeting would go ahead. It was to begin at about half past four in the afternoon.

<aside class="note"><b>Proclamation</b>: an official public announcement, read aloud so that everyone knows the rule.</aside>

## Dyer's plan

Early in the afternoon, Dyer heard about the meeting. He did not move at once. Later he told the committee that he had thought he had "done enough to make the crowd not meet". If they met anyway, he said, he had made up his mind to fire.

At about four o'clock he had firm news that the meeting was under way. He left with a force. It had 25 Gurkha and 25 Baluchi soldiers with rifles, 40 more Gurkhas with kukris, and two armoured cars.

## The garden

The Bagh was not really a garden. It was a rectangular piece of open ground, covered in places with building material and rubbish. It was almost shut in by the walls of houses. It had five narrow entrances. People often held big meetings there.

The armoured cars could not fit through the gate. They stayed in the street. The Indian members of the committee said Dyer had never seen the place before, and did not know the cars could not get in.

Dyer walked in with his soldiers. At the far end, a crowd sat on the ground. A man was speaking from a small raised platform, about a hundred yards away. The Hunter Committee found no evidence of what he was saying. It also found that nobody in the crowd had a firearm. Some may have carried sticks.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Dyer reported the crowd at about 6,000. The Hunter Committee's majority thought 10,000 to 20,000 people were there. The Indian members said 15,000 to 20,000.</aside>

## Ten minutes

Dyer put 25 soldiers on the high ground on each side of the entrance. He did not tell the crowd to leave. Then he ordered his men to fire.

The crowd began to run as soon as the firing began. They ran for the few exits. The soldiers kept firing for about ten minutes, one shot at a time and not in volleys. Dyer later agreed that he had turned the fire to the places where the crowd was thickest.

In all, 1,650 rounds were fired. Dyer told the committee that if the armoured cars had got in, he would probably have used their machine guns too.

Winston Churchill later described the scene to the House of Commons. The people were "pinned up in a narrow place", he said, with "hardly any exits". When fire was aimed at the centre, they ran to the sides, and the fire turned to the sides. It stopped, he said, only when the ammunition was nearly used up.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Dyer said he kept firing until his ammunition ran short. Churchill said enough bullets were kept back for the troops' safe return. Wikipedia says the troops had fired about a third of their ammunition when the order to stop came.</aside>

Then the soldiers turned and marched out. Dyer took them straight back to their camp outside the city.

## The night

Dyer had set a curfew earlier than usual that day. This meant the wounded could not easily be moved from where they lay. Many died during the night, according to Wikipedia.

Some people died in the crush at the narrow gates. Some, trying to get away, fell into the well in the middle of the Bagh. A plaque put up at the site after independence says 120 bodies were taken out of that well.

## How many?

At first, officials thought about 200 had died, and that is the number the newspapers carried. Later, the government counted about 379 dead. About 87 of them were strangers or villagers who had come in for the fair. The wounded were thought to be about three times as many.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The government's figure of 379 is the one in the Hunter report. The historian Vishwa Nath Datta thought about 700 had died. Madan Mohan Malaviya and the Congress inquiry put the number near 1,000. Many of the families had been afraid to come forward, so the true number is not known.</aside>

## What stands there now

The Bagh is a memorial today. Bullet marks can still be seen on its walls. The memorial was opened on 13 April 1961 by President Rajendra Prasad.

## Why it matters

The committee agreed that the crowd had met against Dyer's order. Many had thought his threat was empty. But some had never heard the order at all. Churchill said the crowd was "not attacking anybody or anything".

The soldiers fired on people who were trying to get out through a few narrow doors. After that day, many Indians who had stayed loyal to British rule turned against it.
