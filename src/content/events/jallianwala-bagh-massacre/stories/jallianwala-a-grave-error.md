In October 1919, six months after the firing in Amritsar, the Government of India set up a committee. Its job was to look into the disturbances in Punjab, Delhi and Bombay that spring. It was led by Lord Hunter, a Scottish judge.

The committee had eight members. Three of them were Indian: Pandit Jagat Narayan, Sir Chimanlal Setalvad and Sardar Sultan Ahmed Khan.

Most witnesses were heard in public. The committee sat for eight days in Delhi, twenty-nine days in Lahore, six in Ahmedabad and three in Bombay. One of the people who came to answer its questions was General Dyer.

<aside class="note"><b>Congress stayed away</b>: The Congress refused to appear in Lahore while the Punjab leaders were still in jail. It held an inquiry of its own. When the leaders were freed, Pandit Malaviya wrote to say his committee could now bring witnesses. Lord Hunter said no, because the Lahore hearings were over.</aside>

## What Dyer said

Dyer was ordered to appear on 19 November 1919. His superiors had suggested he bring a lawyer, but he came alone. The record of his evidence is printed in the Indian members' report. He was asked why he had fired without telling the crowd to go home. He said it had not occurred to him. "I merely felt that my orders had not been obeyed," he said, "and that it was my duty to immediately disperse it by rifle fire."

He was asked whether he had wanted to frighten people far beyond Amritsar. "Call it what you like," he answered. "I was going to punish them. My idea from the military point of view was to make a wide impression." He said he wanted "to reduce their morale; the morale of the rebels".

He was asked whether he could have broken up the crowd without firing. "I think it quite possible that I could have dispersed them perhaps even without firing," he said. But they would "all come back and laugh at me, and I considered I would be making myself a fool".

And what about the wounded? Had he done anything for them? "No, certainly not," he said. "It was not my job." The hospitals were open, he said. "The wounded only had to apply for help." They did not, he added, because they would then have been taken into custody for being at the meeting.

He also wrote a report in August 1919. In it, he said his firing was the least that would produce "the necessary moral and widespread effect". He wanted that effect, he wrote, "not only on those who were present, but more specially throughout the Punjab".

## What the committee found

The report came in two parts. Five members signed the main report, and the three Indian members wrote their own. The main report made two criticisms. Dyer should have warned the crowd to leave. And he fired for too long.

They wrote that a warning "should have been given", because people who had not heard the proclamation would then have had a chance to go. But they thought the crowd would probably not have left, and that firing would still have been needed.

On the length of the firing they were plain. "In continuing to fire for so long as he did it appears to us that General Dyer committed a grave error."

They also said Dyer had been wrong about his duty. A crowd that gathers against a proclamation may have to be fired on. But, they wrote, "continued firing upon that crowd cannot be justified because of the effect such firing may have upon people in other places."

They were fair to Dyer in some ways. They said he spoke frankly and honestly believed he was doing his duty. They noted that he had heard reports of cut telegraph wires and attacks on railway stations. And they did not accept that he had saved India from a rebellion like the Mutiny of 1857. It had not been proved, they said, that a plot to overthrow British rule had been made before the riots.

## What the three Indian members added

The separate report was harder on Dyer.

They said the proclamation had not reached large parts of the city. They said he had arrived at the Bagh with his mind already made up. They said innocent people had been in the crowd, including villagers who had come for the fair. No violence had taken place in the Bagh before the firing.

They also said Dyer left the place at once and did nothing for the dead or the wounded. He should have sent word to someone, they wrote, even after he reached his camp. And they wrote that his method was "inhuman and un-British". It had, they said, done great harm to British rule in India.

## No trial

Lord Hunter sent the report to the Government of India on 8 March 1920. It did not punish Dyer.

He was never tried. Churchill, who was then Secretary of State for War, told Parliament what had happened to him instead. Dyer had been removed from his post in India, passed over for promotion, and told there was no chance of more work. This meant he went on half pay. Churchill said that a line of Dyer's superiors had approved of what he did at the time. That was "virtual condonation", he said, and it made harsher action impossible.

## The Commons

On 8 July 1920, the House of Commons spent an afternoon on the case.

Edwin Montagu, the Secretary of State for India, spoke of "the theory of terrorism". Churchill called what happened "an extraordinary event, a monstrous event, an event which stands in singular and sinister isolation". He also said: "Frightfulness is not a remedy known to the British pharmacopœia."

But Dyer had defenders. Sir Edward Carson argued that to ruin an officer who had been approved by his superiors at the time was "un-English". And an English missionary's letter was read aloud. It was from Marcella Sherwood, the teacher who had been beaten in Amritsar. She wrote that she was sure there had been "a real rebellion" and that Dyer "saved India and us from a repetition of the miseries and cruelties of 1857". Five other English missionary women, she said, had asked her to beg the House "not to do this great wrong to General Dyer".

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia says the Commons voted 247 to 37 against Dyer. The Hansard record shows two votes that evening. A Labour member's motion to cut a government item by £100 was lost by 37 votes to 247. A similar motion by Carson, who defended Dyer, was lost by 129 votes to 230. In both, Churchill and Montagu were on the winning side.</aside>

## Why it matters

The committee did blame Dyer, and Parliament did argue about him in public. That is not nothing. But the men who judged him were part of the same government. It set the limit of his punishment, and it did not go far.

Dyer lived until 1927. For many Indians, what stayed in mind was that a firing on an unarmed crowd had ended in a lost job and half pay.
