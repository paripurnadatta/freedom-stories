Today the Jallianwala Bagh is a memorial. Its brick walls still show marks made by bullets, and visitors can see them. The walls have been kept as they were.

## The well

In the middle of the Bagh there is a well. On the day of the firing, some people jumped into it to escape. A plaque put up after independence says 120 bodies were taken out of it. The well is now a protected monument inside the park. It is called the Martyrs' Well.

## Building the memorial

A trust was founded in 1920 to build a memorial on the site, after the Indian National Congress passed a resolution. In 1923, the trust bought the land. The memorial was designed by an American architect, Benjamin Polk.

It was opened on 13 April 1961 by the President of India, Rajendra Prasad. Jawaharlal Nehru and other leaders were there. A flame was added later.

## Money

In 1922, the British government settled more than 700 claims from Indian families. The total paid to them, Wikipedia says, was less than half of what went to about a dozen European individuals.

## What was said

At the time, the Government of India itself spoke of regret. Sir William Vincent, the Home Member of the Viceroy's Council, spoke in a debate on the Punjab disturbances. He called the use of "overdrastic and severe action" and "excessive use of force" wrong. He said that acts which could be read as "designed to humiliate Indian people" were "unpardonable" and "morally indefensible".

That was the Government of India speaking. It was not an apology from Britain. Many Indians have asked for one ever since.

In 1997, Queen Elizabeth II spoke at a state banquet in India. Jallianwala Bagh, she said, was "a distressing example" of the "difficult episodes in our past". The next day she visited the Bagh and stood in silence for half a minute. Some welcomed her words. Others said they fell short of an apology.

In February 2013, David Cameron became the first British prime minister in office to visit the Bagh. He called the massacre "a deeply shameful event in British history". He did not apologise. He said he did not think the right thing was "to reach back into history and to seek out things that we should apologise for".

In April 2019, a hundred years on, Prime Minister Theresa May told Parliament, "We deeply regret what happened and the suffering caused." She called it "a shameful scar on British Indian history". She did not offer a formal apology either. The Leader of the Opposition, Jeremy Corbyn, said that "a full, clear and unequivocal apology" was needed.

<aside class="note"><b>Written in 2019</b>: These are the statements made up to the hundredth year. None of them was a formal apology.</aside>

## Someone who did not forget

Udham Singh, who Wikipedia says was wounded in Amritsar that day, shot and killed Sir Michael O'Dwyer in London on 13 March 1940. O'Dwyer had been the Lieutenant-Governor of Punjab in 1919. At his trial, Singh said O'Dwyer "wanted to crush the spirit of my people". He was hanged on 31 July 1940. His own story is told on his page.

## Why it matters

The wall, the well and the memorial are there so that the day is not forgotten. The questions that people ask there are still asked. Who was to blame? How many died? And who has said sorry?
