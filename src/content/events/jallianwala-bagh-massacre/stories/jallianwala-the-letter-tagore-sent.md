Rabindranath Tagore was a poet, a writer and a teacher. In 1913 he won the Nobel Prize in Literature. He gave the whole prize money, £8,000, to the school he had started at Bolpur in Bengal. It was called Shantiniketan, which means "abode of peace".

Two years later, in 1915, he accepted a knighthood from the British Crown.

<aside class="note"><b>Knighthood</b>: a title given by the King to honour a person. A man who is knighted is called "Sir". It is not a prize or a job. It is a public honour from the government.</aside>

## The decision

By 22 May 1919, Tagore had heard what had happened in Punjab. He tried to arrange a protest meeting in Calcutta. Then he decided on a different step. He would give back his knighthood, as a "symbolic act of protest".

## The letter

On 31 May 1919, he wrote to the Viceroy, Lord Chelmsford.

He wrote that the way the government had dealt with what it called "local disturbances" had given a "rude shock". It had shown Indians "the helplessness of our position as British subjects in India".

Then he wrote the sentence people still remember. "The time has come," he wrote, "when badges of honour make our shame glaring in their incongruous context of humiliation." He went on: "I for my part wish to stand, shorn of all special distinctions, by the side of my country men."

He thought of ordinary people who were not famous. They were, he wrote, "liable to suffer degradation not fit for human beings". He wanted to stand beside them.

He also wrote that the very least he could do was to speak for "the millions of my countrymen, surprised into a dumb anguish of terror". He would take "all consequences" upon himself for doing so.

## A rare step

A writer for the arts group Iniva says Tagore was not the first person to hand back a knighthood. But he may have been the first from Asia. Few people have given back an honour as a protest against one particular event.

Tagore kept his school and his work. What he gave up was a title from the government that had done this.

## He was not a follower of Gandhi

It would be wrong to think Tagore agreed with every leader of the freedom struggle. He did not.

At the end of 1919, he wrote a letter that was printed in the Indian press. The bulk of English people, he said, could never be in a normal state of mind towards Indians. The reason was "our situation being unnatural". He thought it best for Indians to "do our own work quietly in our own surroundings".

In June 1921, he wrote to the head of his school about Gandhi's campaign of non-cooperation. He called it "perverted nationalism, which was making of India a prison".

<aside class="note"><b>Non-cooperation</b>: Gandhi's campaign of refusing to cooperate with British rule.</aside>

## Why it matters

Tagore and Gandhi did not agree about everything. But in 1919 a poet who had been honoured by the Crown wrote to the Viceroy and gave the honour back. He said, in his own words, that he wished to stand with the people who had been hurt.
