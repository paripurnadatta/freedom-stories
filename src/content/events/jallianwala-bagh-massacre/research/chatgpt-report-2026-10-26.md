# Production research: jallianwala-bagh-massacre

Prepared 26 October 2026. This is a writing pack for Claude, not a set of finished stories.
ChatGPT did the research; Claude writes the stories. No story has been written by ChatGPT, and
nothing here is approved for `main`.

Six angles are planned: two flagship and four short. Every fact below has a source tag that is
resolved in the list of opened sources at the end. Where only one source supports a fact, the
tag carries a `(single)` warning. Quotations are given exactly as they appear in the opened page,
or marked UNVERIFIED.

Sizes: flagship means 900+ words of the writer's own prose; short means 350+.

---

## 1. The garden with five doors — FLAGSHIP

The day itself: the proclamation, the crowd, the firing, the wall and the well.

Facts, in the order a story would need them:

1. The date was Sunday, 13 April 1919, in Amritsar, Punjab. It was Baisakhi, the Punjabi harvest
   festival, and there was also a cattle fair in the city. [H, ch. III, para 35 and Minority
   Report, ch. IV, para 2; T]
2. On the morning of that day Dyer went through the city with a drummer and a town crier and read
   a proclamation. A column of soldiers and two armoured cars went with him. The Tribune account
   says the procession halted at 19 points. [T; H, ch. III, para 35]
3. The proclamation forbade processions and any gathering of four men, and said such a gathering
   would be "dispersed by force of arms if necessary". [H, Minority Report, ch. IV, para 2, quoting
   the proclamation; H, ch. III, para 39]
4. The proclamation was read and explained in the vernacular, and printed Urdu copies were
   distributed. [H, Minority Report, ch. IV, para 2]
5. It was not widely heard. The minority report says it reached "8 to 10,000 people" in a city of
   "160,000 to 170,000", and that important parts of the town were left out of the route. [H,
   Minority Report, ch. IV, para 2]
6. A meeting had been announced the day before, on 12 April, in the compound of the Hindu Sabha
   School. It was announced for the Jallianwala Bagh on 13 April. [H, ch. III, para 35, quoting
   the evidence]
7. The meeting was called for about 4.30 p.m. [H, ch. III, para 36; H, Minority Report, ch. IV,
   para 2]
8. Dyer heard about the meeting at about 1 o'clock, or at 12.40 p.m. according to the minority
   report. [H, ch. III, para 36; H, Minority Report, ch. IV, para 2] Both figures are in the same
   report; a story should say "early in the afternoon" and not build on the exact minute.
9. He asked why he had not prevented the meeting, and said he had to organise his forces and make
   up his mind where to put his pickets. [H, ch. III, para 36, quoting his evidence]
10. At about 4 o'clock he received definite information that the meeting was being held. [H,
    ch. III, para 37]
11. He went with "a special force of 25 Gurkhas and 25 Baluchis armed with rifles, 40 Gurkhas
    armed only with kukris, and two armoured cars". [H, ch. III, para 37]
12. He entered by a narrow entrance that the armoured cars could not pass, so the cars stayed in
    the street outside. [H, ch. III, para 37]
13. The Bagh was "a rectangular piece of unused ground, covered to some extent by building
    material and débris", almost surrounded by the walls of buildings; "the entrances and exits to
    it are few and imperfect". [H, ch. III, para 37]
14. The crowd was at the far end, listening to a man on a raised platform about 100 yards away.
    Dyer reported the crowd as about 6,000. The report says it was probably much more numerous,
    "from 10 to 20,000". The minority report says 15,000 to 20,000, while Dyer believed 5,000 or
    6,000 at the time. [H, ch. III, para 37; H, Minority Report, ch. IV, para 2]
15. "There is no evidence as to the nature of the address to which the audience was listening.
    None of them were provided with firearms, although some of them may have been carrying
    sticks." [H, ch. III, para 38]
16. Dyer placed 25 men on each side of the raised ground at the entrance, and "without giving the
    crowd any warning to disperse" ordered them to fire. [H, ch. III, para 38]
17. The firing lasted about 10 minutes and was individual fire, not volleys. "In all 1,650 rounds
    were fired by the troops." [H, ch. III, para 38]
18. "As soon as firing commenced the crowd began to disperse." [H, ch. III, para 38]
19. Dyer immediately marched his troops back to the Ram Bagh, so no accurate count was made at the
    time. At first about 200 dead was assumed. The later government investigation, using a list
    compiled by the Sewa Samiti, found "approximately 379 people were killed", of whom about 87
    were strangers or villagers from the surrounding district. The wounded were "probably three
    times" that number. [H, ch. III, para 38]
20. The minority report puts it as "at least 379 killed and about 1,200 wounded". [H, Minority
    Report, ch. IV, para 2]
21. Dyer did not arrange help for the wounded. Asked about it, he said the hospitals were open and
    "The wounded only had to apply for help." [H, ch. III, para 41]
22. The next morning a deputation asked permission to cremate or bury the dead, and it was
    granted. Four or five bodies were cremated on each pyre, and no count was kept. [T]
23. Curfew: Dyer imposed a curfew earlier than usual, so the wounded could not be moved and many
    died during the night. [W, citing the Hunter report volumes] The Tribune describes the curfew
    as beginning at 8 p.m. and says nobody dared move after it. [T]
24. Churchill, in the Commons on 8 July 1920, described the scene from the evidence: the crowd was
    unarmed, "Pinned up in a narrow place considerably smaller than Trafalgar Square"; when fire
    was directed at the centre the people ran to the sides, and the fire was directed at the sides.
    "This was continued for 8 or 10 minutes, and it stopped only when the ammunition had reached
    the point of exhaustion." [HH]

The well: a plaque placed at the site after independence says that 120 bodies were removed from
the well. [W] This is a single source. The Hunter report, which is the fullest contemporary
account, does not mention the well. A story may say "a plaque at the site says" and must not
present the figure as a counted fact.

Two versions to carry in the note: 379 (official, Sewa Samiti, Hunter majority and minority) against
about 700 (Vishwa Nath Datta) and about 1,000 (Malaviya and the Congress inquiry). [W] Also note
that the government itself first said about 200. [H, ch. III, para 38; T]

Safe "quiet line" material: the Bagh is still in Amritsar, the walls still carry bullet marks, and
the memorial was opened on 13 April 1961 by Rajendra Prasad. [W]

## 2. A grave error — FLAGSHIP

The inquiry: what it heard, what it decided, and the fact that nobody was ever tried.

Facts, in order:

1. The Government of India appointed the committee by resolution dated Simla, 14 October 1919, to
   investigate the disturbances in Bombay, Delhi and the Punjab, their causes and the measures
   taken. [H, front matter, Resolution No. 2168]
2. Lord Hunter presided. Members: Mr Justice G. C. Rankin, W. F. Rice, Major-General Sir George
   Barrow, Pandit Jagat Narayan, Thomas Smith, Sir Chimanlal Harilal Setalvad and Sardar Sahibzada
   Sultan Ahmad Khan. [H, Resolution]
3. The committee sat in public except for four witnesses. It heard evidence on 8 days at Delhi, 29
   at Lahore, 6 at Ahmedabad and 3 at Bombay. [H, Hunter's letter to the Government of India,
   dated 8 March 1920]
4. The All-India Congress Committee refused to appear. Its resolution said that, while the Punjab
   leaders stayed in prison, it could not co-operate by tendering evidence. [H, Hunter's letter]
5. On 30 December Malaviya telegraphed asking for the inquiry to be reopened once the leaders were
   released. Hunter refused, and the committee approved his refusal. [H, Hunter's letter]
6. Congress ran its own inquiry instead. Its report came out on 20 February 1920, before the
   Hunter report, and was severe. [T]
7. The Hunter report was sent on 8 March 1920. [H, Hunter's letter, dated Agra, 8 March 1920]
8. The majority report criticised Dyer on two grounds only: firing without giving the people a
   chance to disperse, and continuing to fire after the crowd had begun to disperse. [H, ch. III,
   para 39]
9. On the first: "The majority, at all events, of the people who assembled had gone so in direct
   defiance of a proclamation issued in the interests of peace and order, many thinking that the
   reference to firing was mere bluff." Even so, notice should have been given, because many had
   not heard the proclamation. [H, ch. III, para 39]
10. On the second, the sentence to quote: "In continuing to fire for so long as he did it appears
    to us that General Dyer committed a grave error." [H, ch. III, para 40]
11. Dyer's own explanation, quoted in the report: he fired to produce "the necessary moral and
    widespread effect", not only on those present "but more especially throughout the Punjab".
    [H, ch. III, para 40, quoting his report of 25 August 1919]
12. The majority's judgement on that: "In our view this was unfortunately a mistaken conception of
    his duty." [H, ch. III, para 40]
13. The majority also found that there had been no conspiracy to overthrow British rule, and no
    rebellion in the Punjab of the kind Dyer described. [H, ch. X, para 9 (minority numbering in
    the OCR): "On the evidence before us we are of opinion that there was no rebellion"] and [H,
    Minority Report]
14. The minority report, by the Indian members, was harsher. It found that the proclamation was
    "insufficiently promulgated", that innocent people were in the crowd, that there had been no
    violence in the Bagh beforehand, and that Dyer should have helped the wounded or ordered the
    civil authorities to do so. [H, Minority Report, ch. IV]
15. The minority report also records that Dyer intended to use machine guns and could not get the
    armoured cars in, and that he had four hours to think after hearing of the meeting. [H,
    Minority Report, ch. IV, para 2]
16. Dyer's answer when asked whether he could have dispersed the crowd without firing, and whether
    the crowd would have laughed at him, is quoted in the Wikipedia article. It is UNVERIFIED
    against the original minutes: "I think it quite possible that I could have dispersed the crowd
    without firing but they would have come back again and laughed, and I would have made, what I
    consider, a fool of myself." [W] Do not use this as a quotation in a story without checking
    the minutes; paraphrase it and attribute it to his evidence.
17. No penal action followed. The report says his action had been condoned by his superiors. [W,
    citing the report volumes and the Army Council] The Viceroy's Council decided prosecution was
    not possible for political reasons. [W]
18. He was removed from his appointment and told there was no prospect of further employment. He
    reverted to half pay. [HH, Churchill's statement]
19. Hansard, 8 July 1920: Montagu opened for the Government and called Dyer's reasoning "the
    doctrine of terrorism". [HH]
20. Churchill: "one tremendous fact stands out—I mean the slaughter of nearly 400 persons and the
    wounding of probably three or four times as many, at the Jallian Wallah Bagh on 13th April.
    That is an episode which appears to me to be without precedent or parallel in the modern
    history of the British Empire." He called it "a monstrous event". [HH]
21. Sir Edward Carson defended Dyer: the officer had won approval at the time from his divisional
    commander and the Lieutenant-Governor, and was afterwards broken. He said to break a man in
    those circumstances was "un-English". [HH]
22. The Commons divided: Ayes 37, Noes 247. The Government won. [HH]
23. A London newspaper, the Morning Post, opened a fund for Dyer and the money saved him from
    penury. [T] The sum often quoted (£26,000) is not in any page opened for this report; treat it
    as UNVERIFIED and leave it out unless a source is found.
24. Dyer died on 23 July 1927. [T]

Fairness notes for the writer: the same report that censured Dyer also recorded that the crowd was
breaking a proclamation, that some in it carried sticks, and that a small force feared being
overwhelmed. Churchill himself said the civil authorities had failed and that Dyer had been left
without their advice. [HH] A fair story gives the defence room, then gives the finding.

## 3. The lane where people had to crawl — SHORT

1. On 10 April 1919 Marcella Sherwood, a missionary teacher, was cycling in Amritsar when she was
   attacked and left for dead in a street of the city. [H, ch. XI, para 18; T]
2. The Hunter report calls the attackers' offence "dastardly" and says they deserved the severest
   punishment. [H, ch. XI, para 18]
3. Some days later Dyer put up a triangle, or whipping post, at the place where she fell, intending
   that those guilty of the assault should be publicly flogged there. [H, ch. XI, para 18]
4. He placed two pickets in the street with orders that no Indian was to pass between them, but
   that if they had to pass they must go on all fours. [H, ch. XI, para 18]
5. Dating problem, and this is a proper "Two versions" note. The Hunter report says the order was
   "issued on the 10th April, i.e., 9 days after the assault", which cannot be right: 10 April was
   the day of the assault. The Tribune says Dyer saw the injured Sherwood on 19 April and then
   ordered the triangle and the crawling rule, and that it stayed in force until 24 April. The
   Hunter report says it was withdrawn on 26 April on the instructions of the Punjab Government,
   who disapproved of it. [H, ch. XI, para 18; T] The most likely date is 19 April. A story should
   give 19 April and note the disagreement.
6. About 50 people crawled. The report's OCR reads "5V", which is plainly 50 in context. [H,
   ch. XI, para 18]
7. Six men were sentenced to be flogged for a breach of fort discipline, taken to the post, flogged,
   and made to crawl on the way there and back. They were afterwards convicted of the offence
   against Sherwood. [H, ch. XI, para 18]
8. Other people in the street had to crawl simply to reach shops and houses. Dyer thought every
   house had a back entrance; he was wrong. [H, ch. XI, para 18]
9. The report's verdict, to quote: the order "caused unnecessary inconvenience to a number of
   people, and that it unnecessarily punished innocent as well as guilty. Above all ... in
   subjecting the Indian population to an act of humiliation, it has continued to be a cause of
   bitterness and racial ill-feeling long after it was recalled." [H, ch. XI, para 18]
10. A separate order in Gujranwala, the "salaaming order" of 22 April 1919, required Indians to
    salaam European officers. The report calls it open to similar objection. [H, ch. XI, para 19]
    Keep the two orders apart; they were in different districts and by different officers.
11. Gandhi, writing later, put the crawling above the massacre itself: "In Amritsar innocent men
    and women were made to crawl like worms on their bellies. Before this procedure the Jallianwala
    Bagh tragedy paled into insignificance in my eyes, though it was this massacre principally that
    attracted the attention of the people of India, and of the world." [G]
12. The same passage records that Gandhi asked the Viceroy for permission to go to the Punjab and
    was refused. [G]

Care with the assault: say she was attacked and badly hurt. No detail. The story is about the
order, not the attack.

## 4. The letter Tagore sent — SHORT

1. Rabindranath Tagore won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1913. [EB]
2. He accepted a knighthood in 1915, and so was "Sir Rabindranath". [EB] The 1922 Britannica entry
   says "accepted a knighthood in 1915". Some popular accounts say 1915, some say the honour was
   announced in 1915; the entry is the source used here.
3. In 1919 he "addressed a letter to the Viceroy in 1919, resigning the title as a protest against
   the methods adopted for the repression of disturbances in the Punjab". [EB]
4. The letter is dated 31 May 1919 and was addressed to Lord Chelmsford, the Viceroy. [W]
5. The line most often quoted from it: "I ... wish to stand, shorn, of all special distinctions, by
   the side of those of my countrymen who, for their so called insignificance, are liable to suffer
   degradation not fit for human beings." [W, quoting the letter] Single source for the wording;
   treat the quotation as coming through Wikipedia until the letter itself is seen.
6. Wider passage quoted in the same article: "The enormity of the measures taken by the Government
   in Punjab for quelling some local disturbances has, with a rude shock, revealed to our minds the
   helplessness of our position as British subjects in India..." [W]
7. Tagore had received the news by 22 May 1919, tried to arrange a protest meeting in Calcutta, and
   then decided on the renunciation as "a symbolic act of protest". [W] Single source.
8. Fairness: Tagore was not a follower of Gandhi's programme at this time. The same Britannica entry
   records that he later condemned non-cooperation as "perverted nationalism, which was making of
   India a prison". [EB] A story should not turn him into a Congress man.
9. The title he gave up was a British honour, not a prize and not a job. Explain that plainly for a
   child: a knight is addressed as "Sir"; giving it back was a public way of saying he would not be
   honoured by the government that did this.

## 5. How India heard — SHORT

1. The first message to reach Lahore came at about 11 or 11.15 on the night of 13 April and was
   "much mutilated". The text as telephoned to the Chief Secretary read: "Sense seems to be—seven
   arrests were made to day and a prohibited meeting dispersed ... Rumours heavy casualties in
   Amritsar to-day." [H, ch. III, para 42]
2. About 3 a.m. on 14 April two men arrived by car with a message from the District Magistrate: the
   meeting had been dispersed by force and the deaths were about 200. [H, ch. III, para 42]
3. The Punjab Government's first public figure was about 200, and the newspapers on the following
   morning carried the same figure. [H, ch. III, para 38; W]
4. The press had already been gagged. Some newspapers were forced to stop publication. [T]
5. The Tribune's editor, Kalinath Ray, was arrested, tried and convicted for "seditious writings",
   and the paper had to suspend publication. Manohar Lal, vice-president of the High Court Bar
   Association, was arrested because he was a trustee of The Tribune. [T]
6. Amritsar was sealed. Martial law was established in the district with effect from 15 April. [H,
   ch. X, quoting the Punjab Government's own statement; T] Passes were needed to enter or leave.
   [W]
7. Gandhi asked the Viceroy for permission to go to the Punjab and was refused. [G]
8. Details did not reach Britain until December 1919. [W]
9. On 12 September 1919, at the Imperial Legislative Council, the inquiry led by Madan Mohan
   Malaviya reported that 42 of the dead were boys, the youngest seven months old. [W] Single
   source. The Hunter report gives 337 men, 41 boys and a six-week-old baby. [W, citing the report]
   These two counts are close but not identical; a story should use "about 40 boys" and cite the
   commission, not the exact ages.
10. Congress published its own report on 20 February 1920. [T]
11. The Hunter report followed on 8 March 1920 and was published in May. [H; T]
12. The Commons debated it on 8 July 1920 and voted 247 to 37 for the Government. [HH]

Note for the writer: this angle is about rumour, censorship and slow truth, so the mutilated
telegram is the best scene-setter in the whole pack. It is a primary quotation and it can carry an
opening paragraph.

## 6. What the wall remembers — SHORT

1. A plaque at the site, placed after independence, says 120 bodies were taken from the well. [W]
2. A trust to build a memorial was founded in 1920 after a Congress resolution; it bought land in
   1923. The memorial was designed by the American architect Benjamin Polk. [W]
3. President Rajendra Prasad inaugurated it on 13 April 1961, with Jawaharlal Nehru present. A
   flame was added later. [W]
4. The walls with bullet marks and the Martyrs' Well are preserved at the site. [W]
5. Britain has never formally apologised. [W; B]
6. In 1997 Queen Elizabeth II spoke of the events at a state banquet in India and expressed regret
   and sadness; some Indians welcomed it, others said it fell short of an apology. [W]
7. In February 2013 David Cameron became the first serving British prime minister to visit the
   site; he laid a wreath, called it "a deeply shameful event", and did not apologise. [W; B]
8. On 12 April 2019, before the centenary, Theresa May told the Commons: "We deeply regret what
   happened and the suffering caused." She called the massacre "a shameful scar on British Indian
   history" and again stopped short of an apology. [B; W]
9. Udham Singh, who said he was in the Bagh that day, shot and killed Sir Michael O'Dwyer in
   London on 13 March 1940 and was hanged on 31 July 1940. [W; T] His presence in the Bagh is his
   own account; no document proves it. Handle as a "Two versions" note.

## Deliberately left out

- The Gujranwala riots of 15 April, the floggings, the schoolboys' punishments, the aeroplane
  bombing of a village and the Gujranwala "salaam" order. All are in [T] and [H]. They belong to a
  different subject (martial law in the Punjab) or to a later batch. Putting them into these six
  would turn a story about a garden into a catalogue of punishments.
- The compensation figures. [W] They are real and unfair, but they need their own sources and their
  own story.
- The number of rounds per person, the exact number of soldiers inside the Bagh beyond "about 50",
  and any attempt to name individual victims. The Dictionary of Martyrs lists names, but that PDF
  was not opened for this report and must not be used from memory.
- Any quotation from Dyer at the inquiry beyond the two in the Hunter report itself, until the
  minutes are checked.
