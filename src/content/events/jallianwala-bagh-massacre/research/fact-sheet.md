# Fact sheet: jallianwala-bagh-massacre

tag is `chatgpt-report-2026-10-26.md`. Source keys are at the end. If a fact is not here and not in

## 1. The garden with five doors — FLAGSHIP

- Sunday 13 April 1919, Amritsar. Baisakhi, and a cattle fair in the city. [H III.35; T]
- That morning Dyer read a proclamation through the city with a drummer and crier, soldiers and two
  armoured cars. It forbade processions and any gathering of four men, and said such a gathering
  would be "dispersed by force of arms if necessary". [H III.35; H Min. IV.2]
- It was heard by "8 to 10,000 people" in a city of "160,000 to 170,000". [H Min. IV.2]
- A meeting was announced on 12 April for the Bagh, called for about 4.30 p.m. on the 13th.
  [H III.35–36]
- Dyer heard of it early in the afternoon (1 p.m., or 12.40 p.m. in the minority report — say "early
  in the afternoon"). [H III.36; H Min. IV.2]
- He came with 25 Gurkhas and 25 Baluchis with rifles, 40 Gurkhas with kukris, two armoured cars.
  The cars stayed in the street; the entrance was too narrow. [H III.37]
- The Bagh: "a rectangular piece of unused ground, covered to some extent by building material and
  débris", walled, "the entrances and exits to it are few and imperfect". [H III.37]
- Crowd at the far end, a man speaking from a platform about 100 yards away. Dyer reported 6,000;
  the report says "from 10 to 20,000"; the minority report 15,000 to 20,000. [H III.37; H Min. IV.2]
- "There is no evidence as to the nature of the address to which the audience was listening. None of
  them were provided with firearms, although some of them may have been carrying sticks." [H III.38]
- He placed 25 men on each side and "without giving the crowd any warning to disperse" ordered fire.
  [H III.38]
- Firing lasted about 10 minutes, individual fire, "In all 1,650 rounds were fired by the troops."
  "As soon as firing commenced the crowd began to disperse." [H III.38]
- No count was made. Government later found "approximately 379 people were killed", about 87 of them
  strangers or villagers; wounded "probably three times" that. Minority report: "at least 379 killed
  and about 1,200 wounded". [H III.38; H Min. IV.2]
- He gave no help to the wounded: "The wounded only had to apply for help." [H III.41]
- Curfew earlier than usual, so the wounded could not be moved; many died in the night. [W; T]
- Next morning a deputation was allowed to cremate or bury; four or five bodies per pyre, no count.
  [T]
- Churchill, Commons, 8 July 1920: the crowd was unarmed, "Pinned up in a narrow place considerably
  smaller than Trafalgar Square"; when fire was aimed at the centre people ran to the sides, and fire
  was aimed at the sides; "This was continued for 8 or 10 minutes, and it stopped only when the
  ammunition had reached the point of exhaustion." [HH]
- The well: a plaque at the site (after independence) says 120 bodies were taken from it. Single
  source. Write "a plaque at the site says"; the Hunter report never mentions the well. [W]
- **Two versions:** 379 (official; Hunter majority and minority) / about 700 (Vishwa Nath Datta) /
  about 1,000 (Malaviya and the Congress inquiry). The government first said about 200. [W; H III.38]
- Quiet ending: the walls still carry bullet marks; the memorial was opened on 13 April 1961 by
  Rajendra Prasad. [W]

## 2. A grave error — FLAGSHIP

- Committee appointed by resolution from Simla, 14 October 1919. Lord Hunter presided; members
  included Justice G. C. Rankin, Sir Chimanlal Setalvad, Pandit Jagat Narayan, Sardar Sultan Ahmad
  Khan. [H, Resolution No. 2168]
- Sat in public except four witnesses; 8 days at Delhi, 29 at Lahore, 6 at Ahmedabad, 3 at Bombay.
  [H, Hunter's letter, 8 March 1920]
- The Congress refused to appear while the Punjab leaders were in prison; it ran its own inquiry,
  published 20 February 1920. [H; T]
- Hunter's report sent 8 March 1920. [H]
- Majority criticised Dyer on two grounds only: firing without warning, and firing on after the crowd
  began to disperse. [H III.39–40]
- "In continuing to fire for so long as he did it appears to us that General Dyer committed a grave
  error." [H III.40]
- Dyer's reason, quoted in the report: to produce "the necessary moral and widespread effect", not
  only on those present "but more especially throughout the Punjab". Majority: "In our view this was
  unfortunately a mistaken conception of his duty." [H III.40]
- Majority also found "there was no rebellion" of the kind Dyer described. [H X.9]
- Minority (Indian members): the proclamation was "insufficiently promulgated", innocent people were
  in the crowd, there was no violence in the Bagh beforehand, and Dyer should have helped the wounded.
  [H Min. IV]
- No prosecution. His action had been condoned by his superiors; the Viceroy's Council decided
  prosecution was impossible for political reasons. He was removed from his appointment, told there
  was no prospect of further employment, and went to half pay. [W; HH]
- Hansard, 8 July 1920: Montagu called Dyer's reasoning "the doctrine of terrorism". Churchill: "one
  tremendous fact stands out—I mean the slaughter of nearly 400 persons and the wounding of probably
  three or four times as many, at the Jallian Wallah Bagh on 13th April. That is an episode which
  appears to me to be without precedent or parallel in the modern history of the British Empire."
  He called it "a monstrous event". [HH]
- Carson defended him: his divisional commander and the Lieutenant-Governor had approved at the time;
  to break a man so was "un-English". The Commons divided 247 to 37 for the Government. [HH]
- The Morning Post opened a fund for him. The often-quoted £26,000 is UNVERIFIED; leave it out.
  Dyer died 23 July 1927. [T]
- Fairness: the same report records the crowd was defying a proclamation, some carried sticks, and a
  small force feared being overwhelmed. Churchill himself said the civil authorities had failed him.
  [H; HH]

## 3. The lane where people had to crawl — SHORT

- 10 April 1919: Marcella Sherwood, a missionary teacher, was attacked while cycling and left for
  dead. Say only that. [H XI.18; T]
- Some days later Dyer put up a triangle (whipping post) where she fell, and two pickets with orders
  that no Indian might pass between them except on all fours. [H XI.18]
- **Dating:** the report says "10th April, i.e., 9 days after the assault", which cannot be right. The
  Tribune says he saw her on 19 April, the order ran to 24 April; the report says it was withdrawn on
  26 April by the Punjab Government, which disapproved. Use 19 April, with a note. [H XI.18; T]
- About 50 people crawled. Six men were flogged at the post and made to crawl there and back; they
  were later convicted of the assault. Others crawled simply to reach shops and homes. [H XI.18]
- Verdict to quote: the order "caused unnecessary inconvenience to a number of people, and that it
  unnecessarily punished innocent as well as guilty. Above all ... in subjecting the Indian population
  to an act of humiliation, it has continued to be a cause of bitterness and racial ill-feeling long
  after it was recalled." [H XI.18]
- Keep apart: the Gujranwala "salaaming order" of 22 April 1919, a different district and officer.
  [H XI.19]
- Gandhi later put this above the massacre: "In Amritsar innocent men and women were made to crawl
  like worms on their bellies. Before this procedure the Jallianwala Bagh tragedy paled into
  insignificance in my eyes..." He asked the Viceroy for permission to go to the Punjab and was
  refused. [G]

## 4. The letter Tagore sent — SHORT

- Nobel Prize in Literature 1913; knighted in 1915, so "Sir Rabindranath". [EB]
- In 1919 he resigned the title by letter to the Viceroy, dated 31 May 1919, Lord Chelmsford. [EB; W]
- "I ... wish to stand, shorn, of all special distinctions, by the side of those of my countrymen
  who, for their so called insignificance, are liable to suffer degradation not fit for human beings."
  [W — single source for the wording; it comes through Wikipedia]
- "The enormity of the measures taken by the Government in Punjab for quelling some local
  disturbances has, with a rude shock, revealed to our minds the helplessness of our position as
  British subjects in India..." [W]
- He had the news by 22 May, tried to call a protest meeting in Calcutta, then chose renunciation as
  "a symbolic act of protest". [W]
- Fairness: he was not a follower of Gandhi then; the same Britannica entry records that he later
  called non-cooperation "perverted nationalism, which was making of India a prison". [EB]
- For a child: a knighthood is an honour from the government, not a prize or a job; giving it back
  was a public refusal to be honoured by the government that did this.

## 5. How India heard — SHORT

- First message reached Lahore about 11 or 11.15 that night, "much mutilated": "Sense seems to
  be—seven arrests were made to day and a prohibited meeting dispersed ... Rumours heavy casualties
  in Amritsar to-day." [H III.42]
- About 3 a.m. on 14 April two men arrived by car with the District Magistrate's message: dispersed by
  force, about 200 dead. [H III.42]
- That was the government's public figure, and the next morning's papers carried it. [H III.38; W]
- The press was already gagged; some papers were forced to stop. The Tribune's editor Kalinath Ray was
  convicted for "seditious writings" and the paper suspended. [T]
- Amritsar was sealed; martial law in the district from 15 April; passes needed to enter or leave.
  [H X; T; W]
- Details reached Britain only in December 1919. [W]
- 12 September 1919, Imperial Legislative Council: Malaviya's inquiry reported 42 of the dead were
  boys, the youngest seven months. The Hunter report gives 337 men, 41 boys, a six-week-old baby. Use
  "about 40 boys". [W]
- Congress report 20 February 1920; Hunter report 8 March 1920, published in May; Commons debate 8
  July 1920, 247 to 37. [T; H; HH]

## 6. What the wall remembers — SHORT

- Plaque at the site: 120 bodies from the well. [W]
- A trust was founded in 1920 after a Congress resolution; it bought land in 1923. The memorial was
  designed by Benjamin Polk. [W]
- Rajendra Prasad inaugurated it on 13 April 1961, with Nehru present; the flame came later. [W]
- Bullet-marked walls and the Martyrs' Well are preserved. [W]
- Britain has never formally apologised. 1997: the Queen spoke of regret and sadness at a state
  banquet; some welcomed it, others said it fell short. February 2013: Cameron, the first serving
  prime minister to visit, laid a wreath, called it "a deeply shameful event", did not apologise.
  12 April 2019: Theresa May told the Commons "We deeply regret what happened and the suffering
  caused", and called it "a shameful scar on British Indian history" — again no apology. [W; B]
- Udham Singh said he was in the Bagh; he shot Sir Michael O'Dwyer in London on 13 March 1940 and was
  hanged on 31 July 1940. His presence in the Bagh is his own account — a "Two versions" note. [W; T]

## Do not say

- No quotation from Dyer beyond the two in the Hunter report, unless the minutes are checked. His
  "they would have ... laughed" line is UNVERIFIED; paraphrase and attribute to his evidence.
- No names of individual victims; the Dictionary of Martyrs PDF was never opened.
- No invented crowd noises, weather, thoughts or last words.
- Do not present 379 as the true figure; the disagreement is the point.
- Do not make Tagore a Congress man, or Dyer a cartoon. Both were more ordinary and more disturbing.
