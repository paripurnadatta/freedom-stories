On 7 August 1905 a meeting in Calcutta voted to give up British goods. It was an easy promise to make. It was harder to keep. If shops stopped selling foreign cloth, who would make the cloth instead?

The swadeshi movement tried to answer that. The word means "of one's own country". The idea was to buy things made in India, and to make more of them.

## Older roots

The idea was not new in 1905. One page says that the Namdhari Sikhs boycotted English cloth in 1871 and 1872, and wore hand-spun khadi instead. The same page says Bal Gangadhar Tilak led the Ganesh festival in 1893 to promote Indian goods.

Gandhi, who came later, called swadeshi the soul of swaraj, which means self-rule.

## New mills and factories

In Bengal, after 1905, money flowed into Indian business. The Banga Lakshmi Cotton Mills opened in August 1906. Smaller works began to make porcelain, chrome goods, soap, matches and cigarettes.

The pages name the products but give no figures for how many people worked in them or how long they lasted.

## A chemist

One of the men behind this was Prafulla Chandra Ray. He was born on 2 August 1861 in the Jessore district, now in Bangladesh. He sailed to Scotland in August 1882 and studied chemistry at Edinburgh. He got a science degree in 1885 and a doctorate in 1887. He taught for years at Presidency College in Calcutta, and he retired in 1916.

Ray started a small business too. It was called Bengal Chemical Works. One page says he began it in 1892 in a rented house at 91 Upper Circular Road, Calcutta, with seven hundred rupees. He wanted to give young Bengalis a way to earn a living that was not a job under the British.

<aside class="note"><b>Two dates</b>: One page gives 1892 for the start of the works. The same page's box gives 12 April 1901, the day it was renamed Bengal Chemical and Pharmaceutical Works Ltd. The works and the company are not the same date.</aside>

The firm showed herbal medicines at the Indian Medical Congress in Calcutta in 1893. In 1905 it opened its first factory at Maniktala. Later factories followed at Panihati in 1920, Bombay in 1938 and Kanpur in 1949.

## What happened to it

Ray died on 16 June 1944, aged 82. The company began to lose money in the middle of the 1950s. The Union government took over its management on 15 December 1977 and nationalised it three years later.

<aside class="note"><b>What the pages do not say</b>: The page on the company does not use the word swadeshi. It speaks only of Ray's wish to encourage young Bengali businessmen. Linking it to the movement is an inference from its dates and aims.</aside>

## Why it matters

A boycott needs something to put in the place of what it gives up. The mills, soap works and chemical factories of these years were one answer. Ray's company is one that grew for decades.
