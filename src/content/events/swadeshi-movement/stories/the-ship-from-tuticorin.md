Tuticorin is a port on the southern coast of India. In 1906 the sea route from Tuticorin to Colombo, in Ceylon, belonged to one company. It was the British India Steam Navigation Company. It held a near-monopoly.

One man decided to challenge it.

## A lawyer from Ottapidaram

Valliyappan Olaganathan Chidambaram Pillai was born on 5 September 1872 in Ottapidaram, in the Tirunelveli district of the Madras Presidency. He is known as V. O. C. He went to school at Thoothukudi and studied law at Tiruchirappalli. He passed his pleader's examination in 1894. In 1895 he began to work as a pleader in Ottapidaram.

He later moved to Tuticorin and built up a law practice there. The partition of Bengal in 1905 pulled him into politics. He joined the extremist group in the Congress, and Bal Gangadhar Tilak was his political guide. By 1906 he had largely given up the law for swadeshi business.

<aside class="note"><b>Swadeshi</b>: from "swa", own, and "desh", country. It meant using goods made in India.</aside>

## The company

In October 1906 V. O. C. registered the Swadeshi Steam Navigation Company. One page gives the date as 16 October 1906.

<aside class="note"><b>The name</b>: One page calls it the Swadeshi Shipping Company and says V. O. C. turned a British shipping company in Tuticorin into an Indian one. The other pages say he registered a new company.</aside>

It had a capital of ten lakh rupees. That was divided into 40,000 shares of 25 rupees each. Only Indians, Ceylonese and other Asians could buy the shares.

Before him, a trading company of Tuticorin called the Sena Vana company had already been a rallying point for Indian traders. How it turned into Swadeshi Steam is "a bit fuzzy", a reviewer says.

At first the company chartered ships from the Shah Line of Bombay, which Essaji Tajbhoy owned. Then there was trouble over the contract. The company bought two steamships of its own, the *Gallia* and the *Lawoe*. They were reconditioned.

<aside class="note"><b>Where did the ships come from?</b> One page says two steamships were bought from France. Another says one freighter was leased from Sri Lanka. The reviewer says only that the company acquired its own ships, the Gallia and the Lawoe, after the contract trouble.</aside>

It was the first Indian-owned shipping service between Tuticorin and Colombo.

## A price war

The British company fought back. It cut its fares to one rupee. Then it offered free rides, and even free umbrellas. V. O. C. cut fares as well, first to one rupee and then to half a rupee.

Merchants and national leaders sent money to keep the Indian company going. One page says that people of the Vanniyar community gave about three quarters of the funds. The reviewer says Madras gave the most disappointing share, and that Calcutta and Bombay gave more. The two accounts do not fit together.

The reviewer adds that many shareholders did not pay in full, so the money promised was far more than the money received.

## The Coral Mills strike

In February 1908 V. O. C. turned to another cause. The workers of the Coral Mills in Tuticorin, a foreign-owned mill, had little pay and long hours. On 23 February he spoke to them. His friend Subramania Siva spoke too. The workers went on strike.

The strike lasted nine days. The workers won. They got higher pay, shorter hours and a holiday on Sundays.

## Arrest

On 12 March 1908 the police arrested V. O. C. and Subramania Siva. They had held public meetings to celebrate the release of Bipin Chandra Pal. The authorities had set conditions on their political activity. They had refused them.

Pal was a Bengali leader of the swadeshi movement, and Tuticorin lies far from Bengal. The meetings show how widely the movement had spread. The arrest set off protests. There was a general strike in Thoothukudi. One page calls it India's first political strike. Violence followed a public meeting in Tirunelveli and Tuticorin. The riots are called the Tinnevelly riots.

## The sentence

V. O. C. was found guilty of sedition. The court gave him two life terms, which were counted as about forty years. On appeal the High Court cut this to four years in prison plus six years of exile. The Privy Council cut it again. The pages do not give the final sentence.

He was held in Coimbatore and Kannur. In Coimbatore he had hard labour. He had to pull an oil press in place of a bullock. It ruined his health.

<aside class="note"><b>Dates in prison</b>: One page says he was held at Coimbatore until 1 December 1910, yet gives his release as 12 December 1912. The page does not explain the gap.</aside>

He was let out on 12 December 1912.

Subramania Siva was born on 4 October 1884 at Batlagundu in the Madurai district. He edited a journal called Gnanabhanu. He was jailed four times between 1908 and 1922. In prison he caught leprosy. He died of it on 23 July 1925. A memorial to him stands at Papparapatti, in Dharmapuri district.

## Afterwards

The company did not survive. It was liquidated while he was in jail, in July 1911. One page says the British India company bought its ships. Another says they were auctioned.

V. O. C. lost his licence to practise law. Later he went to court and got it back. He wrote to Gandhi between 1915 and 1920. He left the Congress in 1920. He rejoined in 1927 and left again.

He also wrote in Tamil, including a commentary on the Tirukkural. In his later years he worked as a clerk and kept a grocery shop. He died on 18 November 1936 at the Congress office in Tuticorin. He was poor.

## Remembered

Tamils call him Kappalottiya Thamizhan, "the Tamil who sailed the ship". India issued a stamp for his hundredth birthday in 1972. A Tamil film about him, *Kappal Ottiya Tamizhan*, was a big success in 1961. The port of Tuticorin now carries his name. It was renamed the V. O. Chidambaranar Port Trust in 2011, and in 2022 it became the V. O. Chidambaranar Port Authority.

<aside class="note"><b>Did it hurt the British company?</b> The author of a recent book says the price war cost the British company dearly. The reviewer doubts it, because the Indian service ran only now and then, and the British company kept its hold on other routes.</aside>

## Why it matters

The Swadeshi Steam Navigation Company lost. Its ships were sold and its founder was jailed. But the idea behind it lived on. Years later another Indian company, the Scindia Steam Navigation Company, settled with British India on a way to share the routes.
