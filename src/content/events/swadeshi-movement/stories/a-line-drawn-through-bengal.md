Bengal was the biggest province in British India. In 1905 about 78 million people lived in it. It was the largest province in British India.

The Viceroy, Lord Curzon, thought it was too big to govern well. He had a plan to cut it in two.

## The plan

The British decided on the partition in December 1903. The plan was published in January 1904. In February 1904 Curzon travelled through eastern Bengal. He gave speeches at Dhaka, Chittagong and Mymensingh. The visit, says one page, shaped the larger plan that followed.

The official reason was plain. Officials said the province was too big for one government, and that the eastern part was badly governed because of it.

<aside class="note"><b>Viceroy</b>: the ruler who represented the British king in India.</aside>

The final order is dated 19 July 1905. It came into force on 16 October 1905.

Two provinces were made. The new eastern province was called Eastern Bengal and Assam. Its capital was Dhaka. It joined fifteen districts of eastern Bengal with Assam and had about 31 million people, most of them Muslim. The western province was the rest of the Bengali-speaking districts, joined with Orissa and Bihar.

<aside class="note"><b>Two views of the reason</b>: One page says the first plan was about good government, and that political reasons were an afterthought. Another page says officials feared that Bengal was the centre of revolt and wanted to weaken it. The pages leave the matter open.</aside>

## "Vivisection"

Many Hindus in Bengal were against the plan. They were mostly educated people of the towns, called the bhadralok. They said it was a policy of divide and rule. Some called it a "vivisection" of their homeland, as if a living body was being cut.

At first the protests were the old kind: petitions, meetings and articles in newspapers. When these did not work, people turned to something new. On 7 August 1905 a meeting in Calcutta voted to give up British goods. One page calls that day the birthday of Indian nationalism. You can read about the movement that followed in the next stories.

## Swadeshi

The boycott of 7 August was only the start. People tied rakhis on each other's wrists as a sign of unity. Rabindranath Tagore asked for the rakhi to be tied between Hindus and Muslims. He also asked that no food be cooked in Bengali homes on the day of the partition, as a day of mourning.

In Calcutta Tagore began that day with a bath in the Ganga. He walked in a procession through the streets and tied rakhis on the people he met. He even wished to tie one on the maulvis of a mosque near his house. His companions objected, but the maulvis did not. On 16 October 1905 Federation Hall was founded in Calcutta. It was meant as a sign that the two Bengals were one.

A group called the Swadesh Bandhav Samity, led by Ashwini Kumar Datta, worked in the villages. A new national education plan was drawn up by Satish Chandra Mukherjee. In March 1906 a National Society of Education was set up to run something like a parallel university.

Indian business took up the cause too. The Banga Lakshmi Cotton Mills opened in August 1906. Small ventures made porcelain, soap, matches and cigarettes.

The movement spread beyond Bengal. The trio Lal, Bal and Pal, meaning Lajpat Rai, Tilak and Bipin Chandra Pal, organised many samitis. They became inactive after 1908, because of deportations and arrests.

## The other side

Not every Bengali was against the plan. Many Muslims of the east welcomed it.

The leader of this opinion was the Nawab of Dhaka, Khwaja Salimullah. He was born in 1871 into the head family of the Nawabs of Dhaka. At first he had called the plan "beastly". Later he supported it. He took up the cause in 1903 and 1904, and he became a firm supporter once the larger eastern province was in view.

On 16 October 1905 he presided over a meeting at the Northbrook Hall in Dhaka. It founded the Mohammedan Provincial Union.

Opinion among Muslims had changed over time. Muslim groups first condemned the 1903 plan. After the wider province was planned, most Muslim leaders came to support it. But some Muslims did not. Khwaja Atiqullah, for one, backed the movement against partition. A few Muslims took part in the swadeshi campaign and spoke for Hindu–Muslim unity.

In December 1906 a gathering at Dhaka, called on Salimullah's initiative, founded the All-India Muslim League. Riots broke out in Comilla in March 1907 and in Jamalpur in April 1907. Many middle-class and upper-class Muslims moved away from the swadeshi movement.

## What Curzon wanted

Did Curzon mean to split Hindus from Muslims? Historians disagree. Peter Hardy argues that Curzon meant to divide Bengalis from each other, rather than Hindus from Muslims. Yet the same page notes that Curzon described the new province as a Muslim one.

## A plan that backfired

The partition was meant to weaken the Congress and the educated middle class in Bengal. It did the opposite. It made Indian nationalism more angry and more radical. The historian Sekhar Bandyopadhyay says it "magnified the nationalist angst".

## Undone

On 12 December 1911, at the Delhi Durbar, King George V announced that the partition would be cancelled. Bengal was joined together again. Assam, Bihar and Orissa were separated out. The capital of British India moved from Calcutta to Delhi.

The change took effect on 1 April 1912.

Not everyone welcomed the news. Many Muslims saw the reversal as a gain for Hindus, at their cost. Within days of the announcement Salimullah sent a list of demands to the Viceroy, Lord Hardinge. The reply promised a university at Dhaka.

## Why it matters

The partition did not last, but the fight over it changed India. Protest went beyond petitions and speeches. The Muslim League was founded in the same years. And Bengal was partitioned again in 1947, this time along the line of religion. East Bengal became part of Pakistan. It was renamed East Pakistan in 1955, and it became Bangladesh in 1971.
