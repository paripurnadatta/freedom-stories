Not everyone in Bengal mourned on 16 October 1905. In Dhaka, a meeting was held at the Northbrook Hall. The man in the chair was Khwaja Salimullah, the Nawab of Dhaka.

## A family of Dhaka

Salimullah was born on 7 June 1871 at Ahsan Manzil, the family house in Dhaka. He was the eldest son of Khwaja Ahsanullah, the third Nawab of Dhaka, and of Nawab Begum Wahidunnesa. When his father died in 1901, he became the head of the family.

He worked as a deputy magistrate from 1893 to 1895, and afterwards he ran a business in Mymensingh.

## For the partition

Salimullah began by calling the partition plan "beastly". Muslim groups at first condemned the 1903 proposal. But the plan grew larger. It would make a new province with Dhaka as its capital and a Muslim majority. From 1903 to 1904 Salimullah began to support it.

Many Muslims in eastern Bengal agreed with him. The Mohammedan Literary Society put out a manifesto in 1905 in favour of the partition. On 16 October 1905 the meeting under Salimullah founded the Mohammedan Provincial Union.

He sat on the Legislative Council of Eastern Bengal and Assam from 1906 to 1907.

<aside class="note"><b>Other Muslim voices</b>: Not all Muslims agreed. Khwaja Atiqullah, for example, backed the movement against partition. Some Muslim swadeshi speakers, such as Ghaznavi and Rasul, argued for unity between the communities.</aside>

## The Muslim League

In December 1906 an educational conference met at Ahsan Manzil in Dhaka. It lasted from 27 to 30 December. On 30 December the All-India Muslim League was formed. Salimullah was chosen vice-president and sat on the committee that drafted its constitution.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: One page lists Salimullah as President of the League. The text of the same page says he was Vice President. The pages also say that he led the provincial and Bengal leagues.</aside>

## When the partition ended

On 12 December 1911 King George V announced that the partition was undone. Within days Salimullah sent a list of demands to the Viceroy, Lord Hardinge. The answer promised a university at Dhaka.

Many Muslims saw the end of the partition as a loss. It favoured the Hindus, they felt, at their expense.

## The last years

In 1912 Salimullah raised money in East Bengal for Turkish Muslims who suffered in the Balkan Wars. When the First World War started, he backed the Allied powers, even though Turkey sided with Germany. He left active politics in 1914. He died on 16 January 1915 in Calcutta, aged 43.

## Why it matters

The story of 1905 is not told the same way in Calcutta and Dhaka. Hindus of the educated middle class feared that Bengal was being cut. Many Muslims of the east saw a new province in which they were the majority. Both feelings were real. The movement against the partition and the movement for it shaped what came after.
