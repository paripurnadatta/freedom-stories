On 16 October 1905 the partition of Bengal came into force. Rabindranath Tagore called it a day of mourning.

He asked that no food be cooked in Bengali homes that day.

<aside class="note"><b>Rakhi</b>: a thread tied on a person's wrist. In the family it is usually tied by a sister on her brother's wrist.</aside>

## A thread for everyone

Tagore wanted to use the rakhi in a new way. He asked Hindus and Muslims to tie it on each other. It would be a sign that Bengalis were one people, even if a line was drawn through their land.

He began the day with a dip in the Ganga. Then a procession set out through the streets. As he walked, he tied rakhis on everyone he met.

One page tells of a mosque south of his house. Tagore wished to go in and tie rakhis on the maulvis. His companions did not want him to. The maulvis did not mind. The procession went on.

Crowds lined the street. The people with him sang a song that Tagore had written for the day. It was a prayer for a safe and united Bengal. Women stood on rooftops and sprinkled puffed rice. They blew conch shells.

<aside class="note"><b>Not recorded</b>: The page gives no number for the crowd, or for the rakhis tied. It names no Muslim taking part, except the unnamed maulvis.</aside>

## Federation Hall

That afternoon the foundation stone of Federation Hall was laid. It was to stand as a sign of the unity of the two Bengals. The barrister Ananda Mohan Bose was to preside. He was old and ill, so Tagore read out his speech.

A second procession went from Federation Hall to Basu Bati in Bagbazar, the house of Pasupati and Nandalal Basu. There the National Fund was begun. It was meant to pay for students and to send young Bengalis to England to train as entrepreneurs.

## What else happened that day

The same day, in Dhaka, Nawab Salimullah presided over a different meeting. It founded the Mohammedan Provincial Union. Many Muslims in the east welcomed the new province.

<aside class="note"><b>Two Bengals</b>: The day looked different from Calcutta and from Dhaka. The pages do not say that Tagore's procession was joined by many Muslims. They do say that the Swadeshi movement drew mostly Hindu masses, and that some Muslim speakers worked for unity.</aside>

## Why it matters

Many Hindus in Bengal, especially the educated middle class, called the partition a "vivisection" of their homeland. Tagore gave the rakhi a public meaning in answer. He asked people to tie bonds beyond their own family. The bond did not hold the two communities together, and Bengal was divided again in 1947. But the day is still remembered as an attempt to keep it whole.
