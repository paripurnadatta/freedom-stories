On 13 March 1930, the Salt March reached Navagam, its third stop. Navagam was in Kheda district. Twelve years earlier, Gandhi had led the farmers of Kheda in a struggle against the land tax.

"As I enter the Kheda District," he said, "memories, some sweet, some bitter, fill the mind." He had seen nearly all its villages, many of them on foot.

## Officials who said no

Something had just happened in Navagam. The village Matadars had refused to take the post of headman, and the headman had resigned.

Headmen and other village officers held their posts from the government. During the march, Gandhi called on them to resign, because their posts helped hold up British rule in the villages.

Soon after, in Kheda district, Gandhi praised other headmen who had resigned. Why, he asked, should a headman hold on to his post "for the mere five rupees a month that the government pays"?

## Praise, then a warning

Gandhi congratulated them. Then, at once, he turned it round.

"If you have been offering resignations under pressure from anyone, I must ask you to withdraw them," he said. Taking them back would not hurt him at all. He would even protect them from anyone who tried to force them to resign.

"This fight is based on truth," he said. "I want no victory with unworthy help."

## Free to stay

Gandhi went further. "It will make no difference to me even if you do not resign," he told them.

He set them a test in front of everyone. If they felt they did not have the strength to resign, they should say so truthfully.

"I shall regard you as brave men if you truthfully withdraw your resignations," he said, "and also if you truthfully adhere to them."

What mattered was that they were honest, and that the choice was really theirs.

## A promise must be kept

He also reminded them how serious a promise was. He quoted a line from the poet Tulsidas: "The way of the Raghus it has been ever, / Life may be lost but a pledge never."

<aside class="note"><b>The Raghus</b>: the family of Lord Rama in the old stories, known for always keeping their word.</aside>

Nobody, he said, should give him a promise and then go back on it behind his back. Either way, the choice had to be made truthfully, and then kept.

## What the government said

In the Borsad area, more than a hundred village officers resigned. A government report said that local workers were trying to get more resignations "under the threat of social boycott". That meant pressure from neighbours, not free choice. At Navagam, Gandhi had already said he wanted no such help.

## Khadi too

Gandhi also had sharp words about cloth. Navagam had about a thousand people. Only one person wore khadi regularly, and there was just one spinning wheel. If the village took a vow to make and wear khadi, he said, it would save Rs 5,000 a year.

## Why it matters

Gandhi wanted officials to resign. It would weaken the government. But he would not accept a resignation that was forced. A victory won by pushing people, he believed, was not worth having.

He even looked ahead to what freedom should mean. When India won swaraj, he said, even a scavenger would be free to take Vallabhbhai Patel to task. In Gandhi's idea of freedom, nobody was above being questioned, and nobody was pushed into doing right.
