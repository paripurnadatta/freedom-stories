# Fact sheet: dandi-march-salt-satyagraha

One page, for writing. The six stories in `stories/` already exist in an older form: rewrite them
from this sheet, and do not treat the old text as a source. Everything below was opened on
27 October 2026: the four march speeches and two letters on gandhiashramsevagram.org, the Irwin
letter in CWMG XLIII, the mkgandhi.org chronology, and Wikipedia for dates only. Source keys are at
the end. If a fact is not here, do not write it.

Six stories: two flagship (900+ words), four short (350+).

The spine: 2 March 1930 (letter), 12 March (march begins, Aslali), 13 March (Bareja, Navagam),
29 March (Bhatgam), 5 April (Dandi reached), 6 April, 8.30 a.m. (salt law broken).

The best material in this subject is not the walking. It is Gandhi arguing with his own side.

---

## 1. The Letter Before the March — FLAGSHIP

- Sabarmati Ashram, 2 March 1930, to Lord Irwin. Published in *Young India*, 12 March 1930.
  [CWMG XLIII, pp. 2–8]
- The opening: "Before embarking on civil disobedience and taking the risk I have dreaded to take
  all these years, I would fain approach you and find a way out." [CWMG p. 2]
- The limit he set himself first: "I cannot intentionally hurt anything that lives, much less
  fellow human beings, even though they may do the greatest wrong to me and mine. Whilst,
  therefore, I hold the British rule to be a curse, I do not intend harm to a single Englishman or
  to any legitimate interest he may have in India." [CWMG p. 2]
- And: "I have the privilege of claiming many Englishmen as dearest friends. Indeed much that I
  have learnt of the evil of British rule is due to the writings of frank and courageous Englishmen
  who have not hesitated to tell the unpalatable truth about that rule." [CWMG pp. 2–3]
- Why he calls it a curse: "It has impoverished the dumb millions by a system of progressive
  exploitation and by a ruinously expensive military and civil administration which the country can
  never afford." [CWMG p. 3]
- The political trigger: the Viceroy had refused to promise that he or the British Cabinet would
  support full Dominion Status, so "the Round Table Conference could not possibly furnish the
  solution for which vocal India is consciously, and the dumb millions are unconsciously,
  thirsting". The Delhi interview of 23 December 1929 had "miscarried". [CWMG p. 3 and n. 1]
- The salt passage, which is the heart: "Even the salt he must use to live is so taxed as to make
  the burden fall heaviest on him, if only because of the heartless impartiality of its incidence.
  The tax shows itself still more burdensome on the poor man when it is remembered that salt is the
  one thing he must eat more than the rich man both individually and collectively." [CWMG p. 4]
- The salary passage: "Take your own salary. It is over Rs. 21,000 per month, besides many other
  indirect additions... You are getting over Rs. 700 per day against India's average income of less
  than annas 2 per day... Thus you are getting much over five thousand times India's average
  income." Then: "On bended knees I ask you to ponder over this phenomenon." [CWMG p. 5]
- He is careful with Irwin as a man: "I know that you do not need the salary you get. Probably the
  whole of your salary goes for charity. But a system that provides for such an arrangement deserves
  to be summarily scrapped." [CWMG p. 5]
- On the other side, the party of violence: "It is common cause that, however disorganized and, for
  the time being, insignificant it may be, the party of violence is gaining ground and making
  itself felt. Its end is the same as mine. But I am convinced that it cannot bring the desired
  relief to the dumb millions." [CWMG p. 6]
- What he says he is doing: "I have deliberately used the word 'conversion'. For my ambition is no
  less than to convert the British people through non-violence, and thus make them see the wrong
  they have done to India. I do not seek to harm your people. I want to serve them even as I want to
  serve my own." [CWMG p. 6]
- The date he named: "if you cannot see your way to deal with these evils and my letter makes no
  appeal to your heart, on the 11th day of this month, I shall proceed with such co-workers of the
  Ashram as I can take, to disregard the provisions of the salt laws. I regard this tax to be the
  most iniquitous of all from the poor man's standpoint." [CWMG p. 7]
  **The march actually began on 12 March** — CWMG prints its own footnote: "The march, however,
  started on March 12." [CWMG p. 7 n. 1] Say so; it is a good honest detail, not an error to hide.
- He names the risk: "It is, I know, open to you to frustrate my design by arresting me. I hope
  that there will be tens of thousands ready, in a disciplined manner, to take up the work after
  me, and, in the act of disobeying the Salt Act to lay themselves open to the penalties of a law
  that should never have disfigured the Statute-book." [CWMG p. 7]
- The way out he offered: "If you think that there is any substance in my letter, and if you will
  care to discuss matters with me, and if to that end you would like me to postpone publication of
  this letter, I shall gladly refrain on receipt of a telegram to that effect soon after this
  reaches you. You will, however, do me the favour not to deflect me from my course unless you can
  see your way to conform to the substance of this letter." [CWMG p. 7]
- "This letter is not in any way intended as a threat but is a simple and sacred duty peremptory on
  a civil resister." [CWMG p. 8]
- The messenger: "I am having it specially delivered by a young English friend who believes in the
  Indian cause and is a full believer in non-violence and whom Providence seems to have sent to me,
  as it were, for the very purpose." [CWMG p. 8] CWMG's footnote names him: Reginald Reynolds, who
  in *To Live in Mankind* wrote that "Before I went Gandhi insisted I should read the letter
  carefully, as he did not wish me to associate myself with it unless I was in complete agreement
  with its contents." [CWMG p. 8 n. 1]
- The reply: the Viceroy's answer "was simply an expression of regret that Gandhiji should be
  'contemplating a course of action which is clearly bound to involve violation of the law and
  danger to the public peace'." [CWMG p. 7 n. 2] There was no meeting.
- The quiet ending: Reynolds stayed at the Ashram. On 13 March Gandhi wrote to him: "Take care of
  yourself. Besides looking after Young India, do please identify yourself with the activities of
  the Ashram. I am anxious for it to become an abode of peace, purity and strength. You would be a
  gift from God for the advancement of that work." [R]
- Watch: do not write that Irwin "rejected" the letter in a speech, or that he and Gandhi met. The
  only reply on record is the expression of regret. Do not call the letter an ultimatum; Gandhi
  explicitly says it is not a threat. Do not say the letter was secret — it was published.

## 2. The Lamp on the Road — FLAGSHIP

- Bhatgam, 29 March 1930. The march was in its last week and entering Surat district: "As we shall
  reach our destination next Saturday, we shall not have to march any further." [B]
- He does not begin with the Government. "I have been asked to deliver a sermon. I have little
  fitness for the task. But tonight I propose to make a confession and turn the searchlight
  inward." [B]
- Why: "I am plain-spoken. I have not hesitated to describe the mountain-high faults of the
  Government in appropriate language. And I have not hesitated often to picture as mountain-high
  our faults appearing to us as trifling." [B]
- The trigger that day: the local workers had "ordered milk from Surat to be brought in a motor
  lorry and they had incurred other expenses which I could not justify. I therefore spoke strongly
  about them. But that did not allay my grief. On the contrary it increased with the contemplation
  of the wrongs done." [B]
- The argument, which is the whole point: "In the light of these discoveries, what right had I to
  write to the Viceroy the letter in which I have severely criticized his salary which is more than
  5,000 times our average income?... I have no right to criticize the Viceregal salary if we are
  costing the country, say, fifty times, seven pice, the average daily income of our people." [B]
- The excess, in his own examples: "if they will fetch for me from whatever source possible, the
  choicest oranges and grapes, if they will bring 120 when I should want 12 oranges, if when I need
  one pound of milk, they will produce three?" [B]
- And the image he turns on himself: "imagine me with an easy conscience writing the Viceregal
  letter on costly glazed paper with a fountain pen, a gift from some accommodating friend!" [B]
- The verse he quotes, Akho Bhagat: "stolen food is like eating unprocessed mercury". His
  application: "to live above the means befitting a poor country is to live on stolen food. This
  battle can never be won by living on stolen food." [B]
- **The lamp scene.** "I observed that you had provided for the night journey a heavy kerosene
  burner mounted on a stool which a poor labourer carried on his head. This was a humiliating
  sight. This man was being goaded to walk fast. I could not bear the sight. I therefore put on
  speed and outraced the whole company. But it was no use. The man was made to run after me. The
  humiliation was complete." [B]
- What he says should have happened: "If the weight had to be carried, I should have loved to see
  someone among ourselves carrying it. We would then soon dispense both with the stool and the
  burner. No labourer would carry such a load on his head." [B]
- The word he uses for it: "We rightly object to begar (forced labour). But what was this if it was
  not begar?" [B]
- Why it matters to him politically: "Remember that in swaraj we would expect one drawn from the
  so-called lower class to preside over India's destiny. If then we do not quickly mend our ways,
  there is no swaraj such as you and I have put before the people." [B]
- The rules that follow, all short: "Nor may milk and vegetables be brought from Surat." — "Do not
  resort to motor-cars on the slightest pretext. The rule is, do not ride if you can walk." — "In
  order to procure goat's milk for me you may not deprive poor women of milk for their children. It
  would be like poison if you did." — "Let the volunteers account for every pice spent." [B]
- "Extravagance has no room in this campaign. If we cannot gather crowds unless we carry on a
  hurricane expensive propaganda, I would be satisfied to address half a dozen men and women." [B]
- The strange, strong claim: "this campaign will not succeed through newspaper reports, but with
  the assistance of Shri Rama. And, no light is necessary when we are near Him; neither are pen and
  ink and such other accessories required, nor even speech." [B]
- Why he has the right to say it: "I am more capable of offering satyagraha against ourselves than
  against the Government. I have taken many years before embarking upon civil resistance against
  the Government. But I should not take as many days for offering it against ourselves." [B]
- The resolve at the end: "It will continue no matter how co-workers or others act. For me there is
  no turning back whether I am alone or joined by thousands. I would rather die a dog's death and
  have my bones licked by dogs than that I should return to the Ashram a broken man." [B]
- And the last line, which is easy to leave out and should not be: "I admit that I have not well
  used the money you have given out of the abundance of your love. You are entitled to regard me as
  one of those wretches described in the verses sung in the beginning. Shun me." [B]
- Watch: do not name the labourer or make him speak. Do not describe the night, the road or the
  weather. Do not turn this into "Gandhi was a hypocrite" — he is applying to himself the standard
  he used on the Government, and he does it in public. Do not use the word "servant" for the
  labourer; he says "a poor labourer".

## 3. Salt for a Whole Village — SHORT

- Aslali, 12 March 1930, the first halt after the march began. [A]
- He does not start with freedom. He starts with a sum: "Take the case of your own village: For a
  population of 1,700, 850 maunds of salt will be required. For 200 bullocks, 300 maunds of salt
  will be required. That is, total of 1,150 maunds of salt will be required." [A]
- The tax: "The Government levies a tax of Rs. 1-4 on one pukka maund of salt. Hence, on 1,150
  maunds, which is equal to 575 pukka maunds, you pay a tax of Rs. 720." [A]
- And that is not the end of it: 800 cows, buffaloes and calves, the tanner's salt, salt used as
  manure — "you would be paying that amount of tax in excess of Rs. 720." [A]
- The question he puts to them: "Can your village afford to pay this amount in taxes every year?"
  [A]
- The comparison that makes it bite: "In India, the average income of an individual is calculated
  at 7 pice or, in other words, hundreds of thousands of persons do not earn even a single pice and
  either die of starvation or live by begging. Even they cannot do without salt. What will be the
  plight of such persons if they can get no salt or get it at too high a price?" [A]
- The price gap: salt sells at 9 pice a maund in the Punjab, and heaps of it are made on the coast
  of Kathiawar and Gujarat, yet "cannot be had by the poor at less than Re. 1-8-0 a maund". "What
  curses the Government may not be inviting upon itself from the poor for hiring men to throw this
  salt into mud!" [A]
- The political idea in one sentence: "A democratic State is one which has authority to abolish a
  tax which does not deserve to be paid. It is one in which the people can determine when a certain
  thing should or should not be paid." [A]
- And the fairness: "I can understand there being a tax on such things as the hookah, bidis and
  liquor... But should one levy a tax on salt?" [A]
- The jail arithmetic: if 30 lakh adults out of Gujarat's 90 lakhs broke the law, "the Government
  does not have enough accommodation in jails to house so many people." [A]
- The closing image: the crowd from the Ashram to Chandola lake, "for a distance of seven miles",
  "a sight for the gods to see". [A]
- Watch: these are Gandhi's own figures, worked out for one village and spoken aloud. Write "he
  worked out what the tax cost Aslali", not "Aslali paid Rs 720". Do not add a family's budget, a
  shop, or a salt pan. Do not say the crowd was counted.

## 4. The Zeroes at Bareja — SHORT

- Bareja, 13 March 1930, the second halt. Population 2,500. [Ba]
- The turn: "This is our second halt after the march began. As at our first halt, here too I was
  given the required information about this village. I was pained to read it. It is strange that a
  place so near Ahmedabad has zeroes against the columns for consumption of khadi, the number of
  habitual khadi-wearers and spinning-wheels at work." [Ba]
- The personal rule he mentions: "During my tours of North and South India, I used to follow a
  rule, namely, that the barber cutting my hair should be a khadi-wearer." [Ba]
- The claim: "Khadi is the foundation of our freedom struggle. All like khadi, but people are
  nowadays deterred by the fear that those wearing khadi will have to go to jail and die." [Ba]
- The concrete ask: "There is a khadi store here and you can certainly remove this blot... I
  request you to renounce luxuries and buy khadi from this heap before you." [Ba]
- The comparison he uses: "We do not disown our mother because she is fat or ugly to look at and
  adopt another women, more beautiful, to fill her place. Foreign cloth will never bring us
  freedom." [Ba]
- Where the march fits: "At present Sardar is in jail, and I have come here to ask you to join in
  our common struggle for freedom." [Ba]
- The turn back to the village: "The people of this place number twenty-five hundred. If you so
  resolve, you can make the village beautiful and have as many amenities as you choose." — "The
  education imparted in present-day schools is, according to me, no education at all." — "The
  village has a large number of Christians and Muslims also. If you all come together, you will be
  able to achieve much for the village. For winning complete freedom, all these communities will
  have to unite." [Ba]
- The line that links khadi to governing: "We have come forward to win our freedom from this
  tyrannical and oppressive Government. If we cannot put our own house in order in an organized
  manner, how shall we run the country's Government? I ask you, therefore, to learn order and
  organization." [Ba]
- Watch: **do not quote or paraphrase the sentence about Bhangis** ("I believe that they are the
  scum of the earth"). It is in the source, it is ugly, and it is not needed. Do not mention it in
  a note either. The story is the zeroes in the khadi columns and what he asked for instead.
- Do not date Patel's arrest. The speech shows he was already in jail on 13 March; the mkgandhi.org
  chronology puts his arrest at Ras on 19 March, which cannot both be right. Leave the date out.

## 5. A Resignation Must Be Free — SHORT

- Navagam, 13 March 1930, the third halt, entering Kheda district. "As I enter the Kheda District,
  memories — some sweet, some bitter — fill the mind. It was while working in the Kheda District
  that I became one with the lives of people. I have seen nearly all the villages here. I covered
  many of them on foot." [N]
- "I have come to Navagam in the middle of a battle." [N]
- What had happened: the Matadars had refused the headman's office, and the headman had resigned.
  He congratulates them, then immediately turns it round: "If you have been offering resignations
  under pressure from anyone, I must ask you to withdraw them. Not only will that cause me no pain,
  but I will protect you against those who might try to force you to resign. This fight is based on
  truth. I want no victory with unworthy help." [N]
- The freedom to say no: "It will make no difference to me even if you do not resign." — "I shall
  regard you as brave men if you truthfully withdraw your resignations, and also if you truthfully
  adhere to them." [N]
- The test he sets them: "Before all these present here I remind you of this. Listen to me and if
  you so feel, admit truthfully that you do not have the strength to resign." [N]
- The verse: Tulsidas — "The way of the Raghus it has been ever, / Life may be lost but a pledge
  never." [N]
- Why the pressure was real: the Government's own reading of the campaign was that local workers
  were getting resignations "under the threat of social boycott". [M] That is exactly the danger
  Gandhi is naming.
- The village's own figures, in the same speech: population 1,000, "only one person is a habitual
  khadi-wearer and there is just one spinning-wheel to swear by". He asks for a vow to produce and
  wear khadi, which "will save Rs. 5,000 a year". [N]
- The wider claim: "When we win swaraj, even a scavenger will be free to take Vallabhbhai to
  task." [N]
- Watch: this is not a story about a form of words. The headman's post paid about five rupees a
  month [M], and resigning it was a real loss. Do not invent the headman, his name, his family or
  his reasons. Do not say the resignations were forced; say Gandhi knew they might be, and refused
  to profit from it.

## 6. The Walk Ended, the Work Began — SHORT

- The numbers: 12 March 1930, prayers, "looked at his watch and exactly at 6.30 A.M. commenced his
  march with seventy-eight volunteers". [M] About 385 km (240 miles) from Sabarmati to Dandi.
  [W] Twenty-five days on the road, of which they walked 22, with three days of Gandhi's silence;
  they passed "forty villages and towns". [M]
- Reached Dandi on 5 April. Sarojini Naidu was already there. [M]
- Why Dandi, in his words: "Dandi was chosen not by a man but by God. How otherwise could we have
  chosen for the battle-field of satyagraha such an out-of-the-way place — a place where no food
  grains are to be had, where there is scarcity of water, where thousands can assemble only with
  difficulty, walking ten miles from the railway station, and where if you are travelling on foot,
  you have to negotiate creeks full of slush and mud? The truth is that in this struggle we have to
  put up with suffering." [M]
- "Dandi should be a sacred ground for us, where we should utter no untruth, commit no sin.
  Everyone coming here should come with this devout feeling in his heart." [M]
- 6 April, 8.30 a.m.: he broke the law "by taking a lump of natural salt which was deposited in a
  small pit. Hundreds of persons witnessed this scene. Sarojini Naidu stood by Gandhiji's side."
  [M]
- What he said as he picked it up: "With this, I am shaking the foundations of the British Empire."
  [M] No policeman or excise officer was present. [M]
- The auction: "about two tolas of salt which was taken by him in the morning and also cleaned by
  him, was auctioned for Rs. 525/- to Seth Ranchhod Shodhan, a mill-owner of Ahmedabad." [M]
  Wikipedia has "a pinch of salt made by Gandhi himself sold for 1,600 rupees". [W] Two versions —
  if the story uses the auction at all, give Desai's figure and say the other account exists.
- What he told people to do next: manufacture salt wherever it is convenient, "to make use of it
  and to instruct the villagers to do so"; and, for those not making salt, "a vigorous propaganda
  for the boycott of foreign cloth and the use of Khadar". [M]
- The claim he had made before he began: "If the awakening of the people in the country is true and
  real, the salt law is as good as abolished." [M]
- What actually happened: the movement spread across India, and "the British government arrested
  over sixty thousand people by the end of the month". [W] The salt law was not repealed then.
- Watch: do not write "five million people at 5,000 meetings" on 6 April. It appears on mkgandhi.org
  once, with no source, and the figure is not credible. Do not give a crowd size at Dandi. Do not
  end with independence in 1947 as if the march caused it; the honest ending is that the walk
  stopped and the arrests began.

## Do not say

- Do not give the march a distance in both miles and kilometres without choosing: about 385 km, or
  about 240 miles. Wikipedia prints both 385 and 387 km in the same article; do not average them.
- The letter named 11 March; the march began 12 March. Say both, plainly.
- Do not call the letter an ultimatum, a threat or a demand for independence. It offered a meeting
  and offered to delay publication.
- Do not use "five million people at 5,000 meetings".
- Do not quote Bareja's sentence about Bhangis, and do not soften it into a summary.
- Do not date Patel's arrest; the sources opened here disagree.
- Do not invent the labourer, the barber, the headman, the villagers, the driver or the weather.
- Do not describe the Dandi coast, the hut, the beach or the sunrise. None of the opened sources do.
- No flag, no chakra, no tricolour in any scene. No "soldiers of freedom" as a metaphor of your own.
