Midnapore was a district in Bengal, in eastern India. Two of its areas were Tamluk and Contai. In 1942, they were farming country, and the farmers were angry. The war had brought new taxes, and rice was being sent away from the villages.

When Congress leaders were arrested in August 1942, Tamluk did not go quiet. There were strikes, meetings and marches outside government offices and police stations. The crowds were 5,000 to 10,000 people. Then, in September, the police and the villagers came face to face.

## Rice and bullets

On 8 September, about 2,500 people tried to stop mill owners from sending rice out of Danipur, near Mahisadal. Police fired. Three people died. Their bodies were burnt on one pyre and were not handed to the families. The next day, the District Magistrate raided the nearby villages and arrested 200 people. They were made to sit in the sun all day, without food. Only thirteen were sent to prison, for one to two years.

On 27 September, police went to a Congress office at Iswarpur, in Nandigram, to arrest two men. A crowd surrounded them. The police set the thatched office on fire and then fired to make a way out. Four people died, and twenty-five were hurt. The crowd followed the police for five kilometres to their camp. The next day, at a nearby village, two more people died in firing.

People in Tamluk began to cut the roads. They broke culverts, dug trenches and cut telegraph and telephone wires. Letters and orders could now travel only by messengers, who took long ways round. An officer wrote that the attacks came "so suddenly and simultaneously" that nothing could be done to prevent them.

## The twenty-ninth

On 29 September, about 20,000 people marched on the Tamluk police station. They came in five processions, from five different roads. One of them was led by Matangini Hazra. She was a widow, 73 years old, and she carried the national flag at the head of her line.

The police opened fire. Matangini Hazra was shot and died, still holding the flag. Nine other people died at Tamluk that day. Two were boys, Lakshminarayan Das, aged 13, and Purimadhab Parmanik, aged 14.

<aside class="note"><b>Her age</b>: The Indian History Collective gives her age as 73. A Commons picture of her is labelled 1870 to 1942, which would make her 72.</aside>

The same afternoon, a crowd of about 20,000 raided the Mahisadal police station, and thirteen people died in the firing there. At Sutahata, about 40,000 people took the station and disarmed the officer and constables. The crowd, led by the volunteers, set the building on fire. On the 30th, about 10,000 people went to Nandigram station. Four died on the spot and one later in hospital.

Between the 29th and the 30th, the civil government in Tamluk collapsed. The District Magistrate wrote that "large crowds of peasantry are roaming all over the countryside". An official said the crowd in Tamluk was "apparently not afraid of (police or military) firing".

## A government of their own

The Tamluk Congress committee went further than any other. It announced a government for the area. Its name was long: the Tamralipta Jatiya Sarkar, or Tamluk National Government. At its head was a leader called the Sarbadhinayak, and the post passed from one person to the next. The first was Satish Chandra Samanta, a veteran of the area.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The Indian History Collective says the Sarkar was announced on 17 September 1942, twelve days before the big march. Wikipedia gives 17 December 1942 as the day it was founded. The sources agree that it was running by the end of 1942.</aside>

The Sarkar had departments for law and order, money, schools, justice and defence. It ran its own courts. They moved from place to place, and the public could sit and watch. Sometimes 200 or 300 people were there. A person found guilty might be warned, fined or whipped. A newspaper called *Biplabi* became its voice. It had its own post. It also had a group called the Garam Dal, or "hot team", to act against people suspected of spying or cheating.

It had an army too. Young men and students formed the Bidyut Bahini, under Sushil Kumar Dhara. Women formed the Bhagini Sena, led by Subodhbala Kuiti. The training was in drill and in simple guerrilla tactics.

## The great storm

On 16 October 1942, a cyclone and tidal wave struck the coast. The leader Syama Prasad Mookerjee wrote to the Chief Minister that news of the damage, and even calls for help, were kept back for more than two weeks. A later study says the government also did not allow private groups to bring relief to Tamluk and Contai for a long time.

The Sarkar did what it could. Its workers collected clothing, rice and money and gave them out. Hoarders and profiteers were told to stop, and some were made to pay in money and paddy, which was shared among the needy. The men in its army camp lived for months on two small meals a day.

## What the officials said

Government leaders saw what had happened. The Governor of Bengal at the time called it a "large scale rebellion". The Chief Minister, Fazlul Haque, told the assembly that Midnapore had had a parallel government, with "its military and police forces and intelligence branch". It had "its jails", and in some cases, the people had "actually paralysed the government".

## The end

The Sarkar lasted about 21 months. Gandhi advised its leaders to wind it up, and they did. It was dissolved in August 1944. Later, 150 Congress workers linked with it gave themselves up to the government.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The Indian History Collective says the Sarkar was dissolved on 31 August 1944. Wikipedia says 8 August 1944.</aside>

Gandhi went to Tamluk in December 1945. He told the local leaders that the rising had been brave and spontaneous. But it had moved away from non-violence, he said, and without non-violence, swaraj could not be won.

The Sarkar was not wholly non-violent. It had an armed wing, an action squad and courts that could order a whipping. It also kept order, ran relief and gave justice to a whole region at a time when the government had left it to its fate.

## Why it matters

The story of Tamluk is not well known. One newspaper reporter wrote during Gandhi's visit: "Unfortunately, Bengal does not possess a Jawaharlal and that is perhaps why Calcutta does not know enough of Tamluk's glorious record."

Ajoy Mukherjee, one of its leaders, later became Chief Minister of West Bengal three times. In 2002, India issued a stamp for the Sarkar. It shows him and Matangini Hazra.
