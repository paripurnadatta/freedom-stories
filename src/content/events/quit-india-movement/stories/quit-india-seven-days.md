On Sunday, 9 August 1942, the people at the top of Congress were in prison. There was no one to give an order, and no one to say stop. Yet the country did not wait for orders.

In Bombay, the day of the arrests ended with at least nine people dead in clashes with the police. Strikes began in Ahmedabad and Poona. Within a day, the trouble had spread to Delhi, the United Provinces and Bihar. In the first two days of street fighting, the police fired and killed twenty-four people.

## Shops, mills and schools

In Ahmedabad, 8,000 mill workers walked out of their jobs. Cotton mills and factories closed in Bombay and Poona. Students left their schools and joined the marches. For a week, business stopped in Bombay, Ahmedabad, Delhi, Bangalore and Calcutta.

The strikes reached the roads. Private cars were not allowed to go on unless somebody in the car wore a Gandhi cap.

## The rails

Many people turned to the railways and the telegraph. Crowds cut electric and telephone wires and pulled up railway tracks. In mid-August, people in Bihar stopped the trains by lying across the tracks. British air force planes machine-gunned the unarmed men on the line.

<aside class="note"><b>One source</b>: The report of aircraft firing on people lying on the tracks comes from the Swarthmore database alone.</aside>

## The firings

The government answered with force. The firings were many. In Delhi, the police fired on 47 separate occasions on 11 and 12 August. In the United Provinces, there were 29 firings between 9 and 21 August, and 76 people died. In the Central Provinces, in the first three weeks, the police killed 64 people and arrested 11,088.

<aside class="note"><b>Firings, not deaths</b>: The number 47 for Delhi counts times the police fired, not people who died.</aside>

## Not everyone, everywhere

It would be wrong to say that the whole country rose at once. The movement was strongest in some places and weaker in others. One count covers 9 August to 21 September. It lists 550 post offices and 250 railway stations attacked. It lists 70 police stations destroyed. Telegraph wires were cut about 2,500 times. The worst of it was in Bihar.

## Why it matters

The Quit India resolution had asked every person to be "his own guide". In that first week, many people took it at its word. They did it without a plan and without a leader. That is why the story of the movement is also the story of what went wrong with it, which another story on this site tells.
