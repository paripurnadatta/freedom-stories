When the Kakori judgment came, in April 1927, many people in India were shocked. Three young men had been sentenced to death. A fourth, Ashfaqulla Khan, would soon follow.

The whole country was "aghast", says the National Book Trust book on the Kakori prisoners. Leaders protested, and members of the Central Legislature wrote to the Viceroy. This is the story of a plea that did not work, and of the people who made it anyway.

## Who the four were

Ram Prasad Bismil was a poet and the organiser of the group. Ashfaqulla Khan was his close friend and also a poet. Rajendra Lahiri was a university student of history. Roshan Singh was older than the rest. He had been a village schoolteacher.

The people who spoke up for them came from different parties and different faiths.

## The first pleas

After the Chief Court at Lucknow kept the death sentences in August 1927, the next step was mercy. A hanging could still be stopped if a higher authority agreed to a lighter sentence.

<aside class="note"><b>Commute</b>: to change a punishment into a lighter one. A death sentence can be commuted to life in prison.</aside>

Some members of the provincial council wrote first to the Governor of the United Provinces, Sir William Morris. He refused.

when did the Chief Court give its decision? The court history says 11 August 1927. English Wikipedia and the Hindi account say 22 August. The original judgment was not seen, so both dates are shown.

## The Viceroy and the memorial

The next plea went higher, to the Viceroy, Lord Irwin. It was a memorandum, or formal request, from Madan Mohan Malaviya, signed by 78 members of the Central Legislature. Three sources give that number.

Among those who signed were Malaviya himself, Muhammad Ali Jinnah, N. C. Kelkar, Lala Lajpat Rai and Govind Ballabh Pant.

Think about that list. Malaviya and Jinnah belonged to different faiths and different parties. On this one matter, their names stood on the same page.

Other well-known people also spoke for the accused, including Motilal Nehru and Jawaharlal Nehru. Some writers say that Mahatma Gandhi asked for mercy too. The two sources that mention this say it carefully, "reportedly" and "claimed", so it stays a maybe.

The Viceroy said no. A delegation of five people, led by Malaviya, then went to see the authorities. It also failed.

## A letter from the death cell

On 9 September 1927, Ram Prasad Bismil wrote a letter from Gorakhpur jail to Malaviya. Two sources mention it, but its words have not come down to us.

## The last door: London

The highest court that Indians could reach was the Privy Council in London. It was also possible to ask the King-Emperor himself.

<aside class="note"><b>Privy Council</b>: a group of senior advisers in London. In those days, it was the last court of appeal for people in British India.</aside>

The lawyer Mohan Lal Saxena wrote the petition. He sent it to a man in England named S. L. Polak, who was to hand it in. The final appeal was dated 16 September 1927, and it went to both the Privy Council and the King-Emperor, through Henry Polak.

It was turned down.

The officials in London called Bismil a dangerous professional criminal. That was the government's view, as one source reports it. The other sources give no reason.

## News in the jail

Rajendra Lahiri is said to have written a letter to his friends on 14 December 1927. In it, he says that the day before, he heard that the Privy Council had turned down his appeal. "You have done everything to save my life but it seems that the country needs my life," the book gives as his words.

Roshan Singh is said to have written on 13 December, telling his friend not to feel sorry for him.

these letters could not be checked against the originals. They come from a book written in 2009, more than eighty years later. They are given as that book gives them, not as proven words.

## What the appeal did to the others

The plea was for four lives. But the Chief Court's decision had also changed the lives of other accused men. The judges made the sentences heavier for five of them: Jogesh Chandra Chatterji, Suresh Chandra Bhattacharji, Vishnu Saran Dublish, Govind Charan Kar and Pranavesh Kumar Chatterji.

Some of the accused had been defended in court by well-known lawyers. Others had no lawyer of their own. C. B. Gupta and H. N. Misra spoke up for those who had nobody.

## The days in December

After the plea failed, the British government fixed the dates. The four men were to be hanged by 16 December, says one source. English Wikipedia says by 19 December.

Rajendra Lahiri was hanged on 17 December 1927 in the Gonda jail. The other three were hanged on the morning of 19 December. Bismil was hanged at Gorakhpur. Roshan Singh was hanged at Allahabad. Ashfaqulla Khan was hanged at Faizabad.

why was Lahiri hanged two days early? The National Book Trust book says the government feared that his friends might free him from the jail. It says Chandrashekhar Azad and Manmathnath Gupta had come to Gonda for that purpose, and that someone leaked the plan. Another source gives no reason at all. No government record was seen, so it stays an unproven story.

<aside class="note"><b>A slip in one book</b>: the National Book Trust book says Ashfaqulla was hanged in 1929. The same book says Bismil, hanged on the same day, died in 1927. All the other sources say 1927.</aside>

## After the hangings

The people of Gorakhpur took Bismil's body around the city, and people showered flowers and money on it, says the National Book Trust book. After the four were hanged, their group changed its name and its aims. It became the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association.

<aside class="note"><b>Why "Association"?</b> Before September 1928 the group was called the HRA, the Hindustan Republican Association. After that it was the HSRA, with "Socialist" added.</aside>

## Why it matters

A plea can fail and still mean something. The 78 names did not save the four men.

But they show that in 1927, people who disagreed about nearly everything were able to agree on one thing. They felt that these young men, whatever they had done, should not be hanged.

The government saw it differently. According to the Hindi account, officials in London thought Bismil was too dangerous to be spared. Both views were real, and both belong in the story.
