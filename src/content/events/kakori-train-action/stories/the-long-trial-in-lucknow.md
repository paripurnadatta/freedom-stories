On the night of 9 August 1925, a train was stopped near Kakori and a government safe was broken open. A few weeks later, the police began to arrest people all over northern India.

What happened to those people in court is a long story. It has a judge, a famous prosecutor, many lawyers, and two separate trials. It also has a lot of numbers, and the old records do not always agree on them. This story tells you what we know, and where the sources part ways.

## Why the police thought it was a plot

At first, the police had a robbery to solve. Then they noticed something. The pistols and bullets found at several crime scenes looked alike. And holding up a train was very unusual.

So the police decided this was not one robbery. It was a plan. The Allahabad High Court's history of the Lucknow court says a senior detective found a large secret group. Its members were mostly young. Some were teenagers and students. Their aim, in the court's words, was to set up a "Federal Republic of the United States of India" by armed revolution.

The police also found papers. One was a leaflet against what it called official terrorism. Another was the rules and aims of the Hindustan Republican Association, the HRA.

<aside class="note"><b>Conspiracy</b>: a secret plan made by several people together. In law, you can be punished for the plan itself, even for parts you did not carry out.</aside>

## The arrests

Nine men were arrested on one day, 26 September 1925. Among them was Roshan Singh. Ram Prasad Bismil was arrested at Shahjahanpur a month later, on 26 October.

Some of the men sought by the police were never caught. Chandrashekhar Azad is the best known of them.

Altogether, twenty-four people were charged. The court history says they faced two main charges. The first was plotting to end British rule. The second was taking part in robberies in which people were killed. There were four of these robberies in all. Kakori was the last.

## Where, and how long?

The trial of twenty-one of the accused was held in Lucknow, before a special judge named Mr Hamilton. One of the accused was freed before the trial began. Two more, Ashfaqulla Khan and Sachindra Nath Bakshi, were still on the run. They were tried later.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: When did the trial begin? The National Book Trust book says the hearing began on 4 January 1926, in a building called the Ring Theatre. It says that building is now Lucknow's General Post Office. Wikipedia says the trial of the twenty-one began on 1 May 1926. The court history says only that the trial lasted "about 8 months". The judgment came on 6 April 1927. From 1 May 1926, that is almost eleven months. So at least one of these numbers is off.</aside>

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: A Hindi account names a special judge called Ainuddin. The court history, the National Book Trust book and English Wikipedia all name Hamilton. We follow the three that agree.</aside>

## The lawyers

The government hired a famous lawyer, Pandit Jagat Narain, to argue its case. The accused had far less money. The court history says two Lucknow lawyers, C. B. Gupta and H. N. Misra, took an interest in the case from start to end. They stood up for accused men who had nobody else.

C. B. Gupta later became the Chief Minister of Uttar Pradesh. English Wikipedia says other lawyers also defended the accused, among them Govind Ballabh Pant.

A Hindi account adds a few scenes from the courtroom. It says Bismil asked to argue his own case in English, but the court refused and gave him a lawyer. It says he once asked the prosecutor why he called the accused "employees" of anyone. These scenes are told in only one of our sources. We cannot say how exactly they happened.

## The judgment

On 6 April 1927, Mr Hamilton gave his decision. The court history sums it up like this:

- Two men were found not guilty.
- Eighteen men were found guilty of the plot.
- Nine of them were also found guilty of the robberies.
- Three were sentenced to death: Ram Prasad Bismil, Rajendra Lahiri and Roshan Singh.
- Fifteen got prison terms of different lengths.
- One man was found unfit to stand trial. His case was put off.

Sachindranath Sanyal was sentenced to life, and Manmathnath Gupta to fourteen years. The shortest sentences were three years.

The National Book Trust book prints words it says are from the judgment. About Lahiri, the judge said that he was one of the leaders, and that he was "responsible for the death of Ahmed Ali". About Roshan Singh, the judge said he was the leader in the Bamrauli robbery, and that two people had said he carried a pistol that day.

<aside class="note"><b>Bamrauli</b>: the first of the four robberies in the case. It happened on Christmas night in 1924, in the Pilibhit district. It was not at Kakori.</aside>

## The second trial

Ashfaqulla Khan was arrested in Delhi on 7 December 1926. Bakshi was caught later. Both were tried in a separate case before another judge, Mr J. R. W. Bennett. Ashfaqulla was sentenced to death. Bakshi was sentenced to be sent away for life. Ashfaqulla appealed. Bakshi did not.

This is why you may read different numbers. Three men were sentenced to death in the first trial. A fourth, Ashfaqulla, was sentenced in the second. The National Book Trust book says all four were sentenced together. The court history says otherwise. We follow the court.

## The appeal

Fifteen of the convicted men appealed to the Chief Court in Lucknow. One later gave up his appeal. The government also asked the court to give six of them heavier sentences.

Two judges heard the appeal. They were the Chief Judge, Sir Louis Stuart, and Justice Raza. They agreed with the trial judge. They kept all four death sentences. They made the sentences heavier for five of the men.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The court history gives the date of the appeal judgment as 11 August 1927. English Wikipedia and the Hindi account both say 22 August 1927. We have no court record in front of us to settle it, so we show both.</aside>

## What came after

Bismil, Lahiri, Roshan Singh and Ashfaqulla were hanged in December 1927. Some of the other accused came out of jail years later and lived long lives. The court history notes that a few of them became well-known citizens of free India.

## Why it matters

The Kakori trial was one of the biggest trials of its time. Lawyers, leaders and newspapers all followed it.

It is also a good lesson in reading old records. Judges, historians and writers of books each remember it a little differently. Dates move around. Names are spelled in different ways. Some details come from only one writer.

When you read history, it helps to ask: who says so, and does anyone else say the same? That is what we have tried to do here.
