Most stories about Kakori are told by people who admired the revolutionaries. This one is told from a different place: the courtroom.

The High Court at Allahabad has published a history of its bench in Lucknow. One chapter describes the Kakori case, using the judges' own findings. Here is what it says.

## The train

On 9 August 1925, a train called the 8 Down, of the East Indian Railway, left Kakori railway station. Kakori is about eight miles from Lucknow.

Soon after it left, someone pulled the communication cord. That was the alarm chain that passengers could pull in an emergency. The train stopped.

<aside class="note"><b>Brake van</b>: the guard's carriage on a train.</aside>

The government's money travelled in a safe in the brake van. The men broke the safe open and took the money.

## The passenger

A lawyer named Ahmad Ali was on the train. He got down from his carriage, even though he had been warned not to. He was shot dead.

<aside class="note"><b>Who fired?</b> The court history does not say whose bullet killed him. Wikipedia says it was fired by accident by Manmathnath Gupta, one of the young men.</aside>

## How much money?

You would think that a court, of all places, would know exactly how much was stolen. The court history gives the figure as 4,553 rupees, 3 annas and 6 pies.

But the police report written two days after the robbery gives a different figure: 4,601 rupees, 15 annas and 6 pies. And Wikipedia says about 8,000 rupees.

<aside class="note"><b>Three numbers</b>: the court history, the police report and Wikipedia all give different amounts. None of them explains why, and we do not know which is right.</aside>

## The papers the police found

The police searched for the men behind the robbery. Among the papers they seized was a printed pamphlet. It set out the aims and rules of a secret group, the Hindustan Republican Association.

## The trial

Twenty-four people were put on trial. A special judge, Mr Hamilton, heard the case. On 6 April 1927, he gave his judgment.

Eighteen men were found guilty of plotting to wage war against the King, and of the robbery. Two were set free. Three leaders were sentenced to death: Ram Prasad Bismil, Rajendra Lahiri and Roshan Singh. Fifteen others were sent to prison.

Two more men, Ashfaqulla Khan and Sachindra Nath Bakshi, were tried separately, in a second case, by another judge, Mr Bennett. Ashfaqulla was sentenced to death. Bakshi was sentenced to transportation for life.

<aside class="note"><b>Transportation for life</b>: a punishment of being sent away to prison for life.</aside>

## The appeal

The men appealed to the Chief Court in Lucknow. Two judges heard the appeal: Sir Louis Stuart, the Chief Judge, and Justice Raza.

On 11 August 1927, almost exactly two years after the robbery, they gave their decision. They agreed with the trial court. All four death sentences stayed. And for five of the other men, they made the punishment even heavier.

Four months later, in December 1927, Bismil, Ashfaqulla, Lahiri and Roshan Singh were hanged.

## Why it matters

A court record does not tell us how the young men felt, or why they did it. For that, we have their own letters and poems.

But it tells us other things carefully: the train, the alarm cord, the safe, the man who died, and the sentences. Reading it next to the revolutionaries' own stories gives a fuller picture of what happened at Kakori.
