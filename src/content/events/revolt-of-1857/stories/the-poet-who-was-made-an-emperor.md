In May 1857 an old man was in the palace at Delhi. Soldiers had come in from Meerut. They wanted him to be their leader.

He was the Mughal emperor, Bahadur Shah. He was also a poet. His pen-name, Zafar, means "victory".

## An emperor without an empire

Zafar became emperor in 1837, after his father Akbar II died. The Company had already sent his rival, Mirza Jahangir, into exile, and that cleared the way. One account says he was 62. The Mughal empire had shrunk to Delhi and the land near it. The East India Company paid him a pension and collected the taxes of Delhi.

So the emperor ruled little. But his court was full of poets. Mirza Ghalib was there, and so were Zauq, Momin Khan Momin and Daagh Dehlvi. Zafar wrote Urdu verse himself, and a large collection of his poems survives. It is called the Kulliyyat-i-Zafar.

## May 1857

On 12 May 1857 sepoys came to the palace. Zafar did not want to take their side. The account says he was indecisive. He gave way when they told him they could not win without him.

<aside class="note"><b>Two views of him</b>: Some pages say the sepoys forced him. Others say he gave the rebellion public support and accepted their demands. He was over eighty, and the pages do not agree on how willing he was.</aside>

He made his eldest son, Mirza Mughal, commander-in-chief. Mirza Mughal had little military experience, and the sepoys did not accept him. Later the emperor named a better soldier, Bakht Khan, to command. Meanwhile, outside the city, Gujjar herders began to collect their own tolls on the roads.

On 16 May, 52 British prisoners were killed in the palace grounds. Zafar protested, according to one account. Hakim Ahsanullah Khan, his chief adviser, later spoke against him at the trial. But at his trial the court held that he had agreed to the killing.

## The war around him

Zafar never saw the Ridge. He stayed in the palace while the war went on outside the walls. About ten cavalry and fifteen infantry regiments of sepoys came to Delhi in June and July. They attacked the British camp on the Ridge again and again, and they were beaten back each time.

Meanwhile the British brought up more men. John Nicholson arrived in August. The big guns came in early September. The attack on the city began on 14 September, and the fighting inside the walls went on for about a week.

## The fall

By September it was clear that Delhi would be lost. Zafar went to Humayun's Tomb. British troops under Major William Hodson surrounded it. Hodson captured the emperor on 20 or 21 September. Hodson had promised that the lives of Zafar and his wife Zinat Mahal would be spared.

Next day Hodson took three of the emperor's family: his sons Mirza Mughal and Mirza Khizr Sultan, and his grandson Mirza Abu Bakr. He shot them near the Delhi Gate. The place is called the Khooni Darwaza, the Bloody Gate.

<aside class="note"><b>The dates</b>: One page puts the surrender on 21 September and the shooting "the next day". Another gives the shooting as 21 September. The pages differ by a day.</aside>

Zafar was kept at Zinat Mahal's house in the Lal Kuan area of the city. By then British troops held the palace, the Jama Masjid and the fort of Selimgarh. Several hundred prisoners and suspected rebels were hanged in the city without a trial.

## The trial

The trial began on 27 January 1858. It was held in the Diwan-i-Khas, a hall in the Red Fort. Zafar was ill. He had to be helped to walk.

He faced a Military Commission, with Major Harriott as the prosecutor. The charges were waging war against the British, taking up the powers of a ruler, and being an accessory to the murder of Christians. Other pages list them as rebellion, treason and murder. In the end the trial ran 21 days, with 19 hearings and 21 witnesses. His own chief adviser, Hakim Ahsanullah Khan, spoke against him. In return Ahsanullah Khan was pardoned.

On 9 March 1858 the judges found him guilty. They did not give the death sentence, because Hodson had promised him his life. He was sentenced "to be transported for the remainder of his days." The trial lasted from 27 January to 9 March, and Zafar was ill when it began.

<aside class="note"><b>Transported</b>: sent to live far away, as a punishment, under guard.</aside>

## Exile

Zafar's journey took time. The sentence was not carried out for about seven months, while the British decided where to send him. On 7 October 1858 he left Delhi in a bullock cart with his wives, two of his sons and some servants.

They went by road and river to Allahabad and Calcutta. His wife Zinat Mahal and other family members went with him. They sailed from Diamond Harbour on 4 December 1858 in a ship called the Magoera. They reached Rangoon on 10 December.

In Rangoon the family lived in a teak house under British watch. They were not allowed pen, ink or paper. A visitor needed a pass for each day. A British record called him "the old and the enfeebled Ex-King".

## Death

Zafar died in Rangoon on 7 November 1862. He had a paralysis of the throat. He was buried in an unmarked grave.

<aside class="note"><b>How old was he?</b> One page says he was about 87 when he died. Another says about 83. His birth year is not agreed.</aside>

<aside class="note"><b>The grave</b>: One page says he was buried near the Shwedagon Pagoda, and that a shrine was built after his tomb was restored in 1991. Another says only that the grave was unmarked.</aside>

His tomb in Rangoon was restored in 1991, and a shrine was built there.

A verse is often quoted as his epitaph. Scholars dispute whether he wrote one line of it. One of them says that a poet named Simab Akbarabadi wrote it.

## Why it matters

Zafar did not plan a war. He did not have an army of his own, or the money to pay one. For four months the rebels carried his name. Many of them fought for a throne that had little power left.

The British made an example of him. He was the last of the Mughal emperors, and he died in Rangoon, far from Delhi.
