Kanpur was called Cawnpore by the British. In 1857 it was an important army station on the Grand Trunk Road.

This is a hard story, and the people who tell it do not agree on much. It is told here with care.

## The entrenchment

General Hugh Wheeler commanded the British garrison. In early June there were about 900 people in Kanpur who looked to him for safety. About 300 were soldiers. About 300 were women and children. About 150 were merchants and other civilians.

Wheeler did not move them into the magazine in the north of the city, which had stronger walls and more supplies. He chose an entrenchment in the south. Historians call the choice controversial.

On 5 June the sepoys of the 2nd Bengal Cavalry rose before dawn. Other units followed over the next day. About 150 sepoys stayed loyal to Wheeler.

Nana Sahib was the adopted heir of Baji Rao II, the last Peshwa. The British had refused him his father's pension. He went to the magazine and treasury. Then he joined the rebels.

## Three weeks

The attack on the entrenchment began on 6 June. On 13 June a fire destroyed the hospital and most of the medicine. By 21 June the British had lost about a third of their number. A big assault on 23 June failed, and the attackers lost many men.

On 24 June Nana Sahib sent a note offering safe passage to Allahabad. Wheeler first said no. The next day a signed note came, and the British were divided. In the end Wheeler agreed to leave.

## 27 June

About 500 people walked from the entrenchment to Satichaura Ghat, a landing on the river. Boats had been arranged for them. The boats were stuck in shallow water.

Firing broke out on the riverbank. Only a few boats got away. Most of the people in the others were killed. About 120 women and children were taken prisoner.

<aside class="note"><b>Who gave the signal?</b> Some say a bugle sounded. Others say three shots were fired. One account names Tatya Tope. Others say the boatmen started the boats. There is no firm proof that Nana Sahib ordered what followed.</aside>

## July

The prisoners were kept in a small house called the Bibighar. Conditions were hard, and some fell ill. A British force under General Henry Havelock was marching up from Allahabad. It won battles at Fatehpur on 12 July and at Aong on 15 July.

On 15 July the women and children in the Bibighar were killed. Historians give a figure of about 200. The British took Kanpur back the next day.

<aside class="note"><b>Who gave the order?</b> The accounts differ. One says the Begum Hussaini Khanum and her helpers did it. Another says sepoys refused and Tatya Tope threatened them. A third says Nana Sahib left the place so that he would not have to see it.</aside>

After this, the British shouted "Remember Cawnpore!" as a war cry. Their reprisals were severe. They hanged or blew from cannon most of the sepoys they captured. One historian thinks the reprisals were justified. Another thinks they caused the killings.

## Tatya Tope

Tatya Tope was born Ramchandra Panduranga Yewalkar on 16 February 1814 at Yeola, near Nasik. He served Nana Sahib. After the British retook Kanpur, he fought on. In November 1857 he tried to take the city again and was beaten by Sir Colin Campbell in the Second Battle of Cawnpore.

He later joined Rani Lakshmibai and took Gwalior Fort. When the British came, he escaped to Rajputana. He went on fighting as a guerrilla in the jungles of central India.

## The trial

Tatya Tope was put on trial. He admitted the charges but said that he was answerable only to his master, the Peshwa. He was hanged on 18 April 1859 at Sipri.

<aside class="note"><b>Was he hanged?</b> In 1957 a writer named Herdikar wrote a chapter titled "Tantya Tope was not hanged". A newspaper letter said someone else had been hanged in his place. The page gives no proof either way.</aside>

Nana Sahib was never captured. He vanished. By 1859 he was said to have fled to Nepal, but nobody could confirm it.

## Why it matters

Kanpur is a place where both sides suffered, and each side remembers its own dead. The British built memorials at the Bibighar well. Later, others argued about whether and where they should stand.

Historians still ask who gave the orders. Anyone who tells the story should say how much is not known.
