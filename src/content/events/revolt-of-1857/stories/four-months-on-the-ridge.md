On 11 May 1857 the sepoys from Meerut reached Delhi. They came in through the Rajghat Gate. By the end of the next day the old Mughal emperor, Bahadur Shah, had given them his support.

Delhi was now in the hands of the rebels. The British had to take it back. The siege that followed lasted until September.

## The magazine

Inside the city stood the magazine, where the army kept its gunpowder and guns. A few British officers held it. When their ammunition ran out, they blew the magazine up so that the rebels could not use it. Six of the nine officers lived. The Main Guard fell. The surviving British people ran towards Karnal.

On 16 May sepoys and palace servants killed 52 British prisoners and people who had been hiding. The deed took place under a peepul tree. It was a hard day for Delhi, and the British did not forget it.

## The army that came to the Ridge

A British force set out from Ambala. Its commander, General Anson, died of cholera on 27 May. General Barnard took over. On 7 June he joined a column from Meerut at Alipur. The next day his men won the Battle of Badli-ki-Serai and climbed onto the Delhi Ridge.

<aside class="note"><b>The Ridge</b>: a long hill of hard rock, about 18 metres high, running from near the Kabul Gate to the river Yamuna. It looked down on the walls of Delhi.</aside>

The Ridge was a good place to hold. A canal behind the camp gave drinking water. The most dangerous post was a building called Hindu Rao's house. The 60th Rifles and the Sirmoor Gurkhas held it.

But the camp was not comfortable. The British burned the sepoys' old barracks, and that left the men in tents. They sat there through the heat of June and the monsoon rain. Sickness spread. Barnard died of cholera on 5 July, and Archdale Wilson took his place.

A dawn attack planned for 13 June was called off after the orders became confused. On 14 July Neville Chamberlain was badly wounded in a sortie.

## Attacks from inside the city

The rebels were not waiting quietly. Ten cavalry and fifteen infantry regiments reached Delhi in June and July. They attacked the Ridge on 19 June, 23 June and 3 July. Each time they were driven back.

Bahadur Shah named his son Mirza Mughal as commander. The sepoys did not accept him, because he had no army experience. A better soldier came from Bareilly with a large party. He was Bakht Khan, an old artillery officer. The emperor made him commander-in-chief. Bakht Khan put the city's money in order and tried to renew the soldiers' effort.

<aside class="note"><b>Not known</b>: The page on the siege does not say which day Bakht Khan arrived.</aside>

## The assault

On 14 August John Nicholson came to the Ridge with reinforcements. On 25 August he won the Battle of Najafgarh. In early September the heavy guns, called the siege train, arrived. Batteries began to fire on the walls from 6 September.

The British guns were 15 heavy 24-pounders, 20 18-pounders and 25 mortars and howitzers.

By now the British and allied force numbered about 9,000. About 3,000 were European soldiers. About 6,000 were Sikhs, Punjabis and Gurkhas.

At dawn on 14 September the attack began. It went in as four columns. Each column held roughly 850 to 1,000 men, with 200 skirmishers and a cavalry reserve. The third column aimed at the Kashmiri Gate. Two engineer officers, Lieutenants Duncan Home and Philip Salkeld, led a small party to the gate. They laid bags of gunpowder against it, close up. Several men fell while trying to light the fuse.

The charge blew the gate open. Bugler Robert Hawthorne sounded the signal. The column rushed in. All three men, Home, Salkeld and Hawthorne, later received the Victoria Cross. In all, 29 Victoria Crosses were given for the siege.

<aside class="note"><b>Two timings</b>: The page says the assault was set for 3 a.m. on 14 September. It also says it was to start at dawn.</aside>

## Street by street

The fight inside the city lasted about a week. Nicholson led the first three columns. When the fourth column fell into disorder, he pushed towards the Burn Bastion through narrow lanes. Two rushes failed. In the third he was badly wounded. He died on 23 September.

General Wilson had wanted to pull back. Nicholson, it is reported, was ready to shoot him if he gave the order. Others persuaded Wilson to hold what had been won.

On 16 September the British took the magazine. On 18 and 19 September Bahadur Shah left the palace. British troops took the palace, the Jama Masjid and the fort of Selimgarh. Hodson captured the emperor on 20 or 21 September, and the city was declared taken on 21 September.

<aside class="note"><b>Nicholson</b>: One page says he was mortally wounded in the assault and died on 23 September. Another says he was killed in the fighting near the Kabul Gate. The first is the more detailed.</aside>

## The cost

The siege cost 1,254 killed and 4,493 wounded. The final assault alone cost 3,817 casualties. Of these, 992 were killed, 2,795 were wounded and 30 were missing. More than 1,600 of the casualties in the assault were Indian soldiers fighting for the British. The page gives the rebels' losses as over 5,000, but says the number is unofficial.

<aside class="note"><b>Do not mix the numbers</b>: The figure 1,254 killed covers the whole siege. The figure 992 killed covers only the last assault.</aside>

When the fighting ended, the city suffered. Several hundred prisoners and suspected rebels were hanged without trial. Citizens were driven out to camps outside the walls. Looting went on for four days.

## The war elsewhere

Delhi was only one front. At Kanpur, in June, a British force was besieged and gave way. At Lucknow the defenders of the Residency held out for about ninety days. In March 1858 Jhansi fell to Hugh Rose. Each place has its own story on this site.

## Why it matters

Delhi was the heart of the revolt. As long as the rebels held it, the emperor's name gave them a flag. When it fell, the movement lost that centre, and the revolt never recovered it.

Both sides fought hard. Sikh, Punjabi and Gurkha soldiers stood with the British on the Ridge. Sepoys, townspeople and a veteran gunner stood on the other side. The people of Delhi paid for the war, whichever side they were on.
