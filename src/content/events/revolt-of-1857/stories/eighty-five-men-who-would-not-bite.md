On 24 April 1857, ninety sepoys were paraded at Meerut. They were cavalrymen of the Bengal Army. Their commander, Colonel Carmichael Smyth, gave them an order. They were to fire the new cartridges.

They refused.

<aside class="note"><b>Sepoy</b>: an Indian soldier in the army of the East India Company. The Company was a trading firm that had come to rule much of India. It kept its own army.</aside>

## The cartridge

The new cartridge was made for the Enfield rifle. It was a paper tube that held the gunpowder and the bullet. A soldier had to bite the end of the paper open before he loaded.

The paper was greased. In early 1857 a rumour spread through the army. The grease, people said, was made from cow fat or pig fat. The rumour made many sepoys angry and afraid.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The historian Kim Wagner says there is little direct proof that greased cartridges were handed out before the revolt began. The Company did order ungreased cartridges. That did not calm the fear.</aside>

## More than a cartridge

The cartridge was the spark. The dry wood had been piling up for years.

The Company had over 300,000 sepoys in 1857. It had only about 50,000 British soldiers. Many of the sepoys were high-caste men from Awadh and Bihar. They were proud of their place in the army.

They had complaints. A sepoy earned about seven rupees a month. An Indian soldier could not rise above the rank of subedar, however well he served. A new law of 25 July 1856 said that new recruits must be ready to serve overseas. The sepoys feared it would one day be used on them. Many Hindu soldiers worried that a sea voyage would spoil their caste.

The land around them was changing too. In 1853 the Company took over Jhansi, because its Raja had died without a son of his own. In 1856 it took over Awadh, saying the Nawab ruled badly. Thousands of the Nawab's soldiers and servants lost their living. The sepoys of the Bengal Army came in large numbers from Awadh.

Other troubles pressed on ordinary people. Land tax was high. Farmers sank into debt and some lost their land. In 1852 the Company's Inam Commission took away nearly 20,000 estates that had been held rent-free. Missionaries were active. Reforms seemed to some conservative people like meddling in their religion. Examples were the ban on sati in 1829 and the law of 1856 that let widows marry again.

Lord Canning, the Governor-General, also announced that the heirs of Bahadur Shah, the Mughal emperor in Delhi, would lose the title of king.

Soldiers had risen before. There were mutinies at Vellore in 1806 and Barrackpore in 1824. In Sindh and the Punjab, sepoys protested between 1844 and 1850 when their extra pay was cut. At Vellore over 800 sepoys died.

<aside class="note"><b>Two counts</b>: One page gives about 232,000 sepoys and 45,000 British soldiers for 1856. Another gives over 300,000 and about 50,000 for 1857. Both agree that Indian soldiers were far more numerous.</aside>

## Warnings


There were signs of trouble before Meerut. On 26 February 1857 the 19th Bengal Native Infantry objected to the cartridges. The regiment was disbanded on 31 March.

On 29 March, at Barrackpore, a sepoy named Mangal Pandey attacked British officers. He was hanged on 8 April. The 34th Bengal Native Infantry, his regiment, was disbanded in disgrace. Seven of its ten companies were sent away on 6 May, as a punishment for the whole regiment. Earlier, at Dum Dum, sepoys had refused the cartridges and faced a court-martial. ## The parade at Meerut

Now the men of Meerut stood in their turn before the colonel. There were eighty-five of them from the 3rd Bengal Light Cavalry. After their refusal, they were taken before a court-martial.

<aside class="note"><b>Court-martial</b>: a trial held by the army for a soldier.</aside>

The court found them guilty. The men were stripped of their uniforms. Most were sentenced to ten years in prison. They were put in the jail at Meerut.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: One page says ninety men were paraded, yet only eighty-five were tried. It does not explain the difference. One page says all eighty-five were given ten years. Another says most of them were.</aside>

Meerut was a big station. On 10 May the garrison held 2,357 Indian sepoys and 2,038 British soldiers. It also had 12 guns.

## 10 May

On 10 May the imprisoned troopers were set free. The kotwal of Meerut, a man named Dhan Singh, opened the prison gates. The freed men, and others who had been held there, declared themselves free.

<aside class="note"><b>Kotwal</b>: the chief of the town police.</aside>

The sepoys rose. They attacked the British officers and officials in the station. Four British men, eight women and eight children died that day. About fifty Indian civilians were killed too. Soon the sepoys controlled Meerut.

<aside class="note"><b>Who was Dhan Singh?</b> One page calls him Kotwal Dhan Singh and says he opened the prison. Another page links a Dhan Singh Gurjar with riots among the townspeople. It is not certain that these are the same man.</aside>

Then the sepoys left the city. They marched to Delhi, about forty miles away. They reached it on 11 May. There they met the old Mughal emperor, Bahadur Shah. The next stories show what came of that meeting.

## What followed

From Meerut the revolt spread across northern and central India. Soldiers, princes, farmers and townspeople took part, each for their own reasons. It lasted until 1 November 1858, when the British offered pardon to rebels who had not been involved in murder. Fighting formally ended on 8 July 1859.

Not every soldier joined. Of 74 Bengal Native Infantry regiments, 54 mutinied. Sikh and Pashtun troops in the Punjab fought for the British. On 1 April 1858 more than 80,000 Indian soldiers of the Bengal Army were still loyal. There were three mutinies among the 29 regiments of Bombay. In Madras there were none.

The cost was very large. About 6,000 British people died, including civilians. For Indians, one estimate says that "probably" 800,000 died. That number covers fighting, famine and disease together, and it is a rough guess.

After the revolt the East India Company was ended. The Government of India Act of 2 August 1858 handed India to the British Crown.

<aside class="note"><b>What shall we call it?</b> The British called it the Mutiny. In 1909 V. D. Savarkar called it the First War of Independence. The British writer John Seeley said it was a "wholly un-patriotic and selfish Sepoy Mutiny". Historians still argue about the name.</aside>

## Why it matters

Meerut was not the plan of one clever leader. A rumour, a harsh sentence and a jail break did it. Sepoys who had lost their rank, their pay or their kings felt they had nothing left to lose.

Each year 10 May is a local holiday in Meerut. The city remembers the day the eighty-five were set free.
