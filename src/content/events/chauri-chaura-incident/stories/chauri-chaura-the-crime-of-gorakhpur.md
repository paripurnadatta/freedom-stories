In the first week of February 1922, Gandhi was in Gujarat. He was getting ready for the biggest step of the Non-Cooperation movement. The people of Bardoli, a district near Surat, were about to refuse to pay land tax to the government. That was called mass civil disobedience. If it worked, it could be copied across India.

Gandhi had already written to the Viceroy, Lord Reading, to tell him what was coming. The challenge had been sent. The movement was ready.

Then news arrived from Gorakhpur, far away in the east. A crowd had set fire to a police station, and policemen had died.

## The first shock

Gandhi wrote about it on 12 February in his Gujarati paper, *Navajivan*. He wrote in a hurry, on his way to Bombay, because his friend Madan Mohan Malaviya had called him there.

His first account of the facts was not quite right. He wrote that people had gone to break up a Friday market. In fact the market was Mundera Bazaar, and the day was a Saturday. But the heart of what he wrote was clear. The crowd, he said, had set fire to a police station and killed 21 policemen. "I and other thoughtful non-co-operators," he wrote, "will have to hang our head in shame."

<aside class="note"><b>Mistakes in the first news</b>: Gandhi wrote within days, from reports that reached him in Gujarat. The number of policemen killed is given as 21 by Gandhi, and as 22 or 23 by others.</aside>

## Bardoli, 11 and 12 February

The Working Committee of the Congress met at Bardoli on 11 and 12 February. It was the group of leaders who ran the Congress between its big meetings. Gandhi asked the workers who had come to Bardoli what they thought. One account says that almost everyone, young and old, said that if Gandhi retreated, the whole country would be shamed before the world. Only three disagreed.

Gandhi did not follow the crowd. The committee passed two resolutions.

The first one "deplores the inhuman conduct of the mob at Chauri Chaura" and offered sympathy to the families of the policemen who had died.

The second said that mass civil disobedience would be stopped. Every time it had come close, it said, "some popular violent outburst has taken place". That showed that "the atmosphere in the country is not non-violent enough for mass disobedience". The committee told its workers to advise farmers to pay their land revenue. It asked Congress groups to stop volunteer processions, to stop courting arrest, and to stop picketing, except to warn people against drink outside liquor shops. It also told farmers not to withhold rent from their landlords. The people were to turn to the constructive work of the movement instead.

<aside class="note"><b>Constructive work</b>: Useful work that did not need a fight with the government. Swadeshi, which means making and using Indian goods such as homespun khadi cloth, was part of it.</aside>

## Gandhi explains

On 16 February, in his English weekly *Young India*, Gandhi explained his reasons at length. He began: "God has been abundantly kind to me. He has warned me the third time that there is not as yet in India that truthful and non-violent atmosphere which and which alone can justify mass disobedience."

He counted the warnings. The first was in 1919, when riots broke out during the movement against the Rowlatt Act. He had stopped the movement then and called his mistake a "Himalayan miscalculation". The second was in Bombay, in November 1921, when he saw a mob with his own eyes on 17 November. The third was Chauri Chaura.

He did not defend the crowd. He had heard that the policemen had provoked it. But he wrote: "No provocation can possibly justify the brutal murder of men who had been rendered defenceless."

He also explained why it mattered for Bardoli. If one grain of arsenic is dropped into a pot of milk, the whole pot is spoilt. Violence in one place could spoil the movement everywhere.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Gandhi's account came from an informant, and he said so. Wikipedia, which draws on court records, tells some parts differently, for instance who gave which order at the thana. Gandhi wrote only what his informant had told him, and said so.</aside>

## The fast

Gandhi wrote that this was the hardest hour of all. A voice, he said, asked him: surely it was cowardly to withdraw now, after the challenge to the Viceroy. He answered it with a line he repeated in many forms for the rest of his life: "It is better to be charged with cowardice and weakness than to be guilty of denial of our oath and sin against God."

He also set himself a penance. He would fast for five days, taking only water. The fast began on Sunday evening, 12 February, and ended on the Friday. "I urge co-workers not to copy my example," he wrote.

## His friends are angry

Many people were deeply upset. Jawaharlal Nehru was in jail at the time. Gandhi wrote to him on 19 February: "I see that all of you are terribly cut up over the resolutions." He added that his heart went out to "Father", Motilal Nehru, and that he could picture the pain Motilal must have felt.

Gandhi's answer was that Chauri Chaura had been the last straw. He had been getting bad reports from Calcutta, Allahabad, the Punjab and the south. Volunteers were picketing schools and getting out of hand. Of the 36,000 volunteers enrolled in Gorakhpur, he said, not 100 followed the Congress pledge. "The Chauri Chaura news came like a powerful match to ignite the gunpowder, and there was a blaze." If the movement had gone on, he said, it would have been a violent struggle.

On 24 and 25 February the All-India Congress Committee met in Delhi. The opposition was strong. Gandhi told the members, "I am incorrigible. The only tyrant I accept in this world is the still small voice within." The Committee accepted his resolutions with no big change. Gandhi later called himself "a sadder and, I hope, a wiser man".

## Why it matters

The writer Mukul Kesavan says Chauri Chaura shows Gandhi's moral standing, and shows his hold on the Congress. The first great nationwide experiment in civil disobedience, he says, was stopped by Chauri Chaura.

Not everyone agreed that Gandhi was right then, and many do not agree now. Some thought he had given up when the movement was at its strongest. Gandhi's own view was that a freedom won by violence would not be the freedom he wanted. He also thought the country would grow stronger if it learned to control its anger.

What is certain is that for the people of Chauri Chaura, the story did not end in February. The police began to arrest hundreds of men. That is the next story.
