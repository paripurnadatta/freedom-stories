After the fire at Chauri Chaura, the police went looking for the men who had been there. Hundreds were arrested. In the villages, the women went away to their parents' homes.

The accused were tried at the Sessions Court in Gorakhpur before Judge H. E. Holmes. The charges were rioting and arson. The trial lasted eight months.

## The verdict

On 9 January 1923, the judge gave his decision. Of those who stood trial, 170 were sentenced to death by hanging. Forty-seven were let go. Two were given two years in jail. Six had died while they were in police custody.

<aside class="note"><b>How many?</b>: Writers give different numbers. Wikipedia and the Malaviya biography say 225 men were tried and 170 condemned. Britannica and the weekly *Frontier* say 172 were condemned, and *Frontier* gives 228 as the number tried. This story uses 225 and 170, as Wikipedia does.</aside>

The news shocked many people. The Indian communist leader M. N. Roy called the sentences "legalised murder" and asked the workers of India to strike in protest.

## The old lawyer

The accused had a right to appeal to the High Court at Allahabad. For the poor men of Dumri Khurd and the villages around, it was their last hope.

Madan Mohan Malaviya was then the founder of the Banaras Hindu University. He had been a successful lawyer. About ten years before, he had left the High Court to give his time to the university. He had not argued a case there since.

In 1923, he came back for this one. His biographer writes that he did so to plead for the lives of the large number of men sentenced to death.

The Crown's lawyer was a barrister from Aligarh named Qadri. The judges were the Chief Justice, Sir Grimwood Mears, and Justice Sir Theodore Piggot. A dozen other lawyers defended individual men. Malaviya argued the case for all the accused together, and he spoke for four or five days.

At the end, his biographer records, the Chief Justice thanked him. He said the court was grateful that Malaviya had put the case so fairly and so well.

## The High Court's decision

On 30 April 1923, the High Court gave its judgment. Of the 170 men who had been sentenced to death:

- 19 were still sentenced to death;
- 14 were sent to prison for life;
- 19 got eight years, 57 got five years, 20 got three years and three got two years;
- 38 were let go.

The biographer says that 151 of the 170 men were saved from the gallows. The court also criticised the police.

Nineteen men were hanged, between 2 and 11 July 1923.

<aside class="note"><b>Gallows</b>: The frame on which a person was hanged in a prison.</aside>

## Why it matters

Two things stand out. One is the work of an old man who left a quiet life to defend poor men whom he did not know. The other is the number: 151 of the 170 condemned men were saved from the gallows, according to his biographer.

The nineteen who were hanged have their names engraved on the memorial today. They include Nazar Ali, Bhagwan Ahir and Lal Mohammad, whom you met in earlier stories. The Week notes that in the court papers, only two of the nineteen had their surnames written down. The next story tells how the dead came to be remembered.
