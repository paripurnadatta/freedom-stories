A year before the fire, Gandhi had come to Gorakhpur. It was 8 February 1921. A great crowd came to see him, and the historian Shahid Amin says the meeting was "variously estimated at between 1 lakh and 2.5 lakhs".

Other writers put the crowd differently. One account in Amin's study says over 1.5 lakhs came. A local Congress man, Poddar, wrote that a crowd of two to two and a half lakhs coming for a glimpse of Gandhi was "no ordinary thing". Whatever the exact number, it was far bigger than anything the district had seen.

Many people in the district never forgot that day. Amin told The Week that without that visit, "and the electric excitement it generated, the kind of mobilisation that happened would not have been possible". The visit came almost exactly a year before the march. For twelve months the memory of it kept people going.

## A district full of volunteers

Over the year that followed, thousands of men joined Gandhi's movement as volunteers. Later, in a letter, Gandhi wrote that 36,000 volunteers had been enrolled in Gorakhpur. He added that not 100 of them "conformed to the Congress pledge". The pledge was a promise to be non-violent and truthful, and to wear homespun cloth.

Gandhi knew he was giving a harsh opinion. He wrote it after the fire, when he was thinking hard about what had gone wrong.

<aside class="note"><b>Volunteer</b>: In the movement, a volunteer was an ordinary person who signed up to help. Volunteers picketed shops, spun and wore homespun cloth, and joined meetings and marches.</aside>

## The village that led

The centre of the movement near Chaura was not the town. It was a village called Dumri Khurd, whose people were mostly poor. The movement here was led by Muslims, Dalits and members of other backward classes. A committee of the movement had been formed in Dumri Khurd on 13 January 1922.

Four men were the main leaders, according to an account in the weekly *Frontier*. Nazar Ali was 30 and came from Dumri Khurd. Lal Mohammad was 40 and came from Chaura. Bhagwan Ahir was 24 and came from Dumri Khurd. Abdullah Sai was 40 and came from Rajdhani.

Nazar Ali had been a soldier. Lal Mohammad sold coarse cloth. Both were wrestlers, and that mattered. Wrestlers met at gymnasiums called *akharas*, where men from different villages came together. News about the movement travelled quickly through them.

## The call

After the beating at Mundera Bazaar, there were three days of meetings. The plan they agreed on was plain. The volunteers would march to the police station and ask the Sub-Inspector, Gupteshwar Singh, to explain why his men had beaten their companions.

Some of the meetings were held in front of the house of a villager called Shikari. Later, when the case came to court, Shikari became an approver, which is a person who turns witness for the government. The courts thought him the most reliable of those witnesses. His son, Ashik Ali, still lives in Dumri Khurd. When a reporter asked him about it in 2021, he said, "I know nothing of the event."

Letters went out to villages within about ten kilometres. They asked volunteers to gather at Dumri Khurd on the morning of 4 February.

That morning, people went from door to door in the village. They asked for gifts of jaggery and roasted rice, a simple meal for the marchers.

The meeting was held, in most accounts, on a threshing ground, which is an open space where grain is beaten out of the husk. Sacks were laid out, and the volunteers sat on them in groups of six.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Old people in Dumri Khurd told The Week that the meeting was held somewhere else. They said it was in an orchard of mango, jackfruit and shisham trees, in the village cemetery.</aside>

One of the leaders then spoke. He praised the volunteers. He said that anyone who left halfway would be guilty of defiling their religion. Not one volunteer left.

<aside class="note"><b>Who spoke</b>: The Week gives the speaker's name only as "Ali". The likely meaning is Nazar Ali, one of the four leaders.</aside>

## The march

The march began between half past one and two o'clock in the afternoon. It went in good order. As the volunteers walked, people from the villages on the road joined them, and the crowd grew.

Nobody knows exactly how many there were. The High Court judgment later put the number at 1,000 to 1,500. Wikipedia says 2,000 to 2,500. *Frontier* says that by noon, 3,000 to 5,000 had gathered at Chauri Chaura.

<aside class="note"><b>Three counts</b>: The three figures come from different sources and none of them can be checked. The story says "more than a thousand", which fits them all.</aside>

## What they believed

The volunteers were not strangers who had come to cause trouble. They were people from named villages, and many of them were poor. Their leaders were farmers, a cloth seller and an old soldier. 
The court papers that came later treated them unequally. The Week notes that the officer Gupteshwar Singh was written down as "Thakur" Gupteshwar Singh, with his caste surname. The historian Kushwaha points out that of the 19 men hanged, only two had their surnames recorded. The other 17 were written down by first names alone. To this day it is hard to learn who exactly many of them were.

They thought of themselves as Gandhi's followers. Gandhi had told them to be non-violent, and they had taken a pledge. But what each person understood by the movement was not the same. Gandhi himself said, a few days afterwards, that many of the volunteers in Gorakhpur had not kept the pledge.

The march was an answer to the beating at Mundera Bazaar. Messages had gone out, and people came because they had been asked to.

## Why it matters

The day began with jaggery and roasted rice, a speech about religion and an orderly walk. Those who took part were mostly poor men from the villages around, not a gang of strangers. That is worth remembering, because the story of Chauri Chaura has often been told as a story about a mob.
