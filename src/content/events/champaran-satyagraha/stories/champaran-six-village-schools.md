In Champaran in 1917, many children did not go to school. Some were left to roam about. Others worked on the indigo farms from morning to night, for a couple of copper coins a day.

Grown-ups were paid little more. A man's wage was at most ten pice a day. A woman earned at most six, and a child three. Anyone who earned four annas a day was thought very lucky.

<aside class="note"><b>Pice and anna</b>: small coins used in India at that time. An anna was worth more than a pice.</aside>

## A bargain with the villages

Gandhi had come to Champaran to look into the farmers' complaints. But the more he saw, the more he believed that lasting change needed village schools.

So he and his helpers decided to open primary schools in six villages. They made a deal with the villagers. The village would give the teachers food and a place to live. Gandhi's side would pay for everything else. The villagers had very little cash, but they could give grain and other things they grew. They had already offered to.

## Teachers from far away

Finding teachers was hard. Gandhi did not want ordinary teachers. He cared more about their character than their certificates. So he sent out a public appeal for volunteers.

People came. Gangadharrao Deshpande sent two men, Babasaheb Soman and Pundalik. Avantikabai Gokhale came from Bombay, and Anandibai Vaishampayan from Poona. From Gandhi's ashram came Chhotalal, Surendranath and Gandhi's son Devdas. Mahadev Desai and Narahari Parikh came with their wives, Durga and Manibehn. Gandhi's wife Kasturba came too.

## A problem with language

There was a problem. The children spoke Hindi. Durga Desai and Manibehn Parikh knew only a little Gujarati. Kasturba did not have even that. How could they teach?

Gandhi told them not to worry about grammar. They should teach the children cleanliness and good manners first. The letters of the alphabet and simple numbers were not hard, he said, even in a new language.

It worked. Their classes went well. Avantikabai's school became a model for the others.

<aside class="note"><b>The three R's</b>: reading, writing and arithmetic. Gandhi said these mattered less, at first, than cleanliness and good manners.</aside>

## Clean wells and castor oil

Gandhi wanted more than schools. The villages were dirty. Wells were surrounded by mud. Many people had skin diseases.

The Servants of India Society lent him a doctor, Dr Dev, for six months. Each school was run by one man and one woman. Besides teaching, they gave simple medicine. They had only three: castor oil, quinine for fever, and sulphur ointment for skin trouble. No patient could take medicine home. Dr Dev visited each centre on fixed days.

The volunteers swept lanes and cleaned out wells. In some villages, people joined in. In others they did not want to.

The teachers had one more rule. They must not get involved in complaints against the planters, or in politics. Those went to Gandhi. Gandhi could not remember a single time they broke this rule.

## The fire at Bhitiharva

The school at Bhitiharva was a hut of bamboo and grass. One night someone set it on fire. Gandhi wrote that it was "possibly" the work of some of the planters' men. Nobody was ever shown to have done it.

The school was run by Soman and Kasturba. Soman decided to build again, this time in brick. Many people helped him, and a brick house was soon ready.

## Why it matters

Gandhi admitted that his plan did not last. The volunteers had come for a short time. He could not find more from outside, or full-time unpaid workers from Bihar. Other work soon called him away.

But the schools showed something new. Gandhi's struggle was not only about winning a case against the planters. It was also about the daily lives of the people he was fighting for.
