The government wanted Gandhi's inquiry in Champaran to end. He had been collecting farmers' statements for weeks. Officials asked him to finish the work and leave. Gandhi replied that the complaints still needed an answer.

He offered two ways forward. The government could provide relief. Or it could appoint an official inquiry into the farmers' grievances. Simply sending the listeners away would leave the problem in place.

Sir Edward Gait, the Lieutenant-Governor, invited Gandhi to meet him. A committee would investigate. Would Gandhi join it?

Gandhi was willing, but he set conditions. He must remain free to consult his colleagues. Membership must not stop him from supporting the farmers. If the committee's work failed to give them justice, he must remain free to help them again.

Gait accepted these conditions. Gandhi then joined the inquiry. Sir Frank Sly served as its chairman.

<aside class="note"><b>Committee</b>: A group chosen to examine a question and recommend what should be done.</aside>

The farmers' complaints now reached an official body. Gandhi brought experience from the listening work in Champaran. That work had included careful questioning of witnesses. His helpers had been told to leave out doubtful statements.

The committee agreed on its recommendations. Gandhi described that agreement as unanimous. The members had reached a common position despite the opposition the proposed changes would face. Part of certain money taken from the farmers should be returned. Tinkathia, the system requiring indigo on part of a tenant's land, should end.

Agreement inside the committee did not mean agreement everywhere. Planters opposed the changes. They had much to lose when an old arrangement ended. The recommendations still had to become measures that farmers could use.

Gandhi later credited Gait with helping carry the reforms through. Legislation followed. The requirement that had tied tenants to indigo cultivation was brought to an end.

<aside class="note"><b>A careful ending</b>: Ending tinkathia did not remove every form of rural poverty or unfair treatment. This story concerns that particular system and the inquiry into it.</aside>

The decision to join the committee could have separated Gandhi from the people who trusted him. His conditions were meant to prevent that. He would take a seat at the table without giving up the right to speak for the farmers.

Outside, volunteers had spent long days recording complaints. Inside the committee, those complaints became part of an argument for change. Listening and negotiation belonged to the same piece of work.
