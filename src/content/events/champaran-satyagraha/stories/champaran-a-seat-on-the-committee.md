By the end of May 1917, Gandhi's team had written down thousands of statements from Champaran's farmers. The planters were angry. They wanted him gone.

One day a polite letter came from the government of Bihar. It asked whether his inquiry had not gone on long enough. Should he not now end it, and leave Bihar? Gandhi wrote that the letter was polite, "but its meaning was obvious".

## Gandhi's reply

Gandhi wrote back. He would not leave until the farmers got relief. The government could end his inquiry in one of two ways, he said. It could accept that the farmers' complaints were true and put them right. Or it could start an official inquiry of its own.

## The call to Ranchi

Gandhi was then asked to meet the Lieutenant Governor of Bihar, Sir Edward Gait. The meeting was fixed for 4 June 1917, at Ranchi.

Gandhi's friends feared he might be held there. They made plans for carrying on the work without him. Gandhi sent for his wife Kasturba and his son Devdas to meet him at Ranchi.

He was not arrested. Instead, Gait offered to set up an official committee of inquiry. He invited Gandhi to be a member.

## Three conditions

Gandhi talked it over with his co-workers. Then he agreed, on three conditions.

First, he must be free to talk to his co-workers during the inquiry. Second, the government must accept that he was still the farmers' advocate, even as a member. Third, if he did not agree with the result, he must be free to guide the farmers afterwards.

Gait accepted all three as fair. On 13 June 1917 the government announced the committee. Its chairman was Frank Sly, a senior official from the Central Provinces. Gandhi sat on it with British officials, a Raja who was a member of the Bihar council, and a member who spoke for the planters.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: The historian Ramachandra Guha says the committee had seven members, four of them from the Indian Civil Service. The government announcement printed in Rajendra Prasad's book lists a chairman and five members, Gandhi among them, with a secretary as well.</aside>

## The report

The committee heard planters and farmers through the summer. In September Gandhi fell ill with malaria at Ranchi, but kept working. On 3 October 1917 all the members signed the report. Not one of them disagreed.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Rajendra Prasad's book says the report was signed on 3 October and handed to the government on 4 October. Guha writes that it was submitted on 3 October.</aside>

On 18 October the government said it accepted almost all of it. Notices were spread across the district to tell the farmers.

The main points were these:

- The tinkathia system would end completely.
- Any agreement to grow indigo must be voluntary, and last no more than three years. The farmer would choose the field and the price.
- The extra rent the planters had added would be cut, by 20 per cent in one estate and 26 per cent in others.
- Farmers who had paid tawan, a kind of compensation demanded by the planters, would get a quarter of it back.

## The Act

The planters fought hard against the report. But on 29 November 1917 a bill based on it was brought before the Bihar Legislative Council. In 1918 it became the Champaran Agrarian Act.

Gandhi gave Gait much of the credit. Without Gait's firmness and tact, he wrote, the report would not have been unanimous, and the Act would not have been passed.

## Why it matters

Gandhi had not won his case in a court. The case against him had been dropped. He won by gathering careful evidence, and by working inside the government's own committee without giving up the farmers' side.

Gandhi wrote that the tinkathia system had lasted about a hundred years. It was now gone. When he passed through Bettiah that autumn, a police report counted about 4,000 people waiting at the station to welcome him.
