Kasturba Gandhi went to speak with women in a Champaran village. Their clothes were dirty. The volunteers had been encouraging families to keep themselves and their surroundings clean. One woman answered with a problem that advice alone could not solve.

She had only the sari she was wearing. Gandhi placed this encounter at Bhitiharva, one of the villages where his helpers worked.

In Gandhi's later account, the woman asked Kasturba to look inside her home. There was no box or cupboard full of spare clothes. She could not change into another sari while she washed this one. She needed another garment before she could follow the advice.

The woman's name does not appear in Gandhi's account. He did remember her answer. She asked for help obtaining a second sari. With that help, she said, she would be able to bathe and change her clothes.

<aside class="note"><b>Remembered later</b>: Gandhi retells this exchange in his autobiography. The story follows that account without inventing the woman's words or appearance.</aside>

This happened during the village work that accompanied the indigo inquiry. Gandhi and his helpers were gathering farmers' complaints. At the same time, they opened schools and tried to improve village cleanliness. Kasturba was among the volunteers helping families.

The work often began with very basic needs. Dr Dev helped organise health and sanitation work. Volunteers found dirty lanes and neglected places around wells. They tried cleaning some places themselves, hoping that villagers would join them.

Not everyone welcomed every suggestion. Some people were reluctant to do the work. Yet the woman's answer showed a different difficulty. A person might be willing to follow advice and still lack what was needed.

Telling her to wash more often would not produce another sari. Repeating the lesson more firmly would not put spare clothing in her home. Kasturba had to hear the answer before she could understand the obstacle.

Gandhi included the incident when describing what the volunteers learned. Champaran was not only a place where visitors gave lessons. It was also a place where villagers explained their lives to those visitors.

The account does not give us the woman's whole later story. It preserves one clear request. She did not need a longer lecture about cleanliness. She needed something clean to wear while her only sari was being washed.
