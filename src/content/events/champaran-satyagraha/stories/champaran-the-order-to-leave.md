In 1916, at the Congress meeting in Lucknow, a farmer from Bihar would not leave Gandhi alone. His name was Rajkumar Shukla. He came from Champaran, a district in the far north of Bihar, close to Nepal.

Shukla wanted Gandhi to see how the farmers of Champaran lived. Gandhi said he would come one day. Shukla followed him to Cawnpore. Then he followed him to his ashram in Gujarat. Then he was waiting for him in Calcutta.

Gandhi later wrote that this simple, determined farmer had "captured" him.

## The stain of indigo

What was wrong in Champaran?

European planters there grew indigo, a plant that gives a blue dye. The farmers who rented land were tied to a rule set by law. On every twenty parts of his land, a tenant had to grow indigo on three parts, for his landlord. Three kathas out of twenty make up an acre, so the rule was called tinkathia.

<aside class="note"><b>Tinkathia</b>: "three kathas". A katha is a small measure of land in Bihar. Under tinkathia, three kathas in every twenty had to be planted with indigo for the landlord.</aside>

For a while, the planters needed less indigo. In Germany, people had learned to make blue dye in factories. Some tenants paid higher rent instead of growing indigo. Then the First World War cut off the German dye. Indigo paid well again, and many tenants were forced to grow it once more.

Gandhi admitted that before 1916 he did not even know where Champaran was.

## The journey

On 9 April 1917, Gandhi and Shukla left Calcutta by train. They reached Patna the next morning, 10 April. Shukla took him to the house of a lawyer, Rajendra Prasad. But Prasad was away, and the servants did not know who the visitor was. They treated him like any stranger.

Then Mazharul Haque, a lawyer Gandhi had known in London, heard that he was in town. He came and took Gandhi to his own house. He advised him to go first to Muzaffarpur. Gandhi caught the evening train.

At Muzaffarpur station, a college teacher named J. B. Kripalani was waiting with his students. When they found Gandhi, the students pulled his carriage themselves, instead of horses.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Rajendra Prasad's book says the train reached Muzaffarpur at 10 at night, and that Kripalani and Gandhi had never met before. Gandhi's autobiography says the train arrived at midnight. The historian Ramachandra Guha says the two men had first met at Santiniketan in 1915.</aside>

## Two warnings

Gandhi wanted to hear the planters' side first. On 11 April he met J. M. Wilson, the secretary of the Planters' Association. Wilson told him plainly that he was an outsider. He had no business coming between the planters and their tenants.

Gandhi also called on the Commissioner, the senior British officer of the area. In Gandhi's words, the Commissioner "proceeded to bully me". He told Gandhi to leave the area at once.

Gandhi expected trouble now. If he was going to be arrested, he wanted it to happen in Champaran itself, among the farmers.

## Motihari

On 15 April he took the midday train to Motihari, the main town of Champaran. Two young lawyers went with him, Dharanidhar Prasad and Ramnavmi Prasad. Gandhi could not understand the village dialect, so they would act as his interpreters. He had packed his things so that he could go to jail quickly if he had to.

He reached Motihari at three in the afternoon. He stayed at the house of a lawyer, Gorakh Prasad. The same day, news came of a farmer who had been badly treated in a village called Jasaulipatti.

## The elephant ride

On the morning of 16 April, Gandhi and his two interpreters set out for Jasaulipatti on an elephant. In Champaran, Gandhi noted, an elephant was as common as a bullock cart in Gujarat.

They did not get there. On the way, the police caught up with them. Gandhi understood at once. He told his companions to go on to the village without him and do the work. He went back to Motihari with the police.

On the way back, he was handed an order. It was signed by the District Magistrate, W. B. Heycock. It said his presence would endanger the peace. He was to leave the district by the next available train.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: In his autobiography, Gandhi says a messenger from the Police Superintendent stopped them and brought him back in a hired carriage. Rajendra Prasad's book gives more steps: a police sub-inspector came on a bicycle, Gandhi rode back by bullock cart and then a small horse cart, and a Deputy Superintendent of Police showed him the order on the road.</aside>

He was asked to sign for the order. Instead, Gandhi wrote that he would not obey it. He would not leave Champaran, he said, until his inquiry was finished.

## A night of letters

A summons followed. He was to stand trial on 18 April for disobeying the order.

Gandhi now prepared for jail. He sat up through the night, writing letters and instructions for the people who would carry on the work. He wrote a statement to read in court. Telegrams went out to leaders around India.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Gandhi's autobiography says the summons came straight after he refused the order. Rajendra Prasad's book says it came on the evening of 17 April, after a whole day of recording farmers' statements.</aside>

## 18 April 1917

The news spread fast. Gandhi wrote that Motihari saw "unprecedented scenes" that day. Gorakh Prasad's house and the court building overflowed with people.

<aside class="note"><b>Crowd size</b>: Gandhi gives no number. Rajendra Prasad writes that several thousand farmers waited in the court compound, and about 2,000 followed Gandhi towards the courtroom, breaking the glass of the doors.</aside>

The case began at 12.30 in the afternoon. The government lawyer had brought law books. He expected a long legal argument from Gandhi, who was himself a trained lawyer. He asked the Magistrate to put off the case.

Gandhi asked the Magistrate not to delay. Then he read his statement. He said he had come only to study the farmers' problems. He did not want to cause any trouble. But he could not leave the people who had asked for his help. So he would accept whatever punishment the law gave him.

He ended with words that people still quote. He had disobeyed the order, he said, "not for want of respect for lawful authority, but in obedience to the higher law of our being, the voice of conscience."

The Magistrate asked if he was pleading guilty. Gandhi said yes. The Magistrate offered to drop the case if he left the district and promised not to come back. Gandhi refused. He said he would make Champaran his home, even after he came out of jail.

The Magistrate did not know what to do. He said he would give his orders on 21 April. Until then, he would let Gandhi go on bail of Rs 100. Gandhi said he had no one to stand bail for him. In the end the Magistrate let him go on his own promise to return.

The whole trial took about half an hour.

<aside class="note"><b>Conscience</b>: the inner sense that tells a person what is right and what is wrong.</aside>

## Case withdrawn

Gandhi never had to hear his sentence. Before the court met again, the Lieutenant Governor of Bihar ordered the case to be withdrawn. The Collector wrote to Gandhi. He was free to carry on his inquiry, and officials would help him.

Gandhi later wrote that the officials and he had become almost friendly. He had not fought their orders with legal tricks. He had simply refused them, politely. In return, officials let his friends help control the crowds.

## Why it matters

Gandhi called this India's first direct lesson in civil disobedience. He had broken an order in the open, told the court why, and asked for no mercy.

The government had tried to send him away. Instead, it gave him permission to stay. Now he could start the real work: listening to the farmers of Champaran.
