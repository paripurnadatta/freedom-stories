The journey began on an elephant. Gandhi and Dharanidhar Prasad were going to see a farmer near Motihari. They had heard that he had been badly treated. Before they reached him, a messenger from the police stopped their journey.

Gandhi went back with the messenger. Dharanidhar continued towards the village. Soon Gandhi received a written order. He must leave Champaran.

This was Bihar in 1917. Gandhi had come to learn about the troubles of indigo farmers. Indigo was a plant used to make blue dye. Many tenants had to grow it on part of their land. The arrangement was called tinkathia. Under it, three parts out of twenty were set aside for indigo.

<aside class="note"><b>Tenant</b>: A farmer who uses land belonging to someone else and pays for that use.</aside>

Before reaching Champaran, Gandhi had gone with Shukla to Patna. They visited Rajendra Prasad’s home, but he was away. Shukla knew people who might help. Gandhi still needed to learn his way around Bihar and find colleagues for the inquiry.

Gandhi had not arrived with a finished plan. A farmer, Raj Kumar Shukla, had pressed him to visit. Local lawyers already knew about the tenants' complaints. Gandhi wanted to hear the farmers himself before deciding what to do.

First, he tried to hear the other side. He approached the planters' association. Its secretary treated him as an outsider. Gandhi then met the British commissioner. That meeting brought another warning. He should leave the district.

The warnings did not answer the farmers' complaints. Gandhi decided to continue his inquiry. He went to Motihari with several local helpers. They stayed at Gorakh Prasad's house. Farmers began coming to see him there.

Then came the interrupted elephant ride and the police notice. The question had changed. Could Gandhi remain long enough to listen?

He wrote that he would not obey the order to leave. He expected that this decision might put him in prison. His reply did not ask anyone to attack the police. He accepted that officials could bring him before a court.

That night, there was work to do. Letters had to be written. The inquiry needed instructions in case he was taken away. Brajkishore Prasad and other friends had to know what was happening. A summons then ordered Gandhi to appear before the magistrate.

News of the case travelled through the villages. When Gandhi reached the court, people had gathered outside. Many had never met him before. They knew that someone who had come to hear their troubles faced punishment.

The gathering created a problem for the officials. Gandhi and his companions helped keep the crowd under control. Refusing an order did not mean refusing every act of cooperation. He could challenge the case and still help prevent disorder.

Inside the court, the government lawyer wanted more time. Gandhi thought a delay was unnecessary. He was ready to admit that he had disobeyed the notice. He wanted to explain why.

His statement was brief. He had come to Champaran for public service. He could not understand the farmers' condition without meeting them. He also wanted the help of the officials and the planters. He denied that his visit aimed to disturb the peace.

There were two duties before him, he explained. One was to obey the official order. The other was to stay with people who needed his help. Leaving would break that second duty. He had therefore chosen to stay and accept the punishment.

He did not claim that the magistrate had no power. He did not pretend that the notice had never reached him. The important fact was precisely that he had received it. He had read the order and had knowingly refused it.

The magistrate did not give an immediate judgment. The case was postponed. Gandhi sent messages about what had happened. Among those informed were the Viceroy and Madan Mohan Malaviya. Friends in Bihar also received the news.

Outside the courtroom, the inquiry was still waiting. The farmers' problem could not be solved by a single hearing. The court case concerned Gandhi's conduct. The indigo complaints concerned years of demands placed on the tenants. He needed to keep those two matters clear.

Before the next hearing, another message arrived. The case had been withdrawn on the Lieutenant-Governor's orders. Gandhi was no longer being prosecuted for staying. The collector told him that he could conduct his inquiry. Officials would provide help if he needed it.

This was not yet the end of tinkathia. No judge had settled every farmer's claim. No general promise had returned every rupee collected by the planters. The immediate change was smaller and very useful. Gandhi could stay, travel, and collect evidence.

He met the collector and sought that cooperation. The inquiry could now continue without the same threat of immediate removal. Local lawyers and volunteers had much more work ahead. They needed to hear complaints, ask questions, and write down dependable accounts.

Gandhi later described these events in his autobiography. He remembered the people's response as a turning point. Fear had brought many tenants to accept things they believed were wrong. Now they had watched a visitor refuse an order without striking anyone.

The people outside the court had also seen something less dramatic. Gandhi had helped the officials manage the gathering. He had treated them as people while challenging their order. His refusal did not depend on insulting the magistrate or frightening the police.

The journey to one farmer's home had been stopped. The larger journey through Champaran could now begin again. This time, the pages waiting to be filled belonged to the farmers' inquiry.
