## 1. The Order to Leave — FLAGSHIP

- Champaran is a district of the Tirhut division in north Bihar, "right at the foot of the Himalayas
  in close proximity to Nepal", cut off from the rest of India; the Congress was "practically
  unknown in those parts". [A138]
- The system: "The Champaran tenant was bound by law to plant three out of every twenty parts of his
  land with indigo for his landlord. This system was known as the *tinkathia* system, as three
  kathas out of twenty (which make one acre) had to be planted with indigo." [A136]
- Rajkumar Shukla, an agriculturist who had been under this system, caught hold of Gandhi at Lucknow
  during the Congress of 1916, followed him to Cawnpore, then to the Ashram, then to Calcutta.
  Gandhi: "Thus this ignorant, unsophisticated but resolute agriculturist captured me." [A136]
- 9 April 1917: left Calcutta with Shukla. 10 April: Patna, then Muzaffarpur at 10 p.m., met by
  J. B. Kripalani and his students, who "dragged his carriage". [RP pp. 100–101]
- 11 April: saw J. M. Wilson, Secretary of the Planters' Association, who told him "plainly that I
  was an outsider and that I had no business to come between the planters and their tenants"; then
  the Commissioner, who "proceeded to bully me, and advised me forthwith to leave Tirhut". [A138;
  RP p. 101]
- 14 April: decided to start the next day by the midday train; asked for an interpreter, because he
  could not understand the village dialect. [RP p. 103]
- 15 April 1917, midday train with Dharanidhar Prasad and Ramnavmi Prasad; reached Motihari at
  3 p.m.; stayed at the house of Gorakh Prasad, pleader. [RP pp. 104–105]
- That evening he heard that a tenant had been ill-treated about five miles away at Jasaulipatti, and
  decided to go next morning on an elephant. [A138; RP p. 105]
- Halfway there a messenger from the Police Superintendent overtook them. Gandhi sent Dharanidhar on
  alone, took the hired carriage, and was served a notice to leave Champaran. He wrote on it that he
  did not propose to comply "till my inquiry was finished". A summons followed for the next day.
  [A138]
- He sat up all night writing letters and instructions to Brajkishore Prasad. [A138]
- 18 April 1917, 12.30 p.m., before the Sub-divisional Officer. [RP p. 123] The crowd: "Motihari that
  day witnessed unprecedented scenes. Gorakhbabu's house and the court house overflowed with men."
  [A138] No number is given anywhere; do not invent one.
- He asked the Magistrate not to postpone the case and read a statement. The line to quote:
  "I have disregarded the order served upon me, not for want of respect for lawful authority, but in
  obedience to the higher law of our being, the voice of conscience." [A139]
- He then pleaded guilty in so many words: "I do not wish to waste the time of the Court and I plead
  guilty." [RP p. 128]
- The Magistrate offered to withdraw the case if he left the district and promised not to return.
  Gandhi: "That cannot be. Not to speak of this time alone, I shall make Champaran my home even
  after my return from jail." [RP pp. 128–129]
- The trial lasted "about half an hour". Orders were to be passed on 21 April; he was released on
  his own recognizance because he had no bailor. [RP p. 129]
- Before sentence, the Lieutenant Governor ordered the case withdrawn, and the Collector wrote that
  he was at liberty to conduct the inquiry and might count on official help. [A139]
- Fairness, from the same chapter: "A sort of friendliness sprang up between the officials -
  Collector, Magistrate, Police Superintendent - and myself." He accepted the notices instead of
  resisting them legally, and the officials helped his people regulate the crowds. [A138]
- Collector Heycock "seemed to be a good man, anxious to do justice". [A139]

## 2. The House That Listened — FLAGSHIP

- The inquiry could not be run in Gorakhbabu's house "without practically asking poor Gorakhbabu to
  vacate it", and the people of Motihari "had not yet shed their fear to the extent of renting a
  house to us". Brajkishorebabu "tactfully secured one with considerable open space about it". [A140]
- Money: he refused to take anything from the Champaran ryots, and refused to appeal to the country,
  because either would be misread. Friends in Bombay offered Rs. 15,000; he declined. Dr. P. J. Mehta
  of Rangoon agreed to send what was needed. His recollection: "we expended in all not more than
  three thousand rupees" and saved a few hundred. [A140]
- The work: "At least five to seven volunteers were required to take down statements, and even then
  some people had to go away in the evening without being able to make their statements." [A140]
- The rule: "Each peasant had to be closely cross-examined, and whoever failed to satisfy the test was
  rejected. This entailed a lot of extra time but most of the statements were thus rendered
  incontrovertible." [A140]
- A C.I.D. officer was always in the room. They had decided to treat him with courtesy and give him
  all the information they could. "On the contrary the very fact that the statements were taken down
  in the presence of the C.I.D. officers made the peasants more fearless" and restrained
  exaggeration. [A140]
- Scale: by the end of May, after six weeks in the district, "almost seven thousand first-hand
  testimonies". [G]
- He wrote to and met planters against whom serious allegations were made, and met the Planters'
  Association: "Some of the planters hated me, some were indifferent and a few treated me with
  courtesy." [A140]
- The scene to build on: a room, a queue of villagers, a clerk writing, a policeman in plain clothes
  in the corner, and one old man sent away because his story does not hold up.

## 3. One Kitchen for the Inquiry — SHORT

- Each of the vakils had a servant and a cook, "and therefore a separate kitchen", and they often had
  dinner "as late as midnight". [A140]
- Gandhi ridiculed it; they took it in good part because they had become close friends. [A140]
- Agreed: servants dispensed with, all kitchens amalgamated, regular hours kept. "As all were not
  vegetarians, and as two kitchens would have been expensive, a common vegetarian kitchen was decided
  upon. It was also felt necessary to insist on simple meals." [A140]
- Result: "These arrangements considerably reduced the expenses and saved us a lot of time and energy,
  and both these were badly needed." [A140]
- Watch: no menu, no names of cooks, no invented grumbling. The only quoted words are Gandhi's own
  summary. The point of the story is that the inquiry was treated as a shared discipline, not a
  lawyers' picnic.

## 4. Six Village Schools — SHORT

- "I became convinced that work of a permanent nature was impossible without proper village
  education. The ryots' ignorance was pathetic." Children were left to roam, or made to work on the
  indigo plantations "from morning to night for a couple of coppers a day". [A141]
- Wages: a male labourer's did not exceed ten pice a day, a female's six, a child's three; a man who
  earned four annas a day "was considered most fortunate". [A141]
- They opened primary schools in six villages. The villagers' side of the bargain: "they should
  provide the teachers with board and lodging while we would see to the other expenses". The village
  folk had little cash but could give grain and raw materials, and had already offered to. [A141]
- Teachers came from a public appeal: Gangadharrao Deshpande sent Babasaheb Soman and Pundalik;
  Avantikabai Gokhale came from Bombay and Anandibai Vaishampayan from Poona; Chhotalal, Surendranath
  and Devdas came from the Ashram; Mahadev Desai and Narahari Parikh came with their wives; Kasturbai
  was summoned. [A141]
- The problem, and the answer: Durga Desai and Manibehn Parikh had "nothing more than a bare knowledge
  of Gujarati, and Kasturbai not even that". Gandhi told them to teach "not grammar and the three R's
  so much as cleanliness and good manners"; the alphabet and numerals were not difficult. "Avantikabai's
  became a model school." [A141]
- Sanitation: "The villages were insanitary, the lanes full of filth, the wells surrounded by mud and
  stink and the courtyards unbearably untidy"; the people had skin diseases. [A141]
- Dr. Dev was lent by the Servants of India Society for six months. Medicines were castor oil, quinine
  and sulphur ointment, and "No patient was permitted to take home any medicine." [A141–142]
- Discipline: the teachers were told not to touch grievances against planters or politics, and to send
  complaints to Gandhi. "I do not remember a single occasion of indiscipline." [A141]
- The school hut at Bhitiharva, of bamboo and grass, was set on fire one night: "Someone - possibly
  some of the neighbouring planters' men - set fire to it." Soman then built a brick house. [A142]
  Suspicion, not proof — say so.
- Honest ending: "my hope of putting this constructive work on a permanent footing was not fulfilled.
  The volunteers had come for temporary periods, I could not secure any more from outside, and
  permanent honorary workers from Bihar were not available." [A142]

## 5. The Only Sari — SHORT

- At Bhitiharva, Gandhi visited a smaller village nearby and found some of the women "dressed very
  dirtily". He told his wife to ask them why they did not wash their clothes. [A142]
- One woman took Kasturba into her hut and said: "Look now, there is no box or cupboard here
  containing other clothes. The sari I am wearing is the only one I have. How am I to wash it? Tell
  Mahatmaji to get me another sari, and I shall then promise to bathe and put on clean clothes every
  day." [A142]
- Gandhi's comment: "This cottage was not an exception, but a type to be found in many Indian
  villages." [A142]
- This is his recollection, written years later; the woman is not named and no date is given. Do not
  name her, do not describe her house beyond the one sari, and do not give her a reply she did not
  make. The story is about a lesson turning round on the teacher: cleanliness advice that assumes a
  second set of clothes.

## 6. A Seat on the Committee — SHORT

- A letter came from the Bihar Government: "Your inquiry has been sufficiently prolonged; should you
  not now bring it to an end and leave Bihar?" It was "couched in polite language, but its meaning was
  obvious". [A143]
- Gandhi replied that the inquiry must go on until relief came, and that Government could end it by
  accepting the grievances or by recognising a prima facie case for an official inquiry. [A143]
- Sir Edward Gait, the Lieutenant Governor, asked to see him, offered an inquiry and invited him to be
  a member. [A143]
- He agreed on three conditions: he must be free to confer with his co-workers during the inquiry; the
  Government must recognise that "by being a member of the Committee, I did not cease to be the ryots'
  advocate"; and if the result did not satisfy him he must be free to guide the ryots afterwards.
  Gait accepted them "as just and proper". Sir Frank Sly was appointed Chairman. [A143]
- The committee had seven members, four of them ICS officers, with Gandhi; its chairman was an
  official from the Central Provinces. [G]
- Dates: Gandhi met Gait at Ranchi on 4 June 1917; the Government communique announcing the committee
  was published on 13 June 1917. [RP pp. 161–163]
- The report was signed unanimously on 3 October and submitted on 4 October 1917; the Government
  published its Resolution accepting almost all the recommendations on 18 October 1917. [RP pp. 179,
  183]
- What it recommended: abolition of the tinkathia system; indigo agreements to be voluntary, for no
  more than three years, with the ryot choosing the field and the price; and a refund of 25 per cent
  of the tawan (compensation) taken from tenants. [RP pp. 179–183]
- The Bill was introduced in the Bihar Legislative Council on 29 November 1917, republished in the
  Gazette on 20 February 1918, and became the Champaran Agrarian Act. Under it tinkathia was void, and
  the *sharahbeshi* (enhancement) was cut by 20 per cent for the Turkaulia concern and 26 per cent in
  other cases. [RP pp. 189–192]
- Gandhi's own verdict on Gait: "Had he not adopted a firm attitude, and had he not brought all his
  tact to bear on the subject, the report would not have been unanimous, and the Agrarian Act would
  not have been passed." [A143]
- Watch: the criminal case against Gandhi was withdrawn, not decided. Never write that a court found
  the planters guilty. The system ended by an Act, after a committee report.

## Do not say

- Do not give the size of the Motihari crowd on 18 April. No opened source gives a number.
- Do not date the Calcutta departure more precisely than 9 April 1917, and do not say he reached
  Champaran that day: he reached Patna on the 10th and Muzaffarpur that night.
- Do not use Guha's line that Gandhi "arrived in the district of Champaran" on 10 April; his own
  sequence (Patna, Muzaffarpur, Motihari on the 15th) is the careful one. Guha is reliable for the
  seven thousand statements and for the shape of the story.
- Do not quote the autobiography's court statement at length; it is long and formal. One sentence is
  enough.
- No invented dialogue from Shukla, Kasturba, the unnamed woman, the statement clerks, or the C.I.D.
  officer.
- Do not present the burning of the Bhitiharva hut as proved arson.
- Do not make Gait a hero or the planters cartoons. The autobiography gives both their obstruction and
  the officials' help.
