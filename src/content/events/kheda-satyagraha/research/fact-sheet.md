# Fact sheet: kheda-satyagraha

One page, for writing. The six stories in `stories/` already exist in an older form: rewrite them
from this sheet, and do not treat the old text as a source. Everything below was opened on
27 October 2026: autobiography chapters 147–149, the CWMG volume XIV letter of 26 February 1918 and
the Dantali speech of 17 April 1918, plus the Wikipedia article for the campaign dates. Source keys
are at the end. If a fact is not here, do not write it.

Six stories: two flagship (900+ words), four short (350+).

The one thing to get right: the rule was about a **four-anna crop**, which is a quarter of the normal
yield. It was not "a 25 per cent reduction". Say "a crop of four annas or less" and explain it in a
note.

---

## 1. The Promise on Paper — FLAGSHIP

- A condition approaching famine in Kheda district after a widespread crop failure; the Patidars were
  asking for that year's revenue assessment to be suspended. [A147]
- The rule: "Under the Land Revenue Rules, if the crop was four annas or under, the cultivators could
  claim full suspension of the revenue assessment for the year. According to the official figures the
  crop was said to be over four annas. The contention of the cultivators, on the other hand, was that
  it was less than four annas." [A147]
- Before Gandhi: Amritlal Thakkar had inquired, reported and discussed it with the Commissioner;
  Mohanlal Pandya and Shankarlal Parikh had agitated; Vithalbhai Patel and Gokuldas Parekh had raised
  it in the Bombay Legislative Council; "More than one deputation had waited upon the Governor".
  Gandhi was then President of the Gujarat Sabha, which sent petitions and telegrams and "even
  patiently swallowed the insults and threats of the Commissioner". [A147]
- The Government "was in no mood to listen, and regarded the popular demand for arbitration as lèse
  majesté". [A147]
- 16 February 1918: Gandhi arrived at Nadiad. He and his co-workers divided the villages into groups;
  within a week they had reports on 425 of about 600 villages, and he had personally investigated
  conditions in 30. [CWMG XIV, p. 215 n.]
- 26 February 1918, letter to the Collector of Kheda: "I am satisfied from the inquiry which I have
  made myself, and from inquiries made by other fellow-workers, that postponement of land revenue is
  justified, but if you are nevertheless unable to accept our findings, there is, I suggest, still
  time to have the whole question examined by a Board of independent men representing both the
  Government and the public." [CWMG XIV, p. 215]
- The same letter reports the pressure already at work: "several thousands of agriculturists have paid
  up the first instalment because of the severe pressure brought to bear upon them and some have even
  paid up both instalments simultaneously. In order to do this, many were compelled to sell their
  cattle." [CWMG XIV, pp. 215–216]
- The pledge the satyagrahis signed, as Gandhi gives it: "Knowing that the crops of our villages are
  less than four annas, we requested the Government to suspend the collection of revenue assessment
  till the ensuing year, but the Government has not acceded to our prayer. Therefore, we, the
  undersigned, hereby solemnly declare that we shall not, of our own accord, pay to the Government the
  full or the remaining revenue for the year. We shall let the Government take whatever legal steps it
  may think fit and gladly suffer the consequences of our non-payment. We shall rather let our lands
  be forfeited than that by voluntary payment we should allow our case to be considered false or
  should compromise our self-respect." [A147]
- The clause that makes it a story about solidarity, not just refusal: "Should the Government,
  however, agree to suspend collection of the second installment of the assessment throughout the
  district, such amongst us as are in a position to pay will pay up the whole or the balance of the
  revenue that may be due. The reason why those who are able to pay still withhold payment is that,
  if they pay up, the poorer ryots may in a panic sell their chattels or incur debts to pay their
  dues, and thereby bring suffering upon themselves. In these circumstances we feel that, for the sake
  of the poor, it is the duty even of those who can afford to pay to withhold payment of their
  assessment." [A147]
- Headquarters: the Nadiad Anathashram, "no other place being available which would have been large
  enough to accommodate all of us". [A147]
- Dates: the campaign is dated 22 March to 5 June 1918 in the Wikipedia infobox. [W] Gandhi's own
  account gives no start date and puts the end at the beginning of June. Say "March to June 1918",
  and use 22 March only with "according to one chronology".
- Watch: the pledge is long. Use the first paragraph and one sentence of the second; do not print the
  whole thing.

## 2. An Unfinished Victory — FLAGSHIP

- The campaign "came to an unexpected end". Gandhi: "It was clear that the people were exhausted, and
  I hesitated to let the unbending be driven to utter ruin. I was casting about for some graceful way
  of terminating the struggle which would be acceptable to a Satyagrahi." [A149]
- The way out came from the Mamlatdar of Nadiad Taluka: if the well-to-do Patidars paid up, the poorer
  ones would be granted suspension. Gandhi asked for that in writing, and got it. [A149]
- But a Mamlatdar could speak only for his taluka. Gandhi asked the Collector whether it held for the
  whole district. The Collector replied "that orders declaring suspension in terms of the Mamlatdar's
  letter had been already issued". [A149]
- So the pledge had been met on paper, and they accepted it. But: "the end was far from making me feel
  happy, inasmuch as it lacked the grace with which the termination of every Satyagraha campaign ought
  to be accompanied. The Collector carried on as though he had done nothing by way of a settlement.
  The poor were to be granted suspension, but hardly any got the benefit of it. It was the people's
  right to determine who was poor, but they could not exercise it. I was sad that they had not the
  strength to exercise the right." [A149]
- "Although, therefore, the termination was celebrated as a triumph of Satyagraha, I could not enthuse
  over it, as it lacked the essentials of a complete triumph." [A149]
- The test he set: "The end of a Satyagraha campaign can be described as worthy, only when, it leaves
  the Satyagrahis stronger and more spirited than they are in the beginning." [A149]
- What it did leave: "The Kheda Satyagraha marks the beginning of an awakening among the peasants of
  Gujarat, the beginning of their true political education." Educated public workers were forced into
  contact with the actual life of the peasants. "That Vallabhbhai found himself during this campaign
  was by itself no small achievement." [A149]
- And the honest coda: "Nevertheless the Kheda peasants had not fully understood the inner meaning of
  Satyagraha, and they saw it to their cost, as we shall see in the chapters to follow." [A149]
- Fairness: this is not a story of betrayal. The Collector did issue the orders; the richer farmers did
  pay; what failed was the working out — who counted as poor, and who got the paper relief.
- Watch: do not write that all taxes were cancelled. Suspension was for the poorer ryots, for that
  year, and many did not receive it.

## 3. The Onion Harvest — SHORT

- The Government turned to coercion: "The attachment officers sold people's cattle and seized whatever
  movables they could lay hands on. Penalty notices were served, and in some cases standing crops were
  attached. This unnerved the peasants, some of whom paid up their dues, while others desired to place
  safe movables in the way of the officials so that they might attach them to realize the dues." [A148]
- One of Shankarlal Parikh's own tenants paid the assessment on his land, "which created a sensation".
  Parikh "immediately made amends for his tenant's mistake by giving away for charitable purposes the
  land for which the assessment had been paid. He thus saved his honour and set a good example to
  others." [A148]
- To steady the frightened, Gandhi advised the people, under Mohanlal Pandya, to remove the onion crop
  from a field which had been, "in my opinion wrongly attached". He did not call it civil
  disobedience, but argued that the attachment, though legal, was "morally wrong, and was nothing short
  of looting". [A148]
- Pandya volunteered, "and in this seven or eight friends joined him". They were arrested. "When the
  fear of jail disappears, repression puts heart into the people. Crowds of them besieged the court-house
  on the day of the hearing." They were convicted and given "a brief term of imprisonment". [A148]
- No appeal was filed, because "the policy was to avoid the law courts". [A148]
- "A procession escorted the 'convicts' to jail, and on that day Sjt. Mohanlal Pandya earned from the
  people the honoured title of *dungli chor* (onion thief) which he enjoys to this day." [A148]
- Watch: Gandhi thought the conviction wrong, because removing a crop could not be "theft" under the
  Penal Code. That is his legal opinion, not a court finding. Say "Gandhi thought".
- The crop, the field and the number of onions are not described anywhere. Do not describe them.

## 4. The Lawyer at Nadiad — SHORT

- Vallabhbhai Patel "in joining the struggle, had to suspend a splendid and growing practice at the
  bar, which for all practical purposes he was never able to resume." [A147]
- Headquarters were the Nadiad Anathashram. The other comrades named by Gandhi: Shankarlal Banker,
  Anasuyabehn, Indulal Yajnik, Mahadev Desai "and others". [A147]
- The work was village to village: "We had, therefore, to go about from village to village explaining
  the principles of Satyagraha." [A148]
- The lesson he was trying to teach: "to rid the agriculturists of their fear by making them realize
  that the officials were not the masters but the servants of the people, inasmuch as they received
  their salaries from the taxpayer. And then it seemed well-nigh impossible to make them realize the
  duty of combining civility with fearlessness." [A148]
- The image: incivility "would spoil their Satyagraha, like a drop of arsenic in milk". [A148]
- His verdict on the hardest part: "Experience has taught me that civility is the most difficult part
  of Satyagraha. Civility does not here mean the mere outward gentleness of speech cultivated for the
  occasion, but an inborn gentleness and desire to do the opponent good. These should show themselves
  in every act of a Satyagrahi." [A148]
- Honest note: "I realized later that they had less fully learnt the lesson of civility than I had
  expected." [A148]
- Watch: no invented scenes of Patel in court, no dialogue with villagers. The story is the giving up
  of a practice and the daily teaching, not a courtroom drama.

## 5. The Bullock-Cart Lesson — SHORT

- The speech at Dantali, 17 April 1918, opens with something he saw on the road, not with the tax:
  "Whilst coming here, I observed people using goads to urge bullocks yoked to carts or ploughs. This
  cruelty is unforgivable in us, especially as we claim to be protectors of cows. To save the cow we
  engage in big fights with Muslims, but we do not hesitate to apply goads to bullocks. It is for sins
  such as these that we have to suffer. I trust the use of goads will disappear from now." [CWMG XIV,
  p. 352]
- The personal detail: "When, driving here in a bullock-cart from Petlad station, I saw the bullocks
  being pricked with a goad, I felt that refraining from wearing shoes yet driving in a cart was a far
  greater sin than wearing shoes." [CWMG XIV, p. 352]
- Why it belongs in a political story: "I am here today not to talk about kindness to animals but about
  justice; all the same, believing that we must do justice to others if we want justice for ourselves,
  I took the occasion to say all this." [CWMG XIV, p. 352]
- The turn back to the campaign: "It is not so very difficult to pay the land revenue to the
  Government. One can borrow the money from someone or sell some land or a buffalo and pay up. Then
  why take upon ourselves all this bother of not paying...? ... The question is one of principle, not
  merely of paying the land revenue. We pay because we are afraid of the Government; it is this fear we
  wish to get rid of." [CWMG XIV, pp. 352–353]
- The line that carries the whole idea: "there is no disloyalty in disobeying an unjust order of the
  Government; rather, it is the purest loyalty to do so. To submit to an unjust order without even a
  protest is a sign of weakness; it is sheer cowardice." [CWMG XIV, p. 353]
- "We seek justice through self-suffering. Till today, we suffered out of fear and timidity and now we
  wish to suffer on purpose." [CWMG XIV, p. 353]
- Watch: do not invent the driver, his answer, or the village he was passing. Gandhi does not name
  them. The only scene is what he says he saw from the cart.

## 6. Before the Refusal — SHORT

- The order of events matters: inquiry first, refusal second. "At last all petitioning and prayer
  having failed, after taking counsel with co-workers, I advised the Patidars to resort to Satyagraha."
  [A147]
- The letter of 26 February 1918 is the proof that they offered the Government a way out: a Board of
  independent men "representing both the Government and the public". [CWMG XIV, p. 215]
- The same letter shows what was already happening in the villages: thousands had paid the first
  instalment under pressure, some had paid both, and "many were compelled to sell their cattle" to do
  it. [CWMG XIV, pp. 215–216]
- The Government's refusal is recorded in the autobiography as a refusal even to arbitrate: the demand
  for arbitration was "regarded ... as lèse majesté". [A147]
- At Dantali, Gandhi restated the sequence to the villagers: "Our crops have been in most places less
  than four annas and, in some villages, less than six annas. That being so, according to the Revenue
  Code, the collection of revenue should be suspended to the extent of half the amount. This is all our
  demand, one which we claim as our right. Accordingly, we requested the Government to order the
  suspension. It did not accept our assessment of the crops. Thereupon, we asked the appointment of a
  committee of inquiry. We assured the Government that, if it were proved correct, we would accept the
  officers' assessment of the crops. Even then, the Government refused to appoint a committee." [CWMG
  XIV, pp. 353–354]
- Note the detail in that passage: suspension "to the extent of half the amount" — the Revenue Code
  scaled relief; it was not all or nothing. Do not simplify it into "they wanted everything cancelled".
- The stakes, in his words: "If we fail, in this struggle, to get our rights recognized by the
  Government, we shall never be able again to lift our heads." [CWMG XIV, p. 354]
- Watch: no invented meeting with the Collector, no invented reply from the Government. The record has
  the offer and the refusal, not the conversation.

## Do not say

- Not "25 per cent reduction". The crop was measured in annas out of sixteen; four annas is a quarter
  of the normal yield.
- Not "all taxes were cancelled". Suspension was for the poorer cultivators, for one year, and Gandhi
  says most did not get it.
- Do not give a date for the start of the campaign in the text. Use "March 1918"; 22 March is a single
  chronology.
- Do not make Mohanlal Pandya a comic figure. "Onion thief" was an honour given by the people, and he
  went to jail for it.
- Do not give Patel a speech he did not make. The quoted words in this subject are Gandhi's.
- Do not describe the Dantali driver or the bullocks beyond what Gandhi says he saw.
- No weather, no crowd sizes, no thoughts.
