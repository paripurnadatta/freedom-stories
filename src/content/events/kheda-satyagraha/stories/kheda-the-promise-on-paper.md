In 1917 the crops failed across Kheda, a farming district in Gujarat. Gandhi later wrote that the district came close to famine.

The Patidar farmers of Kheda now faced their land tax for the year. They asked the government to put off collecting it. Was that too much to ask?

<aside class="note"><b>Patidars</b>: a farming community of Gujarat. Many of the Kheda cultivators were Patidars.</aside>

## The four-anna rule

The government had its own rule for bad years. Crops were measured in annas, out of sixteen. A normal harvest counted as sixteen annas. If the crop was four annas or less, the farmers could ask for the whole year's tax to be put off.

The farmers said their crop was below four annas. The government's figures said it was above four annas. Everything turned on that difference.

<aside class="note"><b>A four-anna crop</b>: a harvest of four parts out of a normal sixteen. That is a quarter of a normal crop or less. It does not mean the tax would be cut by a quarter. Under the rule, it meant the year's tax could be put off altogether.</aside>

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia describes the rule as a crop that was "four sixteenths" worse than normal. Gandhi's own words, in his autobiography and in his letters, are "four annas or under", meaning a crop of a quarter or less of the usual harvest.</aside>

## Asking politely

The farmers did not start with a protest. Their friends tried every polite way first.

Amritlal Thakkar looked into the matter and wrote a report. He discussed it with the Commissioner, the senior British officer of the area. Two local leaders, Mohanlal Pandya and Shankarlal Parikh, campaigned for the farmers. Vithalbhai Patel and Gokuldas Parekh raised the question in the Bombay Legislative Council. More than one group of people went to see the Governor of Bombay.

Gandhi was then president of the Gujarat Sabha, a public body in Gujarat. The Sabha sent petitions and telegrams. Gandhi wrote that it even put up with insults and threats from the Commissioner.

In February 1918, Gandhi and his helpers checked the crops themselves in hundreds of villages. They asked the government to let a group of independent people check the figures too. The government refused. It treated the very idea of an outside check as an insult to its authority.

## The decision

At last all the petitions had failed. Gandhi talked it over with his co-workers. Then he advised the farmers to use satyagraha. They would refuse to pay, peacefully, and accept the punishment.

<aside class="note"><b>Satyagraha</b>: Gandhi's word for resisting injustice without violence. People openly break an unjust rule and accept the punishment, instead of fighting back.</aside>

## The pledge

The farmers who joined signed a written promise. In Gandhi's English version, it began: "Knowing that the crops of our villages are less than four annas..."

It said they had asked the government to put off the tax until the next year. The government had refused.

So, the pledge went on, they would not pay the tax of their own accord. They would let the government take whatever legal steps it chose. They would rather lose their land than pay and let their case be called false.

## Paying for the poor

The second part of the pledge was the surprising one.

Some farmers could easily have paid. Why should they refuse?

The pledge gave the answer. If the better-off farmers paid, the poorer ones might panic. They might sell their few belongings, or borrow money, to pay too. And that would ruin them. So the richer farmers would hold back their payment for the sake of the poor.

The pledge also offered a way out. If the government agreed to put off the rest of the tax across the whole district, the farmers who could pay would pay in full.

This was not a refusal to pay for ever. It was a refusal to let the poorest families be crushed.

## The headquarters

Gandhi and his team set up their base at the Nadiad Anathashram, in the town of Nadiad. No other building was big enough to hold them all.

With Gandhi were a lawyer from Ahmedabad named Vallabhbhai Patel, and Shankarlal Banker, Anasuyabehn, Indulal Yajnik, Mahadev Desai and others. Patel gave up a busy law practice to join. He was never really able to go back to it.

## Village by village

The Kheda farmers had never done anything like this before. So the volunteers went from village to village, explaining what satyagraha meant.

The main thing was to help the farmers lose their fear. Gandhi wanted them to see that government officials were not their masters. They were servants of the people, because their salaries came from the people's taxes.

But he also wanted the farmers to stay polite to those officials. A rude word, he said, would spoil the satyagraha "like a drop of arsenic in milk".

<aside class="note"><b>Arsenic</b>: a strong poison. Gandhi meant that even a little rudeness would spoil the whole struggle.</aside>

## Watched by all Gujarat

Champaran had been far away, and the press had been kept out. Kheda was different. Newspapers reported on it every day. People all over Gujarat followed the struggle with excitement.

Merchants in Bombay sent money. Gandhi told them the campaign did not need much. They sent more than was needed anyway, and money was left over at the end.

## The pressure begins

At first the government did not act strongly. But the farmers did not give way, so it began to use force.

Officers took and sold farmers' cattle. They seized whatever goods they could find. Fines were announced, and in some places crops still growing in the fields were taken over.

Many farmers were frightened. Some paid. Others wanted to leave things where the officers could easily take them, so the tax would be collected without their paying it themselves. Some were ready to fight to the end.

Gandhi had already warned the Collector about this pressure in February. In a letter on 26 February 1918, he wrote that thousands had paid because of severe pressure. Many, he said, had been forced to sell their cattle to do it.

## Why it matters

The Kheda pledge was a promise on paper, signed by ordinary farmers. It asked for one year's breathing space.

What made it unusual was its second half. The richer farmers chose to risk their own land so that the poorest would not be forced to pay. In Kheda, the struggle was not only against the government. It was also a promise that farmers made to each other.
