The farmers' promise included people who could still pay their tax. They, too, would refuse to hand it over. This was a surprising part of the pledge being signed in Kheda in 1918.

Why should someone with enough money join someone without it?

The answer lay in the fields and the revenue offices. Crops had failed across parts of the district. Farmers asked the government to postpone the land tax. They said the harvest was too poor to meet the demand.

Government officers did not accept their estimate. The disagreement was about more than sympathy. Rules already allowed relief when crops fell below a certain level. The farmers wanted those rules applied to their situation.

<aside class="note"><b>Land revenue</b>: A tax collected by the government from landholders. Postponing it delays payment; cancelling it removes the payment owed.</aside>

Local workers had been pressing the case before Gandhi took charge. Mohanlal Pandya and Shankarlal Parikh urged him to help. Amritlal Thakkar had investigated the condition of the crops. Vithalbhai Patel and Gokuldas Parekh had also made inquiries.

The Gujarat Sabha sent petitions and messages to officials. Gandhi was its president. The Sabha asked for relief, but its requests did not settle the dispute. Arguments passed between public workers and the commissioner.

The next step was to examine the crops more closely. Gandhi and fellow workers divided the task among themselves. Teams went to different villages. They gathered information that could be placed before the district authorities.

On 26 February, Gandhi wrote to the collector. He said that his own inquiry supported postponement. Reports from his colleagues pointed the same way. He enclosed a statement concerning villages with especially poor crops.

He also offered a way to test their findings. An independent board could examine the question. It should include people representing the government and the public. If officials doubted the farmers' figures, another inquiry could compare the evidence.

His letter described pressure already being felt. Some farmers had paid one instalment. Others had paid both at once. According to Gandhi, some had sold cattle to find the money. Paying the bill could therefore leave a family with fewer resources for farming.

The government continued to demand payment. It did not accept the inquiry Gandhi proposed. Petitions and meetings had not secured the postponement the workers sought. Gandhi and his colleagues now had to decide what advice to give.

They chose a refusal to pay voluntarily. The farmers would not use violence to prevent collection. They would accept the legal consequences. Their houses, goods, or land might be put at risk by that decision.

The promise was written down. Its opening described the poor harvest. The signers said they had asked for payment to be delayed until the following year. Their request had not been granted.

Next came the difficult part. They would not pay voluntarily. They were prepared to let the government use its legal powers. They would accept the suffering that followed, including the possible loss of their land.

This was not a promise that nobody would be hurt by the struggle. It warned the signers about loss. A farmer needed to understand that warning before adding a name. Signing was not simply a way to applaud a leader.

Then the pledge explained the place of wealthier farmers. If they paid, poorer neighbours might feel forced to follow. A poor family could borrow money or sell belongings to avoid looking unreliable. The richer farmers would withhold payment to reduce that pressure.

Their refusal therefore had a condition attached. If the government postponed collection for poor farmers, those able to pay would pay. The signers did not claim that all taxes must disappear forever. They were joining together around a particular demand for relief.

<aside class="note"><b>Four annas</b>: Gandhi used an old sixteen-part scale for the harvest. Four annas meant about one-quarter of a normal crop, not a harvest only one-quarter smaller.</aside>

The campaign needed somewhere to work. The volunteers made their headquarters at the Nadiad Anathashram, an orphanage. Anasuyabehn and Shankarlal Banker were among the people helping the campaign. Gandhi named them alongside Patel and other volunteers when he later described the work. Their different tasks supported the same promise. Gandhi could not find another building large enough for the group. From there, workers went into the villages.

Vallabhbhai Patel joined the work and put aside his legal practice. Other helpers included Indulal Yagnik and Mahadev Desai. The campaign depended on people willing to explain the promise repeatedly, in places far from a single meeting hall.

Money arrived from supporters. Gandhi later remembered that more money came than the campaign needed. What mattered more was how the volunteers lived. They had to make their own daily arrangements simpler while asking farmers to accept hardship.

The meetings also had to explain the limits of resistance. Gandhi wanted villagers to lose their fear of officials. He did not want them to replace fear with insults. A refusal could be firm without becoming cruel.

At Dantali in April, he returned to that point. The question, he said, was not merely whether money could somehow be found. A farmer might borrow or sell an animal and pay. The larger question was whether an unjust demand should pass without protest.

He asked the people to remain truthful and courteous. He also warned that losing property would test their resolve. The written promise would matter most when following it became expensive.

Gandhi later recalled that the beginning had seemed full of courage. The harder days would come when collection officers arrived. Yet the pledge had already joined two groups whose immediate situations differed. Some could pay. Some could not.

The stronger households had promised to stand beside the weaker ones. Their names on the paper made that choice visible.
