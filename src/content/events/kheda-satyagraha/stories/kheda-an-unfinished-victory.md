When is a victory not quite a victory? The farmers of Kheda and Gandhi gave two different answers in June 1918.

## A question in Bombay

In April 1918, the Kheda struggle was in its hardest stretch. The farmers had refused to pay that year's land tax because their crops had failed. The government was seizing cattle, goods and growing crops to make them pay.

On about 13 April, Gandhi met Mr Carmichael in Bombay. He was the head of the government's revenue department, which collected the land tax. Carmichael asked Gandhi a serious question. Would this struggle make the people better or worse? Would it teach them to respect the law, or to despise it?

Gandhi told the farmers about this meeting in a speech at Dantali on 17 April. He had answered that there was no fear. In satyagraha, people could only grow in moral strength. They would learn to be more courteous, not less. If the struggle made them worse, he said, it would not be satyagraha at all. It would be duragraha, stubbornness.

<aside class="note"><b>Duragraha</b>: Gandhi's word for a stubborn struggle carried on in the wrong spirit.</aside>

The government also had a message for the farmers. Gandhi said the Commissioner's words came down to this: people must not disobey the government's orders. Gandhi answered that obeying an unjust order without even a protest was weakness.

So both sides were watching. Would the farmers hold out? And would they stay peaceful and polite if they did?

## What it had cost

The weeks that followed were hard. Officers sold cattle and seized household goods. Fines were announced, and in some places growing crops were taken. Mohanlal Pandya and seven or eight friends went to jail for removing onions from a seized field. Shankarlal Parikh gave away a piece of his land for charity, because his tenant had paid the tax on it.

Money was never the problem. Merchants in Bombay sent more than was needed, and some was left over at the end.

## Tired people

By early June, the farmers were worn out. Gandhi could see it. Some of them were still refusing to pay, whatever it cost. He did not want to let those brave holdouts be driven to complete ruin.

So he began looking for a way to end the struggle. It had to be an ending that a satyagrahi could accept with honour.

<aside class="note"><b>Satyagrahi</b>: a person who takes part in a satyagraha.</aside>

## An offer from the Mamlatdar

The way out came suddenly. A local official, the Mamlatdar of Nadiad taluka, sent word to Gandhi. If the better-off farmers paid their tax, he said, the poorer ones would be let off for the year.

Gandhi asked for this in writing. He got it.

<aside class="note"><b>Mamlatdar</b>: the official in charge of a taluka, a smaller area inside a district.</aside>

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Gandhi says the offer came from the Mamlatdar of Nadiad taluka. Wikipedia says it came on 3 June 1918, when Gandhi was at Uttarsanda, from the Mamlatdar there.</aside>

But a Mamlatdar could only speak for his own taluka. Gandhi wanted to know whether the promise held for the whole district. So he asked the Collector, who was in charge of the district.

The Collector replied that orders putting off the tax on these terms had already been issued. Gandhi had not known that. But if it was true, then the farmers' pledge had been met. That had been exactly their aim: the poor would be spared, and those who could pay would pay.

So they declared themselves satisfied. The struggle was over. By one count, it ended on 5 June 1918.

## A victory with no grace

The farmers celebrated. People called the ending a triumph of satyagraha.

Gandhi could not join in. In his autobiography he explained why.

The ending, he said, lacked grace. The Collector behaved as if he had agreed to nothing at all. The poor were supposed to be let off, but hardly any of them actually got the relief. It was the people's right to decide who counted as poor. They were not strong enough to use that right, and that made Gandhi sad.

He set a hard test for any satyagraha. It ends well, he wrote, only if it leaves the people who fought it stronger and braver than when they began. Kheda, he felt, did not fully pass that test.

<aside class="note"><b>Two versions</b>: Wikipedia's page on Vallabhbhai Patel says the government agreed to suspend the tax for a year and even cut the rate, and that Patel came out of it a hero. Gandhi's own account says the poor were promised relief, but hardly any of them received it.</aside>

## What the struggle did change

Still, Gandhi did not call Kheda a failure. He saw results that lasted.

Kheda, he wrote, was the start of an awakening among the farmers of Gujarat. It was the beginning of their real political education.

Educated public workers had been forced to go into the villages and live close to farmers' lives. They learned to see the farmers' troubles as their own. They found real work to do.

One of them stood out. Vallabhbhai Patel had given up a busy law practice to join. "That Vallabhbhai found himself during this campaign," Gandhi wrote, "was by itself no small achievement."

The farmers learned something too. They felt their own strength for the first time. They learned that their own rescue depended on themselves, on their own courage to suffer and give things up. Satyagraha took root in Gujarat.

## Why the farmers were happy

Gandhi also understood why the farmers celebrated. They knew they had gained as much as they had put in. And they believed they had found a sure way to fight injustice. That, he wrote, was reason enough for them to be happy.

But he also admitted something. The farmers had not fully understood the inner meaning of satyagraha. They had been brave. Gandhi felt they had learned the lesson of courtesy less well than he hoped.

## Why it matters

Both sides of this story are true. The farmers were right to be proud. They had stood together, many of them had lost cattle and goods, and the government had given way on paper.

Gandhi was right to be uneasy. A promise on paper is not the same as help reaching the poorest family. Kheda showed that winning a struggle and finishing it well are two different things.
