The message came from an official in Nadiad. If the better-off farmers paid their revenue, poorer farmers could receive a postponement. Gandhi saw a possible way to end the Kheda struggle.

But first he wanted the offer in writing.

The campaign had reached a difficult point. Mohanlal Pandya and Shankarlal Parikh had helped bring the farmers’ case to Gandhi. Now their work belonged to a wider effort. Meetings, visits, and explanations had to continue while families faced collection. Signing the promise had been the beginning. Farmers had promised to withhold payment after a poor harvest. The government had continued to collect it. By now, the consequences were reaching homes and fields across the district.

At first, Gandhi had seen courage in the villages. Volunteers explained that officials were public servants. People began to speak to them with less fear. Yet learning to stand firm was only one part of the task.

The next part was keeping that firmness when property was taken. Collection officers seized household goods and animals. Cattle were sold. In some places, standing crops were attached to meet the demand.

<aside class="note"><b>Attached</b>: Taken under official control because money is said to be owed.</aside>

For a farming family, these were not distant legal words. Cattle helped support the household and its work. Crops were the result of months of labour. Losing either could make the next season harder as well as the present one.

Some people paid. Others hoped officials would take enough belongings to clear the demand. Still others kept to their promise. Gandhi's later account does not describe a whole district acting with one unchanging mind. It describes people making different choices under pressure.

One payment involved land belonging to Shankarlal Parikh. A tenant paid the revenue without consulting him. Other workers were troubled by this breach. Shankarlal responded by giving the land to a charitable purpose.

The donation was his own response to the problem. It did not erase the wider pressure upon poorer tenants. The incident showed how a public promise could reach relationships inside a village. Landholders, tenants, and volunteers did not all control the same decisions.

Elsewhere, a crop of onions became the centre of a new case. Gandhi believed that officials had wrongly attached it. He advised Mohanlal Pandya to harvest it openly. Pandya went with a small group of companions.

They were arrested and received short prison sentences. Gandhi disputed the use of a theft charge. The court nevertheless convicted them. The campaign gained a memorable nickname for Pandya, but prison was a real cost behind the name.

<aside class="note"><b>Two judgments</b>: Gandhi considered the onion attachment wrongful and rejected the theft label. The court convicted the men. His opinion was not the court's ruling.</aside>

Gandhi also kept warning against anger. He wanted the people to resist the revenue demand without abusing officials. Courage, in his view, did not excuse spite. He found this lesson harder to teach than fearlessness.

In an April speech at Dantali, he had explained the difference. The campaign should increase respect for truth and justice. If it made people dishonest or cruel, it would fail in another way. Getting a payment delayed was not its only test.

By the time the Nadiad offer arrived, Gandhi saw exhaustion among the farmers. He worried that continuing much longer could weaken the struggle. An ending had to protect the promise that had brought them together. It also had to recognise how much people had already endured.

The original pledge gave him a clear point to examine. Better-off farmers had agreed to pay if poorer farmers received postponement. The new offer appeared to meet that condition. A written assurance would make it more than a passing conversation.

The mamlatdar, the local revenue official, provided that assurance. Gandhi then took the matter to the collector. The local official could speak for his own area. Gandhi needed to know whether the arrangement applied more widely.

The collector said that orders of this kind had already been issued. He agreed that the assurance covered the district. Gandhi had not known about those orders. He and his colleagues accepted this as fulfilment of the pledge's condition.

The campaign ended. Farmers who could pay were expected to do so. The ending was a settlement around the promise, not a declaration that every landholder owed nothing.

Later, Gandhi looked back without complete satisfaction. Who counted as poor enough to receive relief? The people had little power to decide that question themselves. He believed that fewer farmers benefited than should have done.

That left an important distance between an official order and a family's experience. A written instruction could announce relief. A farmer still had to qualify for it in practice. Gandhi did not treat the announcement alone as proof that every need had been met.

He also felt that the struggle had not ended with the strength he wanted. In his view, a complete satyagraha victory should leave people stronger. Here he saw weariness and an imperfect result. He said so when writing his autobiography.

Yet he did not regard the work as empty. Villagers had organised around a shared demand. Volunteers had learned to work with them. Patel had entered a period of close cooperation with Gandhi. Public work in Gujarat had gained experience it did not have before.

These gains did not put back every lost possession. Nor did they cancel the difficulties of deciding who deserved postponement. They belonged beside those limits, rather than hiding them.

<aside class="note"><b>Remembered later</b>: The account of the final negotiations and Gandhi's dissatisfaction follows his autobiography. It should not be read as a claim that every farmer received the same relief.</aside>

The message from Nadiad had opened a door out of the struggle. Gandhi walked through it because it answered the written pledge. Years later, he was still asking how far that answer had reached into the villages.
