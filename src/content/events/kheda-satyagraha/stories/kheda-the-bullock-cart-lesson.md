On 17 April 1918, Gandhi rode in a bullock cart from Petlad railway station to a village called Dantali, in Kheda district. He had come to speak to the farmers. They were refusing to pay their land tax that year, because their crops had failed.

## What he saw on the road

On the way, Gandhi saw people using goads on their bullocks. A goad is a pointed stick. It was used to prick the animals so that they pulled the carts and ploughs harder.

When he spoke, he said first that they were fighting for justice from the government. Then, before a word about the tax, he talked about the bullocks.

<aside class="note"><b>Goad</b>: a stick with a sharp point, used to drive cattle.</aside>

## A hard thing to hear

Gandhi called this cruelty unforgivable. People said they protected cows, he pointed out. They even fought with Muslims to save the cow. Yet they did not think twice about pricking bullocks with goads.

He also blamed himself. He had given up wearing shoes. Yet he had driven there in a cart pulled by bullocks that were being goaded. That, he said, felt like a far greater sin than wearing shoes.

He hoped the use of goads would stop from that day.

## Why bullocks belonged in the speech

Then he explained why he had said all this. He had come to talk about justice, not about kindness to animals. But he believed that people who want justice for themselves must also be just to others.

## Back to the tax

Then he turned to the struggle. Paying the tax was not really so hard, he admitted. A farmer could borrow, or sell some land or a buffalo, and pay. So why go to all this trouble?

His answer was that the question was one of principle. "We pay because we are afraid of the Government," he said. "It is this fear we wish to get rid of."

He told them that disobeying an unjust order was not disloyal. In his words, "it is the purest loyalty to do so." Giving in to an unjust order without even protesting was weakness.

He also explained what kind of struggle this was. They were not trying to bring down the government. "We seek justice through self-suffering," he said. Until now, people had suffered out of fear. Now they would suffer on purpose, for what was right.

## Why it matters

Gandhi did not keep his ideas about justice in separate boxes. The farmers wanted fair treatment from the government. He asked them to give fair treatment, too, even to the bullocks that pulled their carts.

He asked them to be polite and truthful at every step. A struggle for justice, he believed, had to make the people who fought it better as well.
