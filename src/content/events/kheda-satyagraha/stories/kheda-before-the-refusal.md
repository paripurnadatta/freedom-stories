Gandhi's letter to the collector offered a test. If the government did not accept the workers' findings, an independent board could examine them. Both officials and the public should be represented.

He wrote on 26 February 1918. Kheda farmers were asking for land revenue to be postponed. Their harvest had been poor. The argument depended partly on how poor it had been.

Gandhi had not begun with a call to refuse payment. Others had already investigated and petitioned. Amritlal Thakkar had examined the situation. Vithalbhai Patel and Gokuldas Parekh had also made inquiries. The Gujarat Sabha carried the request to officials.

Gandhi and his fellow workers then divided themselves into groups. Each group investigated villages. They compared crop conditions with the claims being made. Gandhi carried out visits himself.

The letter told the collector that these inquiries supported postponement. Gandhi asked for orders delaying collection where the crop had reached only a quarter of normal or less. It included a statement about villages where the crop was especially poor. The proposed board offered a way to examine the disagreement before it became a larger conflict.

The letter also described what collection pressure was doing. Some farmers had already paid both instalments. Gandhi reported that some had sold cattle to do so. The money could reach the revenue office while leaving the household less able to manage.

<aside class="note"><b>Instalment</b>: One part of a payment that is divided into several payments over time.</aside>

His account was an argument made to the government. Officials did not accept every conclusion. Gandhi's autobiography records their different estimate of the crops. It also records the refusal of the request for an independent inquiry.

That refusal helped lead to the next step. The volunteers advised farmers not to pay voluntarily. A written pledge set out the conditions and the risks. The campaign moved from requests for an inquiry to organised resistance.

The February letter shows the work before that change. Someone had to visit the villages. Someone had to compare information and prepare a statement. Someone had to ask how two different estimates could be tested fairly.

The famous refusal did not begin with an empty page. Before the pledge came inquiries, petitions, and an offer to let others check the evidence.
