How do you prove that a crop has failed? In Kheda in 1918, that question came before any protest.

## Counting the crops

On 16 February 1918, Gandhi arrived in Nadiad, a town in Kheda district. The farmers said their crop that year was a quarter of a normal harvest or less. The government said it was better than that.

Gandhi and his fellow workers split into groups. Each group took a number of villages and looked at the crops there. Within a week they had reports on 425 of the district's 600 or so villages. Gandhi checked 30 villages himself.

## A letter to the Collector

On 26 February 1918, Gandhi wrote to the Collector of Kheda, the top British officer of the district.

He said that his own inquiry, and his helpers', showed that the tax should be put off. He sent a list of villages where the crop had been 25 per cent of normal or less.

Then he made an offer. If the Collector did not accept their findings, there was still time to let a board of independent men examine the whole question. The board would include people representing both the government and the public.

<aside class="note"><b>Collector</b>: the British official in charge of a district, who also collected its land tax.</aside>

## Cattle sold to pay

The same letter told the Collector what was already happening. Thousands of farmers had paid the first part of their tax because of severe pressure. Some had even paid both parts at once. To find the money, many had been forced to sell their cattle.

On the same day Gandhi wrote another letter about Kheda. In it he set out the farmers' case simply. If the land gives nothing, he argued, no tax can be collected from it.

## The answer

The government would not accept the farmers' figures. It also would not agree to an independent check. Gandhi wrote later that it treated the very idea as an insult to its authority.

Only then did Gandhi advise the farmers to refuse to pay.

## Telling the villages

On 17 April 1918 Gandhi spoke at a village called Dantali. He went over the steps once more for the villagers.

Their crops, he said, had been less than four annas in most places, and less than six annas in some villages. In such a year, by the rules, at least half the tax should be put off. That was all they had asked for.

The government had not accepted their figures. So they asked for a committee to check. They even promised to accept the officers' figures if the committee proved them right. Still the government refused.

"If we fail, in this struggle, to get our rights recognized by the Government," he told them, "we shall never be able again to lift our heads."

## Why it matters

The Kheda farmers did not refuse first and ask questions later. They checked the facts. They asked politely. They offered a fair test, and promised to accept the result.

Only when every door was closed did they say no. That order of steps, first the facts and then the refusal, was part of what Gandhi meant by satyagraha.
